## Overview

### Why Founders Use Convertible Notes

**The Promise:**
- Quick capital raise ($100K-$1M+) in weeks, not months
- Avoid premature valuation conversations
- Demonstrate investor confidence
- Lower legal complexity vs equity rounds

**The Reality:**
- Still debt - appears on balance sheet
- Must repay if Series A doesn't happen
- Creates maturity pressure
- Interest accrues, diluting equity

### Should You Use Convertible Notes?

**Use If:**
```
✓ You're pre-revenue or early traction
✓ Series A planned within 18-24 months
✓ You want faster capital raise than equity
✓ Investors are angels/early VCs (expect convertibles)
✓ You have strong team that justifies deferring valuation
✓ You can realistically service repayment if needed
```

**Avoid If:**
```
✗ Series A timeline uncertain (>3 years away)
✗ No revenue and uncertain path to profitability
✗ Taking multiple convertible rounds (complexity)
✗ You need clear cap table immediately
✗ Company has existing bank debt (complicates subordination)
✗ You're risk-averse about debt obligations
```

## Key Risks & Concerns

### 1. Balance Sheet Liability - The Accounting Problem

**What Happens:**
Convertible notes appear as debt (liability) on your balance sheet. This creates cascading problems:

#### The Accounting Treatment

```
Traditional Balance Sheet Without Note:

ASSETS:                          LIABILITIES + EQUITY:
  Cash: $100,000                   Accounts Payable: $50,000
  Equipment: $50,000               Equity: $100,000
  ────────────                     ───────────────
  Total: $150,000                  Total: $150,000


With $500K Convertible Note:

ASSETS:                          LIABILITIES + EQUITY:
  Cash: $600,000                   Accounts Payable: $50,000
  Equipment: $50,000               Convertible Note: $500,000
  ────────────                     Equity: $100,000
  Total: $650,000                  ───────────────
                                   Total: $650,000
```

**The Problems This Creates:**

**Problem 1: Debt-to-Equity Ratio Deteriorates**
```
Without Note: $50K / $100K = 0.5x debt-to-equity (healthy)
With Note:    $550K / $100K = 5.5x debt-to-equity (alarming)

Implications:
- Looks worse to banks (if you need credit line later)
- Looks worse to customers (if they care about financial stability)
- Creates problems if you have venture debt (covenants on debt ratios)
```

**Problem 2: Audit & Financial Reporting**
```
Your accountant must classify the note as:
- Liability (debt), OR
- Equity-like instrument

If classified as debt:
- Must track interest accrual
- Must accrue repayment amount
- Creates complications for cap table calculations
- May trigger accounting questions from auditors

Depending on accounting treatment, Series A investors may require
adjustments (e.g., "we'll treat the note as equity" for cap table)
```

**Problem 3: Loan Covenant Violations**
```
If you have bank financing, their agreement may say:
"Company shall not incur debt exceeding 2x EBITDA"

Your convertible note counts as debt, so:
- $500K convertible note = debt increase
- May trigger covenant violation
- Bank can demand immediate repayment
- Forced unwinding of note or bank relationship

This is often overlooked but CRITICAL
```

**Problem 4: Venture Debt Complications**
```
Venture debt lenders (e.g., Silicon Valley Bank) often require:
- Clean cap table with no convertible notes, OR
- All convertible notes converted to equity before lending

Why? Because they want clear seniority (their debt over all equity)
Convertible notes create ambiguity

Solution: Negotiate with venture debt lenders early about
convertible notes
```

#### How to Mitigate Balance Sheet Issues

**Strategy 1: Use "Equity-Like" Accounting**
```
Work with your accountant to:
- Classify the note as "temporary equity" or "convertible equity"
- Instead of "debt" (impacts balance sheet classification)
- More favorable optics
- Still requires disclosure but less alarming

Caveat: IRS and auditors may not allow this
Consult your CPA before assuming
```

**Strategy 2: Plan for Series A to Absorb It**
```
Financial Projection Strategy:
- Assume note converts in Series A (most likely outcome)
- Don't plan balance sheet around long-term note repayment
- Include conversion in Series A cap table discussions

Pro: Forces focus on Series A timeline
Con: If Series A delayed, balance sheet gets worse
```

**Strategy 3: Track Interest Accrual Carefully**
```
Even though interest accrues (typically):
- Run monthly calculations of accrued interest
- Don't surprise yourself in 2-3 years
- Plan for 30-40% principal increase from interest
- Use this in financial projections

Example:
  Initial: $500K
  With 5% interest over 3 years: $575K
  With 8% interest over 3 years: $620K

This matters for Series A cap table
```

**Strategy 4: Negotiate Lowest Possible Interest**
```
Your best defense against balance sheet damage:
- Push for 3-5% interest (not 8-10%)
- Every 1% saves ~$50K over 3 years
- Use team track record to justify low rates

Typical negotiation:
  You: "5% is fair for early-stage risk"
  Investor: "8% is market standard"
  Compromise: 6% or 5.5%
```

**Strategy 5: Limit Number of Convertible Notes**
```
Danger Zone: Multiple convertible notes

Scenario: 10 investors × $100K each @ 8% interest

Total at Year 2:
  Principal: $1M
  Interest: $160K
  Total Liability: $1.16M

Balance sheet shows:
  Liabilities: $1.16M
  Equity: $100K
  Debt/Equity: 11.6x (horrible)

Better approach:
- Raise $1M from fewer investors
- Or use SAFEs (don't appear as debt)
- Or do small equity round (appears as equity)

Max 3-4 convertible notes before balance sheet gets unwieldy
```

**Strategy 6: Have Bank Relationship Discussion**
```
If you have or plan to have bank debt:
- Discuss convertible notes with banker BEFORE issuing
- Get subordination agreement (Section 7 of main note)
- Ensure bank comfortable with equity conversion plan
- Don't surprise banker with note later

Banks can be deal killers - involve them early
```

**Key Takeaway:**
The balance sheet liability is REAL. It's not accounting magic or something you can ignore. Start with lowest interest possible, limit number of notes, and plan aggressively for Series A conversion.

### 2. Mandatory Repayment Obligation - The Maturity Date Crisis

**What Happens:**
The note has a maturity date (e.g., 3 years). On that date, if the note hasn't converted to equity, YOU MUST REPAY IT IN CASH.

This is not optional. It's a debt obligation.

#### Why This Is Terrifying

**Scenario: The Maturity Wall**

```
TIMELINE:

Year 0: Raise $500K convertible note
  ├─ Maturity set for 3 years (Year 3, Q1)
  └─ You have $500K for product, hires, marketing

Year 1-2: Growth Phase
  ├─ Revenue grows to $100K/month
  ├─ Team grows from 2 to 10 people
  └─ Series A conversations starting

Year 2.5: Series A Challenges
  ├─ Series A investor wants to see more traction
  ├─ Negotiations slow down
  ├─ Market conditions deteriorate
  └─ Series A not closing yet

Year 3.0: MATURITY DATE ARRIVES
  ├─ Series A still not closed
  ├─ Investor sends letter: "Time to repay $500K+ (with interest)"
  ├─ You have ~$100K cash (operating reserves)
  ├─ You cannot repay $550K-$600K
  └─ CRISIS MODE

Your Options (All Bad):
  A) Default and negotiate extension (shows distress to Series A investors)
  B) Sell company quickly (take bad deal)
  C) Emergency bridge financing (expensive debt)
  D) Declare bankruptcy (worst case)
```

**Why This Happens More Than Expected:**

1. **Series A Takes Longer Than You Think**
   - You estimate 18 months to Series A
   - Reality: 24-30 months
   - Note maturity doesn't adjust

2. **Market Conditions Change**
   - When you're raising at maturity, market may be bad
   - Forced to raise at lower valuation or delay
   - Meanwhile, note due

3. **Company Performance Miss**
   - Initial projections: $500K revenue by month 18
   - Reality: $100K revenue at month 18
   - Series A investors less interested
   - Slows fundraising timeline

4. **Investor Fatigue**
   - Series A investors do diligence, move slow
   - Your convertible investor gets impatient
   - Threatens to demand repayment
   - Creates tension with Series A investors

**Real Example:**
```
Company: SaaS startup
Funding: $300K convertible note @ 8%, maturity 3 years
Plan: Raise Series A by month 18

Reality:
  Month 18: Series A discussions begin
  Month 20: First term sheet, but valuation too low
  Month 22: Valuation improved, but Series A investor wants more traction
  Month 30: Series A finally closes

  Problem: Note matured at Month 36

  Timeline collision: Series A closed before maturity, but barely

  If Series A delayed 6 more months = note maturity crisis
```

#### How to Mitigate Maturity Date Risk

**Strategy 1: Negotiate Longest Possible Maturity**
```
Typical: 3 years (36 months)
Better: 4 years (48 months)
Ideal: 5 years (60 months)

Additional 12 months = 40% more time for Series A

Negotiation:
  You: "5-year maturity is more realistic for Series A timeline"
  Investor: "3 years is standard"
  You: "For early-stage, Series A takes 24-30 months minimum"
  Investor: "OK, 4 years as compromise"

Each month of additional maturity is worth negotiating for
```

**Strategy 2: Automatic Extension Clause**
```
Build in automatic extension:

"If no Qualified Financing by Maturity Date, Note automatically
extends 12 additional months at interest rate of [X]%"

Benefit:
- Removes maturity cliff
- Removes need for emergency renegotiation
- Gives you breathing room
- Investors still get timeline pressure (extended rate incentive)

Negotiation:
  You: "Can we add automatic 12-month extension?"
  Investor: "What's the interest rate during extension?"
  You: "Interest goes from 5% to 7% during extended period"
  Investor: "OK, done" (they still get incentive to convert)
```

**Strategy 3: Early Series A Focus**
```
Reverse-engineer Series A timeline:

Maturity: Year 3
Series A process timeline (backward):
  - Closing: 12 months before maturity (Month 24)
  - Due diligence: 3 months before closing (Month 21)
  - Term sheet: 2 months before DD (Month 19)
  - Initial meetings: 2 months before term sheet (Month 17)

Therefore: Must start Series A conversations by Month 17

Implication:
- If maturity Year 3, Series A MUST be discussed by Month 17-18
- No time for extended exploration
- Disciplined fundraising timeline

Pro tip: If by Month 12 no Series A traction, start raising bridge round
         or consider SAFE-based second round (no maturity)
```

**Strategy 4: Bridge Financing Backup Plan**
```
If maturity approaching without Series A closed:

Option: Arrange "bridge note" to cover maturity
- Easier to get than Series A (bridge investors understand situation)
- Typically shorter term (12-18 months)
- Higher interest (8-12%)
- Converts to Series A

Timeline:
  Year 3 Month 6: Original note maturing in 6 months
  Year 3 Month 6: Close $300K bridge note (converts to Series A)
  Year 3 Month 12: Series A closes (bridge note converts)

Caveat: Bridge notes are more expensive than initial notes
        But better than default or forced sale
        Last resort option
```

**Strategy 5: Build Cash for Repayment**
```
Conservative approach:
- Assume note must be repaid (don't count on Series A)
- Set aside $600K cash by Year 3 (principal + interest)
- Build to $50K/month revenue with 40%+ gross margin
- Use operating cash flow to build reserves

Reality check:
- Most early-stage companies won't hit this
- But showing this path makes Series A easier
- Also gives you genuine repayment option

Implication:
- Keep burn rate low
- Hit revenue milestones
- Don't waste capital on vanity
- Focus on path to profitability/Series A
```

**Strategy 6: Investor Communications**
```
Quarterly investor updates (per information rights):
- Include Series A timeline and status
- Monthly burn rate
- Runway months
- Series A pipeline/meetings

Why: Keeps investors informed, reduces surprise
     If Series A delayed, they see it coming
     Less likely to demand repayment if they track progress

Bad approach: Ghosting investors for 2 years, then "surprise!"
             They're frustrated, may demand repayment

Good approach: Monthly updates, Series A traction shown
              When note matures, mutual understanding of next steps
```

**Key Takeaway:**
Maturity date is REAL. It's not a suggestion. Plan for it from day one. Get longest maturity possible, negotiate automatic extension, and track Series A progress religiously. By Year 2.5, you should know if Series A is happening by maturity.

### 3. Interest Accrual - The Equity Dilution Penalty

**What Happens:**
Even if you never pay cash interest, interest accrues on your convertible note. This increases the amount converted to equity, diluting all shareholders.

#### How Interest Works

**Simple Interest (Most Common):**
```
Interest = Principal × Rate × Time

Example: $500K @ 5% simple interest over 3 years
  Year 1: $500K × 5% = $25K interest
  Year 2: $500K × 5% = $25K interest
  Year 3: $500K × 5% = $25K interest
  Total Accrued: $75K

Amount converting to equity: $575K (not $500K)
Additional dilution: $75K / $575K = 13% more shares issued
```

**Compound Interest (Less Common, More Expensive):**
```
Interest = Principal × (1 + Rate)^Time - Principal

Example: $500K @ 5% compound interest over 3 years
  Amount due = $500K × (1.05)^3 = $578,813
  Total interest accrued: $78,813

More expensive than simple interest
Avoid if possible
```

#### Why Interest Accrual Hurts Founders

**Problem 1: Equity Dilution**
```
Scenario: Pre-Series A cap table

Before Conversion:
  Founder A: 500,000 shares (40%)
  Founder B: 350,000 shares (28%)
  Employee pool: 150,000 shares (12%)
  Total: 1,000,000 shares (100%)

Convertible Note at conversion:
  Without Interest:
    $500K / $5 conversion price = 100,000 shares (8% post-conversion)

  With Interest (5% × 2 years = $50K):
    $550K / $5 conversion price = 110,000 shares (8.9% post-conversion)

  Additional dilution: 10,000 shares = 0.9% of company

Implication:
  Founder A: 40% → 36.6% (lost 3.4%)
  Founder B: 28% → 25.7% (lost 2.3%)
  Series A investor gets 25% (as planned)

  Extra 10K shares to convertible investor = less for Series A investor
  Series A investor might demand higher percentage
  You lose negotiating leverage
```

**Problem 2: Valuation Pressure**
```
Series A investors analyze:
"We're investing $5M at $15M post-money valuation.
 But after convertible note conversion (~$550K),
 our effective stake is 25% instead of 33%.

 To get 25%, we need to invest $X instead of $5M.
 Or we need higher valuation."

Interest accrual increases conversion, reduces Series A investor return
Series A investor either asks for lower valuation or more dilution
Neither is good for you
```

**Problem 3: Debt Accumulation**
```
If note reaches maturity without conversion:
  Without interest: Repay $500K
  With 8% interest over 4 years: Repay $660K

  $160K difference is REAL cash you must pay

If repayment happens (worst case):
  You're paying interest expense
  This reduces company cash for operations
  Creates worse financial situation
```

**Problem 4: Multiple Notes Compound**
```
If you raise multiple convertible notes:

Note 1: $100K @ 5% maturity 3 years
Note 2: $100K @ 5% maturity 3 years (raised 6 months later)
Note 3: $100K @ 5% maturity 3 years (raised 1 year later)

At Year 2.5 (maturity approaching):
  Note 1 interest: 2.5 years × 5% = $12.5K
  Note 2 interest: 2 years × 5% = $10K
  Note 3 interest: 1.5 years × 5% = $7.5K
  Total interest: $30K

  Total to convert: $330K (vs $300K principal)
  10% dilution penalty from multiple notes' interest
```

#### How to Mitigate Interest Accrual

**Strategy 1: Negotiate Lowest Possible Rate**
```
Market rates: 3-8%
Your goal: Get lowest possible

Negotiation points:
- "My team is exceptional" → justifies lower rate
- "Revenue already growing" → de-risks note
- "Series A planned in 18 months" → short interest period
- "We're raising multiple notes" → distribute risk, justify lower rates

Aggressive negotiation:
  You: "I'd like 3% interest"
  Investor: "Market is 5-7%"
  You: "Our team has exited before, revenue growing 20%/month,
       and Series A very likely in 18 months. 3% is fair."
  Investor: "OK, 4% as compromise"

Every 1% saved = $50K+ over 3 years on $500K note
Worth aggressive negotiation
```

**Strategy 2: Pay Interest Annually (If Possible)**
```
Two options:
  A) Interest accrues (not paid until conversion/maturity)
  B) Interest paid annually in cash

If you can negotiate annual cash payments:
- You use operating cash flow to pay interest
- Shows financial discipline
- Demonstrates company profitability path
- Reduces accrual amount
- Shows good faith to investors

Caveat:
- Requires positive cash flow
- Pre-revenue companies can't do this
- But if any revenue, worth considering

Example:
  $500K note @ 5% simple interest
  Annual payment: $25K/year

  Year 1: Pay $25K
  Year 2: Pay $25K
  Year 3: Pay $25K

  At conversion: Only $500K converts (not $575K)
  No additional dilution from interest
```

**Strategy 3: Pay Down Principal Over Time**
```
Aggressive approach (if you have cash):
- Make annual principal payments to reduce balance

Example:
  $500K note issued
  Year 1: Pay $100K principal payment
  Year 2: Pay $100K principal payment
  Year 3: Remaining $300K converts at maturity or Series A

Benefit:
- Interest accrues on lower balance each year
- $100K × 5% × 3 years = $15K interest (vs $75K if no payments)
- When note converts: $315K instead of $575K

Caveat:
- Requires significant cash flow
- Not typical for early-stage startups
- But shows financial strength if possible
```

**Strategy 4: Plan Series A Aggressively**
```
If no interest paid, interest accrual = cost of delay

Strategy:
- Assume $50K/year interest cost for every year note outstanding
- Build 18-month Series A timeline
- Execute disciplined fundraising

Math:
  2-year Series A timeline = $100K interest accrual
  3-year Series A timeline = $150K interest accrual
  4-year Series A timeline = $200K interest accrual (with 4-year maturity)

Every 12 months of delay = ~$50K additional equity dilution

Implication: Speed up Series A timeline = reduce interest cost
Fast + successful Series A > Slow + cheap Series A
```

**Strategy 5: Tiered Interest Rate**
```
Negotiate interest that increases over time:

"Interest is 3% for first 2 years, then 5% thereafter"

Benefit:
- Lower early interest (critical cash flow period)
- Higher later interest (creates maturity pressure for Series A)
- Incentivizes both parties: you to fundraise, them to convert

Example:
  Years 0-2: 3% interest = $30K accrual
  Years 2-3: 5% interest = $20K accrual
  Total: $50K (vs $75K @ 5% flat)
  Savings: $25K equity dilution
```

**Strategy 6: Cap Total Interest Accrual**
```
Negotiate maximum total interest cap:

"Total interest accrued shall not exceed $100K regardless of
time to conversion"

Benefit:
- Protects against extended timelines
- If Series A takes 4-5 years, interest capped
- Reduces worst-case scenario

Investor likely to reject (limits their upside)
But worth proposing for strong teams

Compromise:
"Interest capped at $X unless Series A not closed by Year 4,
in which case interest continues accruing"
```

**Key Takeaway:**
Interest accrual is real dilution that compounds over time. Negotiate the lowest rate possible (3-5% is achievable), pay it annually if you have cash flow, and execute fast Series A to minimize the period it accrues.

### 4. Control Provisions - The Operational Handcuffs

**What Happens:**
Convertible notes often come with "protective provisions" (covenants) that restrict what the company can do without investor approval.

#### Typical Control Restrictions

```
Without Investor Approval (>50% of noteholders), Company Cannot:

DEBT RESTRICTIONS:
  ✗ Borrow more than $[PLACEHOLDER: amount] (e.g., $250K)
  ✗ Grant liens/security on assets (except normal inventory)
  ✗ Guarantee any third-party debt

CAPITAL STRUCTURE:
  ✗ Issue new equity (except Series A financing + employee options)
  ✗ Declare any dividends or distributions
  ✗ Merge with another company
  ✗ Sell the company or substantially all assets
  ✗ Liquidate or wind down
  ✗ Issue new classes of preferred stock

OPERATIONS:
  ✗ Change the core business materially
  ✗ Fire the CEO without investor notification
  ✗ Change Board composition
  ✗ Enter contracts >$[PLACEHOLDER: amount] with related parties
  ✗ Enter long-term contracts (e.g., lease) >$[PLACEHOLDER: amount]
```

#### Why These Provisions Are Problematic

**Problem 1: They're Vague**
```
Example covenant: "Cannot materially change the business"

What does "materially change" mean?
- Pivoting from B2B to B2C? (Investor might say yes, they might say no)
- Entering new market segment? (Depends on how different)
- New product line? (Related or unrelated to current business?)

Result: Ambiguity. Founder wants to move fast, investor wants to
        approve. Creates delays and disputes.

Better language: "Cannot exit [describe specific business] unless
                 Series A investor notified" (gives clarity)
```

**Problem 2: They Slow Decision-Making**
```
Real scenario:

Founder: "We found a strategic partnership with Corp X. They want to
         buy 10,000 units/year at $50/unit. Revenue impact: $500K/year!"

Investor: "How much does the contract restrict your options?"

Founder: "It's a 3-year contract, they get exclusivity in their
         vertical market segment."

Investor: "That might materially change the business. Need to
          review and approve."

Timeline:
  Day 1: Founder sends contract to investor
  Day 5: Investor asks for clarifications
  Day 10: Investor sends revision requests
  Day 15: Founder and investor negotiate changes
  Day 20: Approval received

Meanwhile: Corp X says "Decision needed by Day 5 or we move on"

Result: Missed opportunity worth $500K revenue
        All because of approval delays

This happens constantly in early-stage companies
```

**Problem 3: Board Observation Becomes Board Control**
```
What covenant says: "Investor has right to attend Board meetings"

What it means in practice:
- Investor attends and influences all decisions
- CEO feels need to get investor pre-approval
- Investor de facto has veto power
- Limits founder autonomy

Founder felt: "I control my company"
Reality: "I control my company subject to investor veto"

This dynamic especially problematic if investor has different vision:
- You want to pivot to vertical market X
- Investor has no opinion or wants different vertical
- You ask for approval, investor withholds it
- You're now stuck
```

**Problem 4: Triggers Unexpected Conflicts**
```
Scenario: You want to hire strong second-in-command

Covenant: "Cannot change CEO without investor notification"

You hire CFO-turned-COO (not CEO replacement, but might look like it)

Investor: "Did you change CEO or not?"

You: "No, I hired a COO to help with operations"

Investor: "The covenant says CEO... is a COO-hire material?"

Gray area. Creates conflict where shouldn't exist.

Better language: "Cannot replace the current CEO [name] without
                 investor notification" (clear trigger)
```

**Problem 5: Prevents Opportunistic Actions**
```
Scenario: Competitor company in distress

You could acquire them for $100K (cheap!)
This would accelerate your growth by 18 months

Covenant: "Cannot merge with another company without approval"

You ask investor: "Can we acquire Company X for $100K?"

Investor: "We'd need to do diligence. 2-3 weeks."

In 2-3 weeks: Competitor gets other acquisition offer, gone

Your decision: Miss great opportunity or act without approval
              (risking investor relationship)

Happens more often than you'd think
```

#### How to Negotiate Better Control Provisions

**Strategy 1: Limit Approval Rights to Major Items**
```
Instead of: "Cannot [long list] without approval"

Negotiate: "Cannot engage in any of the following without investor
           notice (notification only, no approval):
             1. Merge with other company
             2. Sell substantially all assets (>80%)
             3. Liquidate or wind down
             4. Incur debt >$[X] in senior position"

Key difference:
- Notice ≠ approval (you can proceed after notifying)
- Limits to truly major items (M&A, major debt, liquidation)
- Allows operational flexibility

Investor still protected (knows what's happening)
Founder can move faster (doesn't need approval)
```

**Strategy 2: Set Clear Dollar Thresholds**
```
Instead of: "Cannot incur debt without approval"

Negotiate: "Cannot incur debt exceeding $250K without approval"

Specific thresholds allow:
- You to incur debt <$250K without approval
- Covers typical business needs (equipment, working capital)
- Investor approval only for major debt
- Clear rules, not ambiguous judgment calls

Thresholds should be tied to:
- Monthly burn rate: threshold = 3-6 months burn
- Current revenue: threshold = 25-50% of monthly revenue
- Company stage: threshold increases as company grows
```

**Strategy 3: Carve Out Operational Decisions**
```
Covenant approval should NOT apply to:
- Hiring/firing employees (except CEO)
- Pricing decisions
- Product development direction (within core business)
- Customer contract negotiations (under $[X] value)
- Marketing spend decisions
- Vendor selection
- Working capital management

These are core founder/manager decisions
Investor approval on these is overreach

Better language: "Investor approval required for changes to [list],
but not for operational decisions within Board-approved annual plan"
```

**Strategy 4: Grandfather Existing Relationships**
```
Before closing note, provide investor with:
- List of existing major contracts (>$[X] value)
- Key vendor relationships
- Customer contracts

Covenant can specify: "Cannot enter NEW contracts >$X,
                     but existing contracts approved as listed"

Protects you from approval requirement for:
- Renewing existing vendor contracts
- Expanding with existing customers
- Maintaining ongoing relationships

Prevents: "Oh, you already signed that? Now you need approval."
```

**Strategy 5: Sunset Approval Rights**
```
Negotiate covenant expiration:

"Investor approval rights for major decisions terminate upon:
  (a) Closing of Series A financing, OR
  (b) 30 months after Effective Date, OR
  (c) Company achieving >$X monthly recurring revenue"

Once these conditions met:
- Covenants end
- You have full operational control
- Investor becomes standard equity holder

Creates natural transition:
- Early stage: more investor protection
- Later stage: more founder freedom
```

**Strategy 6: Pre-Authorize Key Strategic Items**
```
Work with investor to pre-approve certain strategic moves:

"Investor approves Company pursuing:
  1. Expansion into [market segment] if <$[X] initial investment
  2. Hiring VP of Sales at salary <$[X] + equity
  3. Customer contracts with [type customer] if terms within [range]
  4. Equipment/technology purchases <$[X]
  5. Strategic partnerships in [domains]"

Benefit:
- You can move on these without approval
- Investor feels comfortable (they pre-approved direction)
- Reduces future disputes
- Gives you operational freedom within agreed parameters
```

**Strategy 7: Dispute Resolution Process**
```
If dispute about whether action requires approval:

"If Company and Noteholders disagree whether action requires approval,
parties shall:
  1. Meet in person/video within 3 business days
  2. Good faith discussion of business merits (not legal argument)
  3. If unresolved after discussion, Board vote (if investor has seat)
  4. If still unresolved, independent advisor review within 10 days"

This:
- Forces conversation before escalation
- Prevents lawyer-driven obstruction
- Provides neutral mechanism for disputes
- Keeps business moving

Alternative: "Company may proceed if notice given and no objection
             within 5 business days" (approval by silence)
```

**Key Takeaway:**
Control provisions are real restrictions on your operational freedom. Negotiate them carefully. Limit to truly major items (M&A, liquidation, major debt), set clear dollar thresholds, and carve out operational decisions. If you can't get good terms, use SAFEs instead (no covenants).

## Negotiation Strategies

### Pre-Negotiation Preparation

**Step 1: Build Your Investment Narrative**
```
Prepare compelling story:
- Team pedigree (prior exits, accomplishments)
- Traction (revenue, users, growth metrics)
- Market opportunity (size, growth, why now)
- Why convertible is better than equity (speed, deferring valuation)

Use this to justify:
- Lower interest rate ("strong team deserves 4%")
- Higher valuation cap ("huge market warrants $[X]M cap")
- Longer maturity ("Series A realistically 24 months away")
- Lighter covenants ("we'll update quarterly, no approval needed")
```

**Step 2: Understand Investor Type**
```
Different investors have different standard terms:

Accelerators (Y Combinator, etc.):
  - Want lowest interest (0-2%)
  - Minimal covenants
  - Expect conversion within 12-18 months
  - Board observation rights

Angel Investors:
  - More flexible (4-8% interest)
  - Some information rights
  - Longer maturity acceptable (4-5 years)
  - Rarely want approval rights

Venture Capital:
  - Standard terms (5-7% interest)
  - Information rights mandatory
  - Board observation rights likely
  - Control provisions on major items

Know investor type to calibrate expectations
```

**Step 3: Know Your BATNA**
```
BATNA = Best Alternative To Negotiated Agreement

If negotiation fails, what else can you do?

BATNA Options:
  A) Raise from different investors (gives you walking power)
  B) Use SAFEs instead of convertible notes (lower conflict)
  C) Raise smaller round, stretch burn rate (don't take this investor)
  D) Revenue-based financing (if you have revenue)
  E) Bootstrap/debt from other sources (maintains control)

Stronger BATNA = stronger negotiating position

If you have multiple interested investors:
  "Other investor offering 4% at $6M cap, what can you do?"

If no BATNA:
  "I'll take whatever terms you want" (weak position)
  Better: "I need to think about this, can I get back to you?"
```

### Key Negotiation Points & Strategies

**1. Interest Rate (your most important lever)**

```
RANGE: 2-10% (depending on stage, team, market)

YOUR TARGET: 4-5% (reasonable for early-stage)
INVESTOR TARGET: 7-8% (standard market)

Negotiation approach:
  You: "I want 3% interest given our team strength"
  Investor: "Market is 6%, we can do 5.5%"
  You: "5.5% is workable if you do X and Y"
       [Ask for longer maturity + automatic extension]

Leverage points for lower rate:
  ✓ Team track record (prior exits, successful founders)
  ✓ Traction (revenue, user growth, compelling metrics)
  ✓ Timing (Series A very likely within 18 months)
  ✓ Round structure (you're raising efficient amount, clean)
  ✓ Investor syndication (other investors buying in)
  ✓ Relationship (existing relationship with investor)

Counter-leverage (what investor uses):
  ✗ Risk (pre-revenue = higher risk = higher rate)
  ✗ Scarcity (this investor has other deals)
  ✗ Market conditions (tight market = higher rates)
  ✗ Portfolio pressure (need return on capital)

Compromise points:
  If stuck at 6-7%, trade for:
  - Longer maturity (4-5 years vs 3)
  - Automatic extension (cover delay)
  - Lower interest in early period (3% year 1-2, then 6%)
  - Annual interest payment (not accrued)
  - Warrant coverage (kickers to upside)

REMEMBER: Every 1% difference = $50K over 3 years on $500K note
Worth spending hours on this negotiation
```

**2. Valuation Cap**

```
CAP = Maximum valuation for conversion calculation
RANGE: 2-10x seed investment amount

YOUR TARGET: 4-6x (gives investors upside, protects you)
INVESTOR TARGET: 2-3x (gives them huge upside)

Negotiation approach:
  You: "Cap should be 5x seed ($2.5M for $500K seed)"
  Investor: "Market is 3x maximum"
  You: "3x is too low for our traction. We'll do 4.5x"

Leverage points for higher cap:
  ✓ Traction (revenue, growth justifies higher cap)
  ✓ Team (strong team allows higher cap)
  ✓ Market (huge TAM justifies ambitious cap)
  ✓ Investor positioning (cap is fair for risk/reward)

Compromise:
  If stuck between 3x and 5x:
  - Negotiate 4x cap (middle ground)
  - Get anti-dilution protection (weighted average, not full ratchet)
  - Get MFN clause (future better terms auto-apply)

IMPORTANT:
  Cap is most important to investor
  Interest rate is most important to you

  If investor won't move on cap, trade for lower interest:
  "OK, 3x cap if you reduce interest from 6% to 4%"
```

**3. Maturity Date**

```
TYPICAL: 3-5 years
YOUR TARGET: 4-5 years (gives more time for Series A)
INVESTOR TARGET: 3 years (forces conversion earlier)

Negotiation approach:
  You: "Series A realistically takes 24-30 months from now.
       Need 4 years minimum for maturity date."
  Investor: "3 years is standard, that's plenty of time"
  You: "3 years is tight. 4 years + auto-extension works?"

ABSOLUTELY negotiate for:
  - Minimum 4 years (not 3)
  - Automatic extension clause (12-month extension at higher rate)
  - Pre-Series A waiver (if Series A in progress, extends automatically)

These are non-negotiable
```

**4. Discount Rate**

```
DISCOUNT = Reward to early investor for taking risk
RANGE: 15-30%

YOUR TARGET: 20% (standard)
INVESTOR TARGET: 25-30% (more reward for early stage)

Negotiation approach:
  You: "20% discount is fair for Series A risk"
  Investor: "25% is market for pre-revenue companies"
  You: "We have revenue, 20% is appropriate"

This is less negotiable (investors expect 20-25%)
Don't fight hard here
Trade for better cap or interest rate instead

Rule of thumb:
  - 20% discount if you have revenue
  - 25% discount if pre-revenue
  - 30% discount if very early/risky stage
```

**5. Protective Covenants**

```
These are most negotiable

YOUR TARGET: Information rights only (no approval)
INVESTOR TARGET: Approval rights on major items

Negotiation approach:
  You: "I can give you quarterly financial reporting and board
       observation, but need operational control for execution"
  Investor: "OK, but need approval on: merger, liquidation,
            major debt, business change"
  You: "I can handle that. Let's define 'major debt' as >$250K"

Negotiation checklist:
  [ ] Approval only on truly major items (M&A, liquidation, major debt)
  [ ] Clear dollar thresholds ($[X] specified amount)
  [ ] Carve out: hiring, pricing, product, operations
  [ ] Automatic termination upon Series A or [trigger]
  [ ] Notice + 5 days (not approval, just notification)
  [ ] Exemptions for Board-approved annual plan

Most investors will accept this if you're clear
```

### Difficult Negotiation Scenarios

**Scenario 1: Investor Demands Board Seat**
```
INVESTOR: "We need a Board seat for this check"
YOU: "That's unusual for seed round"
INVESTOR: "Non-negotiable"

RESPONSE OPTIONS:

Option A: Decline
  "A seed investor board seat is premature. We'll use observer
   rights instead. Let's revisit for next round."

  When to use: You have other investors, don't need this capital

Option B: Negotiate Conditions
  "Board seat OK if:
    1. You commit to follow-on investment (pro-rata rights)
    2. You get quarterly board option grants (share upside)
    3. Seat disappears upon Series A unless you lead"

  When to use: You want this investor, but want terms

Option C: Hybrid Structure
  "Board observation rights (you attend, participate, but don't vote)
   + Quarterly calls (dedicated 1-hr investor call)"

  When to use: Investor wants involvement without formal seat

REALITY: If investor demands board seat and you need capital, you'll
likely accept. Understand that this gives them significant control.
```

**Scenario 2: Investor Wants Warrant Coverage**
```
INVESTOR: "We want warrants for 10% of the note amount"
YOU: "That's dilution I didn't plan for"

WHAT THIS MEANS:
  $500K note + 10% warrant coverage = right to buy $50K worth of
  stock at favorable price

  This is additional dilution in Series A

NEGOTIATION:
  You: "Warrants are unusual for convertible notes"
  Investor: "Standard for higher-risk stages"

  Compromise options:
  - No warrants (you prefer this)
  - 5% warrant coverage (split difference)
  - Warrants only if note doesn't convert by maturity
  - Warrants at 2x conversion price (less favorable to investor)

WHEN TO ACCEPT:
  If investor offering:
  - Lower interest (e.g., 4% instead of 6%)
  - Higher cap (e.g., 5x instead of 3x)

  Then warrants might be acceptable trade-off

WHEN TO REFUSE:
  If investor already getting good terms
  (low interest, high cap, good discount)
  Don't give additional upside
```

**Scenario 3: Investor Wants MFN + Discount + Cap**
```
INVESTOR: "We want 20% discount, $5M cap, AND MFN rights"
YOU: "That's too much optionality"

ISSUE:
  - Discount: 20% is fine
  - Cap: $5M might be fair
  - MFN: Means if future investor gets better terms, you auto-upgrade

  The problem: Together, these are excessive protection

NEGOTIATION:
  You: "I can do 20% discount + $5M cap, but not MFN"
  Investor: "Why not MFN?"
  You: "MFN means you auto-upgrade to future better terms,
       limiting my flexibility. I'll instead give you better
       base terms (lower interest or higher cap)"

  Compromise:
  - Offer MFN only for cap (not discount or interest)
  - Or MFN only if you raise multiple notes at same time
  - Or MFN with sunset (expires if Series A not in 18 months)

REALITY: If investor insists, you'll probably accept
But negotiate other terms better to compensate
```

## Red Flags & Warning Signs

### Red Flags IN THE INVESTOR

```
1. PRESSURE TO RUSH
   "We can close this in 2 days"
   "No time to negotiate, deal closes Friday"

   Red flag: Investor rushing means less due diligence by them
            AND less time for you to review

   Response: "Happy to move fast, but I need [X] time to review
            with legal counsel. Let's target Monday close."

   If investor won't wait: Something is wrong

2. UNWILLINGNESS TO PROVIDE REFERENCES
   "No, I don't give references"
   "That's confidential, can't share other companies"

   Red flag: If investor won't let you talk to previous companies,
            probably because those companies had bad experience

   Response: "I need to reference at least one of your investments"

   If investor refuses: PASS

3. UNUSUAL TERM REQUESTS
   "We want call option if note converts below valuation X"
   "We get control of next financing"
   "We want liquidation preference equal to equity investors"

   Red flag: These are highly unusual terms suggesting:
            - Investor has bad experience (being defensive)
            - Investor mistrusts founders
            - Investor doesn't understand convertible structure

   Response: "These terms go beyond standard convertible
            structure. Can we discuss your underlying concerns?"

   If investor insists on unusual terms: RED FLAG
```

### Red Flags IN THE TERMS

```
1. INTEREST OVER 10%
   "We want 12% interest"

   Red flag: >10% suggests investor sees very high risk
            or is opportunistic/predatory

   Reasonable ranges:
   - Pre-revenue: 5-8%
   - Early revenue: 4-7%
   - Strong traction: 3-5%

   If offered >10%: PASS or negotiate hard

2. VALUATION CAP <2X SEED AMOUNT
   "Cap is 1.5x seed amount"

   Red flag: Investor pricing in massive failure
            or is being opportunistic

   Realistic caps:
   - Pre-revenue, early stage: 2-3x
   - Traction: 3-5x
   - Strong traction: 4-6x

   If offered <2x: This is predatory

3. MATURITY <2 YEARS
   "Maturity is 18 months"

   Red flag: 18 months is not realistic for Series A
            Investor is either:
            - Doesn't understand Series A timeline
            - Expects default/repayment (not conversion)
            - Will pressure you for extension

   Minimum acceptable: 3 years
   Target: 4-5 years

4. AUTOMATIC CONVERSION AT MATURITY
   "If no Series A by maturity, automatically converts to equity
    at price of [predatory formula]"

   Red flag: This forces conversion at potentially horrible price
            Better to default than convert at 10x valuation

   Better: "Automatic conversion at 90% average valuation
          of prior 12 months" (more fair)

5. RATCHET ANTI-DILUTION
   "Full ratchet anti-dilution protection"

   Red flag: Full ratchet is most founder-hostile protection
            Very rare in seed stage

   What it means: If Series A is down round, investor gets
                 massive additional shares

   If investor insists on full ratchet: Either:
   - Pass on this investor, OR
   - Get much lower interest rate (trade-off)

   Better: "Weighted average anti-dilution" (more fair)
```

### Red Flags IN YOUR SITUATION

```
1. YOU NEED THIS CAPITAL DESPERATELY
   "I have 3 weeks of runway, need this funding"

   Red flag: Investor will sense urgency, demand bad terms

   Lesson: Never fundraise from "need" position
          Fundraise from "growth" position

   If in desperate position:
   - Take the best terms you can get
   - Focus on speed, not optimization
   - Plan to raise second round with better terms
   - Plan to refinance/extend note after traction

2. YOU'RE RAISING MULTIPLE SMALL NOTES
   "I'm raising from 20 angel investors, $25K each"

   Red flag: This creates:
            - Complex cap table (20 noteholders)
            - Coordination problems (need all to agree on extension)
            - Governance nightmare (20 approval rights)

   Better: Raise from fewer investors at larger size
          Or use SAFEs (no approval/extension issues)

   If already doing this: Expect maturity chaos

3. YOU DON'T HAVE LEGAL COUNSEL
   "I'll just use the template from AngelList"

   Red flag: You're signing binding legal document without lawyer
            One missed provision costs thousands

   Reality: Spend $2K-$5K on legal counsel
           This is non-negotiable
           Protects you from mistakes

4. YOU'RE TAKING NOTES FROM PEOPLE YOU DON'T TRUST
   "My uncle offered to invest $100K"

   Red flag: Business relationships with family often end badly
            Need clear boundaries

   If taking from family:
   - Use professional terms (no special treatment)
   - Have lawyer draft agreement (not handshake)
   - Set clear expectations on updates/communication
```

## Decision Framework

### Should You Take This Convertible Note?

Use this framework to decide:

**FILTER 1: CAPITAL NEED TEST**
```
Question: Do you genuinely need this capital to reach Series A?

YES ✓  → Continue evaluation
NO ✗   → Don't take it, focus on efficiency/revenue
        Unnecessary capital = unnecessary dilution + complexity
```

**FILTER 2: INVESTOR QUALITY TEST**
```
Question: Is this investor someone you want helping you for 3-5 years?

Evaluate:
  ✓ Will they help with intros, customers, hires?
  ✓ Do you trust their judgment?
  ✓ Do they understand your business?
  ✓ Will they be supportive vs. controlling?
  ✗ Are they red flag behaviors (pressure, unusual demands)?

Bad investor with OK terms > Good investor with bad terms
        (You'll work with them for years)

If investor is problematic → PASS
If investor is good → Continue
```

**FILTER 3: TERMS TEST**
```
Scorecard:

Interest Rate:
  [ ] 3-4% = Excellent (0 points)
  [ ] 5-6% = Good (1 point)
  [ ] 7-8% = OK (2 points)
  [ ] 9%+ = Bad (3 points)

Valuation Cap:
  [ ] 4-6x seed = Excellent (0 points)
  [ ] 3-4x seed = Good (1 point)
  [ ] 2-3x seed = OK (2 points)
  [ ] <2x seed = Bad (3 points)

Maturity:
  [ ] 4-5 years = Excellent (0 points)
  [ ] 3-4 years = Good (1 point)
  [ ] 2-3 years = OK (2 points)
  [ ] <2 years = Bad (3 points)

Covenants:
  [ ] Info rights only = Excellent (0 points)
  [ ] Limited approval on major items = Good (1 point)
  [ ] Approval on moderate decisions = OK (2 points)
  [ ] Extensive approval rights = Bad (3 points)

Discount:
  [ ] 20% = Good (0 points)
  [ ] 25% = OK (1 point)
  [ ] 30%+ = Bad (2 points)

SCORING:
  0-2 points: Excellent terms, take the capital
  3-5 points: Good terms, take the capital
  6-8 points: OK terms, consider negotiating
  9-12 points: Bad terms, PASS unless desperate

Remember: You can negotiate. This scorecard is starting point.
```

**FILTER 4: SERIES A READINESS TEST**
```
Question: Are you likely to raise Series A within realistic timeline?

Assess honestly:
  [ ] Do you have product-market fit indicators?
  [ ] Is market demand clear?
  [ ] Do you have revenue or strong user growth?
  [ ] Does your team have credibility?
  [ ] Is market environment favorable for raising?

0-1 checkmarks = Series A unlikely in 24 months → Don't take note
               (Maturity will be problem)
2-3 checkmarks = Series A somewhat likely → OK to take note
4-5 checkmarks = Series A very likely → Great for note

If Series A unlikely: Use SAFE instead (no maturity pressure)
                    Or bootstrap with revenue
```

**FILTER 5: ALTERNATIVE OPTIONS TEST**
```
Question: What else could you do?

[ ] I have other investors who might fund at better terms
[ ] I could raise a SAFE instead of convertible note
[ ] I could generate revenue instead of raising capital
[ ] I could take venture debt instead
[ ] I could reduce burn to extend runway

If good alternatives exist: Negotiate harder or walk away
If this is only option: Take the best terms you can get
```

### Decision: GO / NO-GO

```
GO (take the capital) if:
  ✓ You pass Filters 1-4 (capital need, investor quality, good terms)
  ✓ Alternative options are worse
  ✓ Investor is supportive and trustworthy
  ✓ Series A timeline is realistic
  ✓ Terms are acceptable after negotiation

NO-GO (pass on capital) if:
  ✗ You fail any of Filters 1-4
  ✗ Investor is showing red flags
  ✗ Better alternative options exist (bootstrap, SAFE, revenue)
  ✗ Series A timeline uncertain (use SAFE instead)
  ✗ Terms are predatory even after negotiation

MIDDLE GROUND: Accept capital but with modification plan
  Example: Take this note, but plan follow-on SAFE round
          Or take this note, but aggressively target Series A in 18 months
          Or take this note, but build bridge plan for maturity
```

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: The Maturity Crisis

**Company: EarlyStage SaaS Startup**

Timeline:
```
Month 0: Raise $300K convertible note @ 6%, maturity 3 years
         - Principal: $300K
         - Interest: 6% annually
         - Plan: Series A in 18 months

Months 1-12: Strong growth
  - Revenue: $0 → $10K/month
  - Team: 2 → 8 people
  - Series A discussions starting with 3 VCs

Months 13-24: Growth continues, Series A stalls
  - Revenue: $10K → $30K/month
  - Series A investors want $50K/month before investing
  - Multiple term sheet rejections
  - Timeline slips to Month 30

Month 24: Noteholder gets concerned
  - Remaining 12 months until maturity
  - Series A not closed
  - Sends email: "What's the plan for maturity?"

Months 25-30: Panic phase
  - Founder frantically trying to close Series A
  - Noteholder pressuring for clarity
  - Series A finally closes at Month 30
  - Note converts successfully

LESSON:
Company was lucky. Series A closed just before maturity.
But narrow escape created stress for 6 months.

If Series A delayed 6 more months:
- Maturity at Month 36
- Series A still not closed
- Forced to choose:
  a) Repay $318K (principal + interest) - no cash
  b) Default - damage to Series A prospects
  c) Negotiate extension - shows distress to Series A investors
  d) Forced sale at bad terms - to get cash for repayment

HOW TO AVOID:
- Negotiate 4-5 year maturity (not 3)
- Include automatic 12-month extension clause
- Track Series A progress quarterly
- By Month 18, should have clear path to Series A
- If Series A unlikely by Month 18, raise bridge financing
```

### Case Study 2: The Interest Accrual Surprise

**Company: Mobile App Company**

Timeline:
```
Month 0: Raise $500K convertible note @ 8%, maturity 3 years
         Investor Agreement:
         - Principal: $500K
         - Interest: 8% (accrues, not paid)
         - Discount: 20%
         - Cap: $4M

         Founder assumption: "Interest won't matter, we'll convert"

Month 18: Series A discussions begin
         - Startup has $2M ARR
         - Series A investors offer $5M @ $15M post-money
         - Expect to own 25% (for $5M at $15M post-money)

Month 20: Series A closing prep
         - Accountant calculates: Interest accrued = $80K
         - Convertible Note converts $580K (not $500K)
         - Conversion price calculation:
           Cap = $4M / 1,000,000 shares = $4/share
           Discount price = $15 price × (1 - 0.20) = $12/share
           Use Cap (lower) = $4/share
         - Shares from note = $580K / $4 = 145,000 shares

         Updated cap table:
         - Series A investor: 333K shares @ $15 = $5M
         - Old investors: 250K shares
         - Note conversion: 145K shares (MORE THAN EXPECTED)
         - Total: 728K shares (vs 667K expected)

         Effective ownership:
         - Series A: 45.7% (expected 50%)
         - Old investors: 34.3% (expected 37%)
         - Note: 19.9% (expected 15%)

         Series A investor now owns LESS than expected
         (due to extra interest-accrual shares)

Series A investor reaction:
  "We were expecting 50% ownership, getting 45.7%.
   Either you raise more capital to hit 50%, or we revise offer."

Negotiation result:
  - Series A investor demands $5.5M (to get to 50%)
  - Company now facing $500K more dilution
  - All because of $80K interest accrual

LESSON:
Interest accrual is real and impacts cap table meaningfully.
$80K accrual led to $500K+ additional negotiation.

HOW TO AVOID:
- Negotiate lowest possible interest (4% vs 8%)
- Track interest accrual quarterly
- Model Series A with interest included
- Plan for 30-40% growth from interest in convertible amount
- Communicate interest accrual to Series A investors early
- Consider paying interest annually (if you have cash)
```

### Case Study 3: The Control Provision Trap

**Company: B2B Software Company**

Timeline:
```
Month 0: Raise $250K convertible note
         Investor is successful angel with many board seats
         Covenants include: "Cannot merge/sell without approval"

Month 12: Strategic acquisition opportunity
         - Competitor company in financial distress
         - Available for $150K acquisition
         - Would accelerate product roadmap by 18 months
         - Make Series A much easier to raise

Founder approach:
  "This is acquisition of $150K, not merger of company.
   Doesn't require investor approval. Let's do it."

Investor response (after review):
  "The covenant says 'Cannot merge/sell'. Acquisition of another
   company to integrate their product is de facto merger of
   product lines. Requires approval."

Negotiation:
  - Founder: "This is standard acquisition, not material"
  - Investor: "Material to our rights. Need to review. 3 weeks."
  - Timeline: Competitor found other acquisition offer
  - Result: Opportunity lost in 2 weeks

Series A impact:
  - Founder had to build product expansion from scratch
  - Delayed Series A by 6 months
  - Series A was smaller than if acquisition had happened
  - All because of vague covenant language

LESSON:
Vague covenant language ("cannot merge") creates disputes.

HOW TO AVOID:
- Define terms precisely
  Instead of: "Cannot merge"
  Use: "Cannot merge with other entity or acquire >80% assets"

- Carve out small acquisitions
  Instead of: blanket ban
  Use: "Cannot acquire for >$[X] without approval.
       Can acquire smaller strategic assets (<$[X]) with notice only"

- Involve investor in strategic discussions early
  Instead of: coming to investor with done deal
  Use: "We're looking at strategic acquisitions <$200K.
       OK to pursue if terms reasonable?"

- Build in quick approval path
  Instead of: 3-week review period
  Use: "Notice within 2 days, approval within 5 days if no response"
```

## Key Takeaways for Founders

**1. Convertible notes are debt - real obligations**
   - Balance sheet liability is real
   - Maturity date is real
   - Repayment obligation is real
   - Don't treat as "non-binding" or "pure equity play"

**2. Interest accrual compounds the cost**
   - Every 1% interest = ~$50K over 3 years on $500K note
   - Negotiate lowest possible rate (3-5%)
   - Track interest accrual quarterly
   - Plan Series A to convert before excess accrual

**3. Maturity date creates timeline pressure**
   - You MUST convert or repay by maturity
   - Default timeline at maturity is tight
   - Longer maturity (4-5 years) is essential
   - Build automatic extension into terms

**4. Control provisions restrict your freedom**
   - Approval rights slow decision-making
   - Investor relationships matter as much as terms
   - Negotiate to limited list (M&A, major debt, liquidation only)
   - Set clear dollar thresholds for automatic items

**5. Balance sheet liability affects future financing**
   - Impacts debt-to-equity ratios
   - Creates covenant issues with banks
   - Can complicate venture debt
   - Be transparent with future lenders/investors

**6. Series A timeline is everything**
   - Company most likely to convert in Series A
   - Series A realistically takes 24-30 months from seed
   - Plan maturity for 4+ years to cover timeline uncertainty
   - Build Series A focus into quarterly discussions with investors

**7. Investor quality matters more than terms**
   - You'll work with these investors for 3-5 years
   - Good investor + OK terms > Bad investor + good terms
   - Reference checks with previous companies are critical
   - Investor network and support matter

**8. Negotiation is expected and welcomed**
   - Investors expect negotiation
   - Aggressive negotiation doesn't scare them away
   - Compromise on less important items (discount) to win on important (maturity)
   - Use team strength and traction as leverage
