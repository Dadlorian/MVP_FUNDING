## Overview

### What You're Getting

When you invest in a convertible note, you're getting:

1. **Debt instrument** - legally binding debt obligation from company
2. **Conversion rights** - converts to equity at future event (usually Series A)
3. **Downside protection** - interest + conversion discount + valuation cap
4. **Upside participation** - convert to equity at favorable terms

### Why Convertible Notes Exist

**Without convertible notes:**
```
Early-stage funding options for founders:
  A) Equity round - requires valuation discussion
     Problem: Founder doesn't know realistic valuation yet
     Takes 3-4 months, expensive legal process

  B) Debt from banks - requires collateral
     Problem: Startups have no collateral
     Banks don't lend to pre-revenue companies

Solution: Convertible note
  - Defers valuation discussion
  - Gets capital fast (2-3 weeks)
  - Investors get equity conversion + interest
  - Founders get capital without premature valuation
```

## Why Invest Via Convertible Notes

### Advantages for Investors

**Advantage 1: Multiple Layers of Return**

```
Scenario: You invest $100K convertible note

THREE sources of return:

1. INTEREST ACCRUAL (guaranteed)
   - 5% annually = $5K/year
   - If note held 2 years = $10K interest (automatic)
   - Even if company fails, you're ahead of equity investor

2. CONVERSION DISCOUNT (rewarded)
   - Series A price = $10/share
   - Your discount = 20%
   - You buy at $8/share
   - Equity investor at $10/share
   - Extra 2,000 shares from discount = reward for early risk

3. VALUATION CAP (protected)
   - If Series A very high, cap protects you
   - Example: Series A at $20M, cap at $5M
   - You use $5M for calculation = massive advantage
   - You get 4x more shares than if no cap

Triple return sources:
- Interest: guaranteed return even if company fails
- Discount: automatic equity advantage
- Cap: protection if company succeeds spectacularly
```

**Advantage 2: Reduced Valuation Argument**

```
Without convertible note (equity round):
  Founder: "Company should be valued at $3M"
  You: "Company pre-revenue, should be $1M"
  (negotiation takes weeks, is contentious)

With convertible note:
  You: "Let's defer valuation. Use cap of $5M later"
  Founder: "OK, we have $500K capital, can build for 18 months"
  (argument avoided, capital transferred in 1 week)

Benefit: Saves time, money, reduces friction
         Founders more willing to take notes vs equity
```

**Advantage 3: Simpler Legal Process**

```
Equity round:
  - Draft STOCK PURCHASE AGREEMENT (30+ pages)
  - Draft INVESTOR RIGHTS AGREEMENT (20+ pages)
  - Draft AMENDED ARTICLES OF INCORPORATION (5+ pages)
  - Draft VOTING AGREEMENT (10+ pages)
  - Total: 100+ pages, 4-8 weeks, $15K-$30K legal costs

Convertible note:
  - Draft CONVERTIBLE NOTE AGREEMENT (15+ pages)
  - Possibly TERM SHEET (5 pages)
  - Total: 20 pages, 1-2 weeks, $2K-$5K legal costs

Benefit: Faster close, lower costs
         Can close multiple notes simultaneously
```

**Advantage 4: Flexible on Timing**

```
Equity round: Must decide terms upfront
- Board seat? Yes/No
- Anti-dilution? What type?
- Liquidation preference? How much?
- Governance rights? What exactly?
(All negotiated before capital transfer)

Convertible note: Most terms deferred
- Valuation? Deferred to Series A
- Governance? Minimal at seed
- Board seat? Not yet
- These settled at Series A when positions clarified

Benefit: Easier to invest now, detail negotiation later
```

### When to Use Convertible Notes (vs Other Options)

**Use Convertible Notes for:**
```
✓ Pre-revenue companies with strong team
✓ First-check follow-on to SAFE/equity round
✓ Portfolio diversification (many small checks)
✓ When you want conversion discount protection
✓ When you want interest return component
✓ When you want multiple layers of returns
✓ If valuation cap negotiation matters to you
```

**Use SAFEs for:**
```
✓ Very early stage (MVP, founders still figuring out)
✓ When you're happy with truly lightweight agreement
✓ Accelerator portfolio companies
✓ When maturity date creates too much burden
✓ When you want minimal governance
✓ Quick capital transfer without legal overhead
```

**Use Equity for:**
```
✓ Later stage (Series A or beyond)
✓ When you want clarity on ownership immediately
✓ When you want board seat/governance
✓ When you want defined liquidation preferences
✓ When valuation is defensible
✓ When you want to define investor rights clearly
```

## Key Risks You're Taking

### Risk 1: Default Risk - Company Fails Before Series A

**What Can Happen:**
```
You invest $100K convertible note in pre-revenue company.

Company timeline:
  Month 0: Raise capital, launch product
  Month 3: 100 users sign up, initial traction
  Month 6: Usage plateaus, business model unclear
  Month 12: Team conflict, founder leaves
  Month 15: Company shuts down

Your position:
  You're a CREDITOR (debt holder)
  In bankruptcy waterfall:
    1. Employee wages
    2. Vendor payables
    3. Taxes
    4. Senior debt (if any)
    5. YOU (convertible note - junior debt)
    6. Equity shareholders

Reality: Typically nothing left by time you get paid
Recovery: Often $0 or <10% of investment
```

**Your Protection Levels:**

```
Protection Level 1: Diversification
- Don't invest all capital in one note
- Expect 1-3 of 10 investments will fail
- Plan for 70% to hit, 20% to fail, 10% to return capital

Protection Level 2: Team & Traction
- Invest in founders with track record
- Require minimum product/market signals
- Ask for customer references or user metrics
- De-risk through diligence

Protection Level 3: Financial Covenants
- Negotiate minimum cash requirements
- Get quarterly financial reporting
- Watch burn rate vs runway
- Flag if runway <12 months

Protection Level 4: Information Rights
- Quarterly financial updates mandatory
- Get alerts on material adverse changes
- Monitor progress toward Series A milestones
- Red flags trigger action (more capital? pivot? wind down?)

Protection Level 5: Conversion Timing
- Earlier conversion = lower default risk
- If Series A likely in 18 months, you're protected
- If Series A timeline uncertain, your risk is higher
```

**Practical Default Management:**

```
Monitoring approach:

Month 0-3: Founder check-ins (informal, excited)
  Investor contact: Monthly chat
  Red flags: Difficulty hitting initial metrics

Month 3-6: Formal tracking
  Investor contact: Quarterly updates required
  Metrics: Revenue, users, churn, burn rate
  Red flags: Plateau, increased churn, leadership changes

Month 6-12: Intensive monitoring
  Investor contact: Monthly updates
  Focus: Series A progress, funding runway
  Red flags: No Series A pipeline, burn rate increasing, departures

Month 12-18: Series A Pressure
  Investor contact: Bi-weekly calls
  Focus: Series A progress, market feedback, deal status
  Red flags: Series A conversations stalling, market downturn

Month 18-24: Series A Critical
  Investor contact: Weekly
  Action: Help with intros, co-invest if Series A possible

If default apparent (Month 12 without Series A progress):
  Option 1: Bridge financing (to get to Series A)
  Option 2: Company wind-down (minimize losses)
  Option 3: Restructure note (extend maturity, lower interest)

Expect to stay actively engaged. Passive investment doesn't work for convertible notes.
```

### Risk 2: Conversion Price Uncertainty - Series A Mispricing

**What Can Happen:**
```
You invest: $100K @ $5M cap, 20% discount
Timeline assumption: Series A in 18 months at $15M+ valuation

Scenario A: Series A at $10M valuation (down round)
  Your conversion:
    Cap = $5M / 1M shares = $5/share
    Discount = $10 price × (1-0.20) = $8/share
    You use cap (lower) = $5/share ✓
  Result: You get excellent terms (cap saved you)
  But company is in trouble (Series A at lower valuation = bad sign)
  Your equity is worth less (down round = reduced value)

Scenario B: Series A at $3M valuation (massive down round)
  You get favorable conversion price ($3M cap), but:
  - Company only worth $3M (half of cap)
  - Your $100K investment converts to equity worth $[X]
  - But equity stake in troubled company
  - Likely fails or acquires at fire-sale price

Scenario C: Series A never happens
  Note reaches maturity without conversion
  Must decide:
    A) Demand repayment (company has no cash)
    B) Extend maturity (accept further uncertainty)
    C) Convert to equity at negotiated price (likely unfavorable)
    D) Accept haircut and be done (lose $[X])

Scenario D: Series A at $30M (wildly up round)
  Cap = $5M / 1M shares = $5/share
  Price = $30/share
  You use cap (massively favorable) ✓
  You get 4x the shares of Series A investor
  But: Founder is diluted (unhappy)
       Series A investor is unhappy (paying for your discount)
       Potential conflict in cap table
```

**How to Manage Conversion Uncertainty:**

```
Strategy 1: Smart Cap Setting
- Cap too low ($2M for $500K seed): You win, founders lose
- Cap too high ($10M for $500K seed): You lose protection
- Smart cap: 4-6x seed amount = balanced risk/reward
  Example: $500K seed → $2.5M-$3M cap = fair to both

Strategy 2: Understand Qualified Financing Trigger
- How is "Series A" defined?
- What if multiple small rounds happen instead?
- What if acquirer buys company (IPO alternative)?
- Get clarity upfront

Strategy 3: Model Multiple Scenarios
- Optimistic: Series A at $15M valuation
- Realistic: Series A at $8M valuation
- Pessimistic: Series A at $4M valuation
- Worst case: No Series A (conversion price negotiated)

- Model your return in each scenario
- Make sure even pessimistic scenario is acceptable

Strategy 4: Discount + Cap Interaction
- Discount protects you in high valuation rounds
- Cap protects you in ambiguous Series A scenarios
- Together they provide dual protection
- Better than having only one protection

Strategy 5: Monitor Series A Progress
- Year 1: Any Series A discussions?
- Month 18: Do you have Series A pipeline?
- Month 20: Have term sheets been shared?
- If no Series A progress by Month 18: RED FLAG
  Time to have difficult conversation with founders
  Maybe bridge financing needed
  Or company restructuring

Strategy 6: MFN (Most Favored Nation) Clause
- If future convertible note has better terms,
  you automatically get upgraded terms
- Protects against: founder issuing better terms to later investor
- Worth negotiating for
```

### Risk 3: Subordination - You're Junior Creditor

**What This Means:**

```
Capital Structure:

TIER 1 (SENIOR): Bank debt ($500K)
  - Secured by company assets
  - Gets paid first in bankruptcy
  - Can force company to do things (covenants)

TIER 2 (SUBORDINATE): Your convertible note ($100K)
  - Unsecured (no assets pledged)
  - Gets paid after bank debt
  - Limited contractual control

Example liquidation scenario:

Company liquidates, realizes $300K in assets:
  Bank gets: $300K (they're senior)
  You get: $0 (nothing left)
  Equity holders get: $0 (last in line)

You're ahead of equity holders, but behind senior debt.
```

**Subordination Risk Management:**

```
Due Diligence Questions:

1. SENIOR DEBT DETAILS
   "What senior debt does company have?"
   - Bank line of credit? Amount? Covenants?
   - Equipment financing? Amount?
   - Any guarantees from founders?

   Red flag: Large senior debt relative to company size
   Risk: If bank forecloses, you're wiped out

2. SUBORDINATION AGREEMENT
   "Is there a subordination agreement?"
   - Should outline your rights vs senior creditor
   - When can you enforce payment?
   - What happens if senior creditor defaults?

   Red flag: No subordination agreement (unclear who's first)

3. PAYMENT RESTRICTIONS
   "When can company pay me my interest/principal?"
   - Can't pay noteholders if senior debt in default
   - Can't pay if would violate bank covenants
   - Can't pay if would bring cash below minimum

   Red flag: Restrictive payment terms (you never get paid)

4. FUTURE SENIOR DEBT
   "Can company incur new senior debt?"
   - Can they take new bank loan subordinate to this?
   - What's the limit?
   - Who approves?

   Red flag: Unlimited senior debt (dilutes your priority)

5. CONVERSION WATERFALL
   "What happens if Series A closes?"
   - You convert to equity
   - Senior debt remains as company obligation
   - You're now equity (junior to new preferred)
   - This is OK - equity typically junior to preferred

   Not a red flag per se, but understand your position post-conversion
```

**Subordination Strategy:**

```
Best case: No senior debt
- You're senior to all other creditors except employees/taxes
- Clear repayment/conversion path

OK case: Moderate senior debt (<3x your investment)
- Senior debt exists but not overwhelming
- Conversion likely happens before senior debt becomes issue
- Request subordination agreement with clear terms

Bad case: Large senior debt (>5x your investment)
- Senior debt is substantial obligation
- Your priority is questionable if company struggles
- Higher default risk for you
- May want higher interest (to compensate)
- Consider not investing unless:
  - Senior debt mature before your maturity
  - Senior debt unlikely to be called
  - Strong Series A plan de-risks situation

Management strategy:
- Get copy of senior debt agreement (understand covenants)
- Monitor senior creditor's calls (they may restrict company)
- If Series A happening, make sure senior debt gets paid off
  (otherwise, post-Series A cap table is messy with junior debt)
- Have quarterly cash flow discussions (make sure you get paid)
```

### Risk 4: Limited Governance Rights - You're Not in Control

**Your Actual Rights vs Perception:**

```
What you own: DEBT (convertible to equity)
Your rights: Conversion + interest + some information

What you DON'T own: Voting control
Your non-rights:
  - Can't vote on board members
  - Can't vote on major decisions
  - Can't fire CEO
  - Can't change strategy
  - Can't prevent dilution (dilution happens to equity)
  - Can't force sale of company
  - Can't mandate dividend

If founder disagrees with your advice, you have no enforcement mechanism
Exception: Protective provisions (if negotiated)
```

**What Control You Can Get:**

```
Negotiable governance rights:

1. INFORMATION RIGHTS (most important)
   - Quarterly financial statements (balance sheet, income, cash flow)
   - Annual budget
   - Monthly/quarterly updates on progress
   - Notification of material events (key hires, customer losses, litigation)
   - Right to audit books

   This is standard, push for it

2. BOARD OBSERVATION RIGHTS (important if large check)
   - Can attend board meetings
   - Can participate in discussion (but don't vote)
   - Get board materials in advance
   - Helpful for understanding strategy
   - Less common for seed rounds, more common for larger checks

   This is negotiable, push for it if check >$250K

3. PROTECTIVE PROVISIONS (medium importance)
   - Requirement that noteholders approve [specific items]:
     a) Merger/acquisition
     b) Sale of substantially all assets
     c) Liquidation/winding up
     d) Major debt issuance (>$[X])
     e) Materially change business

   - These require noteholder (majority) approval
   - Gives you some veto power
   - But typically only on material items
   - Note: conflicts with founder's operational control

   Negotiate these carefully - don't overreach

4. PRO-RATA RIGHTS (important for downside)
   - If company raises future financing, you can participate
   - Maintain your ownership percentage
   - Right to invest more capital

   This is valuable, push for it

5. ANTI-DILUTION PROTECTION (important for full ratchet)
   - Usually weighted average (standard, fair)
   - Sometimes full ratchet (rare, founder-hostile)
   - Protects you if company does down round

   Weighted average is standard, push for it if not offered

6. MFN (Most Favored Nation) CLAUSE (medium importance)
   - If future convertible notes have better terms,
     you auto-get the better terms
   - Prevents founder from issuing notes with lower discount/cap
   - Useful but not critical (most terms same across notes)

   Worth pushing for, low founder cost
```

**Governance Risk Management:**

```
The key insight: You're passive investor with some downside protection.
You're not control investor.

Management approach:

1. DON'T expect to control company strategy
   - Founders make decisions
   - You can advise/suggest
   - But you can't mandate

2. FOCUS on information rights
   - Most valuable right you'll have
   - Allows you to monitor and flag issues early
   - Enables you to take action if problems arise

3. BUILD relationship with founders
   - Strong relationship = more influence
   - Open communication helps early problem detection
   - Your network/advice becomes valuable (not just capital)
   - Founders more receptive to your input

4. SET EXPECTATIONS upfront
   - During negotiation: "I'll be supportive, hands-off investor"
   - Or: "I'm actively engaged investor, expect regular contact"
   - Misaligned expectations create problems

5. ESCALATE issues early
   - If Series A pipeline not developing by Month 18: talk to founders
   - If key hire leaves: understand implications
   - If burn rate accelerating: discuss path forward
   - Don't ghost for 2 years then be surprised

6. KNOW when to push back
   - If founders propose dilutive financing: push back
   - If they want to pivot away from business: discuss implications
   - If they want to hire expensive people: question ROI
   - These are opportunities to add value

7. KNOW when to accept founder judgment
   - You're not operator, they are
   - Some decisions will surprise you
   - But trust their judgment unless clear red flags
   - Remember: your success depends on their execution

Key principle: You have protective rights (information, some veto),
not control rights. Act accordingly.
```

## Due Diligence Checklist

### Pre-Investment Due Diligence

Use this checklist before committing capital:

#### 1. FOUNDER DUE DILIGENCE

```
[ ] BACKGROUND CHECKS
    [ ] Verify founder claimed experience
    [ ] Check LinkedIn/previous roles
    [ ] Talk to previous employers/colleagues
    [ ] Any litigation history?
    [ ] Any bankruptcy history?
    [ ] Any criminal history?

[ ] FOUNDER REFERENCES
    [ ] Talk to 2-3 companies founder previously worked at
    [ ] Ask about: work style, judgment, follow-through
    [ ] Ask about: failures and how they handled them
    [ ] Any red flags mentioned?

[ ] FOUNDER TRACK RECORD
    [ ] Prior startup experience?
    [ ] Prior exits? Acquisitions? IPOs?
    [ ] Why did previous companies succeed/fail?
    [ ] What would founder do differently this time?

[ ] FOUNDER COMMITMENT
    [ ] Is founder full-time? (must be)
    [ ] Any other jobs or distractions? (red flag if yes)
    [ ] Willing to commit 3-5 years? (early stage needs long commitment)
    [ ] Equity stake? (must be significant - 20%+)

[ ] FOUNDER RELATIONSHIPS
    [ ] Are co-founders getting along?
    [ ] Roles clearly defined? (founder + CTO + business person?)
    [ ] Any relationship friction?
    [ ] Exit clauses if co-founder leaves?

[ ] FOUNDER RECEPTIVENESS
    [ ] Open to feedback?
    [ ] Defensive about criticism?
    [ ] Willing to pivot if data suggests?
    [ ] Take responsibility for failures?
    [ ] Overly confident or realistic about challenges?

RED FLAGS:
  ✗ Founder with no skin in the game (equity stake < 20%)
  ✗ Part-time founder with other job
  ✗ Founder with previous litigation/legal issues
  ✗ Founder unable to name specific customers
  ✗ Founder defensive about market validation
  ✗ Co-founder conflict evident
```

#### 2. MARKET DUE DILIGENCE

```
[ ] MARKET SIZE
    [ ] Total Addressable Market (TAM) size
    [ ] Is market large enough? ($1B+ TAM is good)
    [ ] Any third-party market research supporting size?
    [ ] Growth rate of market?
    [ ] Market trend (growing/shrinking)?

[ ] CUSTOMER DEMAND
    [ ] Founder can name specific target customers
    [ ] Founder has talked to customers (how many? 5+?)
    [ ] Customers expressing interest? (letters of intent?)
    [ ] Customers willing to pay? (how much?)
    [ ] Any paying customers yet?

[ ] COMPETITION
    [ ] Who are direct competitors?
    [ ] Why is company better than competition?
    [ ] Defensible advantage? (technology, team, data, distribution?)
    [ ] Can company sustain advantage? (or easily replicated?)
    [ ] Customer switching cost (high or low?)

[ ] GO-TO-MARKET CLARITY
    [ ] How will company acquire customers?
    [ ] Customer acquisition cost (CAC) estimated?
    [ ] Customer lifetime value (LTV) estimated?
    [ ] Is LTV > 3x CAC? (basic business model test)
    [ ] Distribution already exist or need to build?

[ ] PRODUCT MARKET FIT SIGNALS
    [ ] Working MVP?
    [ ] Early customers using product?
    [ ] Net Promoter Score (NPS)?
    [ ] Repeat usage/retention metrics?
    [ ] Customer enthusiasm?
    [ ] Revenue traction? (even if small)

RED FLAGS:
  ✗ Founder can't name specific target customers
  ✗ No customer conversations
  ✗ Large market but no clear why company will win
  ✗ LTV < 3x CAC (business doesn't math)
  ✗ No MVP or all vaporware
  ✗ Founder's view of competition disconnected from reality
```

#### 3. FINANCIAL DUE DILIGENCE

```
[ ] FINANCIAL STATEMENTS
    [ ] P&L (profit and loss / income statement)
    [ ] Balance sheet (assets, liabilities, equity)
    [ ] Cash flow statement (where money comes from/goes)
    [ ] Quality: are they audited, reviewed, or just compiled?
    [ ] Any accounting issues?

[ ] BURN RATE
    [ ] Monthly spend? ($[X]/month)
    [ ] Runway with this capital? (how many months?)
    [ ] Runway is sufficient? (12+ months is good, <6 months is risky)
    [ ] Any plans to reduce burn or reach profitability?

[ ] REVENUE (IF ANY)
    [ ] Monthly recurring revenue (MRR)?
    [ ] Revenue growth rate?
    [ ] Unit economics? (cost to deliver service)
    [ ] Gross margin?

[ ] CAP TABLE
    [ ] See complete cap table
    [ ] Verify founder equity stakes
    [ ] Any options/warrants outstanding?
    [ ] Any other convertible notes outstanding?
    [ ] Any preferred shareholders?
    [ ] Full dilution realistic?
    [ ] Any equity disputes (locked up founders, etc.)?

[ ] DEBT OBLIGATIONS
    [ ] Any bank debt? Amount? Maturity?
    [ ] Any equipment financing?
    [ ] Any other convertible notes?
    [ ] Subordination hierarchy clear?
    [ ] Are covenants in bank debt being met?

[ ] FINANCIAL PROJECTIONS
    [ ] Does company have financial model?
    [ ] Assumptions documented?
    [ ] Realistic or overly optimistic?
    [ ] Path to profitability shown?
    [ ] Path to Series A ($[X]M revenue?) shown?

[ ] USE OF PROCEEDS
    [ ] What will this $[X] capital be used for?
    [ ] Broken down: product (%), sales (%), team (%)?
    [ ] Timeline for deployment?
    [ ] How will it get them to Series A?

RED FLAGS:
  ✗ Financial statements unclear or not available
  ✗ Burn rate extremely high (monthly spend > expected revenue)
  ✗ Runway < 6 months without clear funding path
  ✗ Projections are clearly overly optimistic (2x revenue growth/month?)
  ✗ Cap table complex or unclear
  ✗ Founder equity stake too small (< 20%)
  ✗ Large bank debt with unclear maturity (subordination risk)
```

#### 4. LEGAL/IP DUE DILIGENCE

```
[ ] LEGAL STRUCTURE
    [ ] Company properly incorporated?
    [ ] State of incorporation? (Delaware preferred)
    [ ] Certificate of incorporation exists and current?
    [ ] Bylaws in place?
    [ ] Corporate governance proper? (board meetings, records?)

[ ] INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
    [ ] Does company own its key IP?
    [ ] Patents filed? (utility patents, provisional patents?)
    [ ] Trademarks registered?
    [ ] Trade secrets protected? (NDAs, employee agreements?)
    [ ] IP assignments from founders/employees documented?
    [ ] Any IP disputes or claims?

[ ] EMPLOYEE AGREEMENTS
    [ ] Stock option plans in place?
    [ ] 83(b) elections filed for founders?
    [ ] Employee offer letters exist?
    [ ] Non-compete/Non-disclosure agreements in place?
    [ ] Key employee equity grants locked in?

[ ] CONTRACTS
    [ ] Any customer contracts with unusual terms?
    [ ] Any vendor contracts with long commitment?
    [ ] Any partnership agreements?
    [ ] Are commercial terms favorable to company?

[ ] REGULATORY/COMPLIANCE
    [ ] Any regulatory approvals needed?
    [ ] Company in compliance with regulations?
    [ ] Any pending litigation or disputes?
    [ ] Insurance in place?
    [ ] Data privacy/GDPR compliance?

[ ] PREVIOUS FINANCING
    [ ] Previous financing documents (SAFEs, notes, etc.)?
    [ ] Any side letters or amendments?
    [ ] Any anti-dilution or MFN clauses outstanding?
    [ ] Any liquidation preferences or preferences granted?

RED FLAGS:
  ✗ Company incorporated in uncertain state (not Delaware)
  ✗ IP ownership unclear (founder disputes, previous employer claims)
  ✗ Employee equity not properly documented
  ✗ Pending litigation undisclosed
  ✗ Regulatory compliance uncertain
  ✗ Previous financing with complex anti-dilution (limits future raises)
```

#### 5. SERIES A READINESS

```
[ ] IS SERIES A REALISTIC?
    [ ] Timeline to Series A? (how many months?)
    [ ] Series A fundraising plan documented?
    [ ] Target VCs identified?
    [ ] Any Series A conversations happening?
    [ ] Founder has Series A fundraising experience?

[ ] SERIES A PREREQUISITES
    [ ] Revenue or strong traction metric?
    [ ] Team complete (founder + CTO/product + business)?
    [ ] Product fully functional (not beta)?
    [ ] Customer validation (letters of intent? paying customers?)
    [ ] Board/advisor network that adds credibility?

[ ] MARKET CONDITIONS
    [ ] Is fundraising environment good for this market segment?
    [ ] Are comparable companies raising at reasonable valuations?
    [ ] Any market headwinds?

[ ] VALUATION CAP REASONABLENESS
    [ ] Is proposed cap reasonable? (should be 3-6x seed amount)
    [ ] Does cap make sense given market opportunity?

RED FLAGS:
  ✗ No Series A plan or timeline
  ✗ Series A unlikely in 18-24 months
  ✗ Founder has never raised Series A before
  ✗ No team yet (just idea + one founder)
  ✗ No customers or validation
  ✗ Series A cap is too low (less than 2x seed) or too high (>10x seed)
```

## Negotiation Strategies

### Key Negotiation Points

#### 1. VALUATION CAP

**Your Goal:** Lower cap (more favorable to you)

```
CAP FRAMEWORK:

Pre-revenue / zero traction:
  Cap = 2-3x seed amount
  Example: $500K seed → $1.5M cap

Early traction (some users/revenue):
  Cap = 3-5x seed amount
  Example: $500K seed → $2.5M cap

Strong traction (meaningful revenue/users):
  Cap = 4-6x seed amount
  Example: $500K seed → $3M cap

Negotiation:
  You: "Company has no revenue yet, cap should be $1.5M (3x)"
  Founder: "We have [X] users and [Y] early interest, cap should be $3M"
  Compromise: $2.25M (middle ground)

Key leverage points:
  ✓ Company traction: more traction = higher cap acceptable
  ✓ Founder track record: proven founder = higher cap acceptable
  ✓ Market size: large TAM = higher cap acceptable
  ✓ Competitive intensity: much competition = higher cap acceptable

Don't accept:
  ✗ Cap < 2x seed (too founder-hostile)
  ✗ Cap > 8x seed (you lose discount protection)
  ✗ Undefined cap (must be specific number)
```

#### 2. DISCOUNT

**Your Goal:** Higher discount (20-25% typical)

```
DISCOUNT FRAMEWORK:

Standard discount: 20-25%
  Most common range
  Accounts for risk reduction from seed → Series A

Aggressive discount: 25-30%
  For very early stage / risky
  Requires founder acceptance

Low discount: 15-20%
  For later-stage / strong traction
  Harder to justify

Negotiation:
  You: "We want 25% discount given early stage"
  Founder: "20% is standard"
  Compromise: 22% or agree to 20%

Note: Discount is less negotiable than cap
      Investors generally expect 20%+
      Founders more willing to compromise here

Trade-off: If founder resists, offer:
  "OK, 20% discount if you give us higher cap ($3M instead of $2.5M)"
```

#### 3. INTEREST RATE

**Your Goal:** Higher rate (5-8% typical)

```
INTEREST RATE FRAMEWORK:

Pre-revenue, very early: 6-8%
  Company is risky
  Seed funding rare or late stage

Early stage, some traction: 5-7%
  Company has early metrics
  Team somewhat proven

Later stage, strong traction: 3-5%
  Company de-risked somewhat
  Clear path to Series A

Negotiation:
  You: "Given pre-revenue stage, we want 7% interest"
  Founder: "4% is what other investors offered"
  You: "Let me talk to those other investors..."
  Founder: "OK, 6% as compromise"

Note: Interest rate highly negotiable
      Founders see as cost of capital
      You see as risk premium

Leverage:
  ✓ You have other investors (creates competition)
  ✓ Interest expense (founder cares about this)
  ✓ Risk profile (higher risk = higher rate)
```

#### 4. MATURITY

**Your Goal:** Shorter maturity (3 years minimum)

```
MATURITY FRAMEWORK:

3 years: Founder prefers (longer = more time for Series A)
2 years: Investor prefers (forces conversion faster)

Realistic: 3-4 years
  Series A takes 24-30 months from seed
  2-year maturity creates unrealistic pressure
  4-5 year maturity removes urgency

Negotiation:
  You: "We want 3-year maturity to create appropriate timeline"
  Founder: "5 years gives us breathing room"
  Compromise: 4 years with auto-extension clause

Key: Build in automatic extension
  "If no Series A by Year 3, auto-extend 1 year at [X]% rate"
  This avoids negotiation nightmare at maturity
```

#### 5. COVENANTS (APPROVAL RIGHTS)

**Your Goal:** Some protective provisions

```
WHAT TO PUSH FOR (in order of importance):

Must have:
  [ ] Information rights (quarterly reporting)
  [ ] Notice of material adverse change
  [ ] Board observation rights (if check > $250K)

Should have:
  [ ] Approval for: merger, asset sale, liquidation
  [ ] Approval for: new senior debt > $[X]
  [ ] MFN clause (future better terms apply to you)

Nice to have:
  [ ] Approval for CEO change
  [ ] Approval for business pivot
  [ ] Pro-rata rights for future rounds

Negotiation:
  You: "We need approval on merger/liquidation and senior debt"
  Founder: "That's too restrictive"
  You: "Only for material items. Approval doesn't apply to hiring,
       product decisions, pricing, etc."
  Founder: "OK, those make sense"

Key: Be specific, not vague
  Don't say: "Cannot materially change business"
  Do say: "Cannot merge/sell > 80% assets without approval"
```

## Exit Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Series A Financing (Expected Path)

**What Happens:**

```
Timeline:
  Month 18-24: Series A fundraising active
  Month 24-30: Series A closes

At Series A Closing:
  1. Series A investors invest capital (e.g., $5M)
  2. Your convertible note automatically converts to Common Stock
  3. You receive stock certificate for converted shares
  4. Series A investors receive Preferred Stock

Conversion calculation:
  Conversion Price = Lower of:
    - Discount method: Series A price × (1 - discount%)
    - Valuation cap method: Cap / fully-diluted shares

  Shares for you = (Principal + Accrued Interest) / Conversion Price

Post-Series A Cap Table Example:
  Series A Preferred: [new investors] - [X]%
  Founder Common: [founders] - [Y]%
  Your Common: [converted shares] - [Z]%
  Employee options: [pool] - [pool %]
  Total: 100%

Your next decision:
  - Participate in Series A financing yourself (pro-rata rights)?
  - Hold Common Stock (junior to Preferred)?
  - Help recruit Series A investors (earn additional shares)?

Typical founder reaction:
  "My convertible note turned into equity!
   I now own [Z]% of company valued at $[Series A valuation]
   My investment is now [# shares] worth approximately $[Series A share price × shares]"
```

**This is the Base Case Scenario - Most Notes Convert This Way**

### Scenario 2: Acquisition Before Series A

**What Happens:**

```
Timeline:
  Month 12-18: Company gets acquisition interest
  Month 18-24: Acquisition negotiations
  Month 24: Acquisition closes

At Acquisition Closing:
  1. Acquirer buys company
  2. This may be considered a "Change of Control" per Note
  3. You must decide:
     - Option A: Convert to equity (take stock in acquired company)
     - Option B: Receive cash repayment (principal + interest + possible premium)

Your decision factors:
  - What's the deal price? (is acquirer overpaying or underpaying?)
  - What's your share would be post-conversion?
  - Is acquirer strong/weak? (equity value risk?)
  - Do you want equity or prefer cash?

Example:
  Acquisition price: $10M (for entire company)
  Your note: $100K principal + $10K interest accrued = $110K

  Option A (Equity): Convert to [X] shares, own [Y]%
  - Your equity value = [Y]% × $10M = ~$[Z]
  - But you only invested $100K
  - Upside = [Z] - $100K

  Option B (Cash): Receive $110K cash
  - Straightforward repayment
  - Get your capital back + interest
  - No upside to deal

  Most notes specify: You get option to choose (equity or cash)
  Smart: choose whichever is better for you

Typical outcome:
  - If acquisition price is high ($50M+): Take equity (participate in upside)
  - If acquisition price is low ($5M): Take cash (get capital back safely)
```

### Scenario 3: Successful Acquisition / Profitable Exit

**What Happens:**

```
Timeline:
  Year 3-5: Company becomes successful
  Year 4-5: Acquisition for strategic reasons (or IPO)

Acquisition price: $[X]M (significant)

If your equity stake post-Series A:
  Your ownership: [Y]%
  Acquisition price: $[X]M
  Your exit value: [Y]% × $[X]M

Example:
  Your original investment: $100K
  Converted to: 5% equity
  Acquisition price: $50M
  Your exit: 5% × $50M = $2.5M

  Return: $2.5M / $100K = 25x return
  (excellent outcome)

Typical successful exit timeline:
  Seed → Series A (18-24 months) → Series B (24 months) →
  Series C (18-24 months) → Acquisition/IPO

Your convertible note was step 1 in this journey
Converting early pays off in ultimate acquisition
```

### Scenario 4: Company Fails / No Series A by Maturity

**What Happens:**

```
Timeline:
  Year 0: Invest in company
  Year 1-2: Company traction slows
  Year 2.5: Clear Series A not happening
  Year 3: Maturity date arrives

Your options:

OPTION A: DEMAND REPAYMENT
  Founder has no cash
  Company cannot pay
  You're back to debt collection
  Likely: Accept significant haircut to settle

OPTION B: EXTEND MATURITY
  Agree to extend another 12-18 months
  Possibly at higher interest rate
  Hope company turns around
  Risk: Company still doesn't succeed, extends pain

OPTION C: CONVERT TO EQUITY AT NEGOTIATED PRICE
  Example: Agree to convert at reasonable valuation
  You become equity holder
  Hope for future acquisition or turn-around
  Risk: Equity likely worthless if company failing

OPTION D: COMPANY WIND-DOWN
  Company shutting down
  Limited assets to liquidate
  You're subordinate to employees/taxes/senior debt
  Likely recovery: $0-25% of investment

Most likely outcome:
  Company is struggling, cannot repay
  You negotiate extension or equity conversion
  At discount to original investment (haircut)
  Accept it and move on

Real scenario example:
  Original investment: $100K
  Year 3: Company failing, cannot repay $110K
  Negotiated outcome: Convert to equity worth $30K-50K
  You've lost $50K-70K on investment
  (This is venture investing - some will fail)

How to position yourself best:
  - If this path emerging: Don't wait until maturity
  - Month 18: If Series A not on horizon, have hard talk with founders
  - Month 24: If still no Series A, start planning exit/extension
  - Month 28: Negotiate extension or debt restructure early
  - Don't wait until Month 36 maturity - too late, founder stressed
```

### Scenario 5: IPO

**What Happens:**

```
Rare path (only 0.1-1% of startups)

Timeline:
  Year 0: You invest $100K via convertible note
  Year 3-5: Company raises Series A, B, C
  Year 7-10: Company becomes large ($100M+ revenue)
  Year 8-10: IPO

At IPO:
  Your convertible note converted to Common Stock at Series A (Year 3)
  You've held equity through growth phase
  Company IPOs at $[X]/share

Your Common Stock Value:
  If you own [Y] shares of Common Stock
  IPO price: $[X]/share
  Your equity value: [Y] × $[X]

Example:
  Original investment: $100K
  Converted to: 100K shares @ $1/share (series A conversion)
  IPO valuation: Company now worth $5B
  Dilution from Series B, C: Your shares diluted to 0.01% ownership
  Your equity value at IPO: 0.01% × $5B = $500K

  Return: $500K / $100K = 5x return
  (still good, but less than acquisition scenario due to dilution)

More realistic:
  Most startup investors don't hold through IPO
  They sell at Series B/C or in secondary markets
  IPOs are rare and far away
  Series A → acquisition is more common path
```

### Management of Multiple Scenarios

**As an investor, plan for:**

```
Portfolio of 10 convertible notes:
  - 2-3 will fail completely (0% return)
  - 3-4 will get acquired at modest return (2-5x)
  - 2-3 will get acquired or Series B at good return (5-15x)
  - 1-2 will be exceptional (15x+)
  - 1 might be IPO or near-IPO (25x+)

Expected return across portfolio: ~5-8x (on average)
  But concentrated in 2-3 good outcomes

This is why:
  - Portfolio approach is necessary (don't invest in just one)
  - Selection is critical (pick good founders, hot markets)
  - Follow-on investment helps (invest more in winners, less in losers)
  - Information rights matter (catch losers early, take action)

For each convertible note:
  - Assume failure possible (diversify accordingly)
  - Hope for Series A acquisition (base case)
  - Plan for extension if Series A delayed (not renegotiation at maturity)
  - Stay engaged quarterly (don't ghost, track progress)
  - Know when to cut losses (if red flags clear, don't follow-on)
```

## Key Takeaways for Investors

**1. Convertible notes provide multiple return sources**
   - Interest (guaranteed if held to maturity)
   - Discount (automatic equity advantage)
   - Valuation cap (protection in high valuation scenarios)

**2. You're taking real risks**
   - Default risk (company fails pre-Series A)
   - Conversion uncertainty (Series A mispricing)
   - Subordination (junior to senior debt)
   - No control (not voting shareholder)

**3. Due diligence is essential**
   - Founders: track record, commitment, judgment
   - Market: validation, competition, TAM
   - Financial: burn rate, runway, unit economics
   - Legal: IP ownership, employee agreements, debt obligations

**4. Negotiations matter**
   - Cap is most important to you (de-risks high valuations)
   - Interest rate is important (return component)
   - Covenants matter (protective provisions)
   - Maturity flexibility critical (auto-extend is valuable)

**5. Post-investment management is critical**
   - Quarterly oversight required (don't ghost)
   - Information rights are most valuable right you have
   - Value-add beyond capital (introductions, advice)
   - Red flags require action (early problem detection)

**6. Exit scenarios are multiple**
   - Series A (base case, ~70% of outcomes)
   - Acquisition pre-Series A (~15%)
   - Failure (~10%)
   - IPO (~5%)

**7. Portfolio approach is necessary**
   - Diversify across many notes
   - Expect some failures
   - Winners pay for losers
   - Follow-on investment in winners increases returns
