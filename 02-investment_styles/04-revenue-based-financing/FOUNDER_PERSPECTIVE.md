## Is RBF Right for You?

### Quick Self-Assessment

**Check all that apply to your situation:**

- [ ] I have **predictable monthly revenue** ($10K+)
- [ ] My revenue is **growing consistently** (10%+ month-over-month)
- [ ] I have **6+ months of runway** without new funding
- [ ] I **don't want to give up equity** to investors
- [ ] I need capital **within the next 2-3 months** (not 6+ months)
- [ ] I'm comfortable with **monthly financial reporting** to investors
- [ ] My **revenue trend is stable** or improving (not declining)
- [ ] I can afford **3-8% of monthly revenue** as a payment
- [ ] I'm **okay with a cap** on investor returns (2x-2.5x)
- [ ] My business model has **predictable unit economics**

**Scoring:**
- **8-10 checks**: RBF is an **EXCELLENT** fit ✓✓✓
- **6-7 checks**: RBF is a **GOOD** option ✓✓
- **4-5 checks**: RBF could work but **has risks** ⚠️
- **<4 checks**: RBF is **NOT RECOMMENDED** ✗

## How to Prepare

### Step 1: Get Your Financials in Order (Months -3 to -2)

#### Required Documents:

**Monthly Financial Statements** (past 12 months):
- [ ] Profit & Loss (income statement)
- [ ] Cash flow statement
- [ ] Balance sheet
- [ ] Monthly revenue breakdown by product/service
- [ ] Customer count and retention metrics

**Example format:**
```
Month | Gross Revenue | Refunds | Net Revenue | Operating Costs | Net Profit
------|---------------|---------|-------------|-----------------|----------
Jan   | $150,000      | -$10K   | $140,000    | $100,000       | $40,000
Feb   | $175,000      | -$12K   | $163,000    | $105,000       | $58,000
Mar   | $190,000      | -$15K   | $175,000    | $110,000       | $65,000
```

**Revenue Breakdown** (show what drives your revenue):
```
SaaS Subscriptions:        65% of revenue
One-time Purchases:        25% of revenue
Services/Consulting:       10% of revenue

Total Monthly Revenue: $175,000
```

#### Pro Tips for Financial Preparation:

1. **Use professional accounting software** (QuickBooks, FreshBooks, Stripe)
2. **GAAP-compliant**: Use ASC 606 revenue recognition standard (what investors expect)
3. **Consistent records**: If you've been informal, clean up NOW before pitching
4. **Separate personal expenses**: Business and personal finances must be clearly separated
5. **Document refund policy**: Investors will question how you handle returns

**Timeline for Getting Ready:**
- Week 1-2: Pull together all financial documents
- Week 3: Clean up accounting and reconcile accounts
- Week 4: Create 24-month revenue forecast
- Week 5: Prepare executive summary of financials
- Week 6: Ready to pitch

### Step 2: Create 24-Month Revenue Forecast

**This is CRITICAL.** Investors make decisions based on your growth assumptions.

#### Build Your Forecast

**Approach A: Bottom-Up (Detailed)**

```
Starting Point: Current Monthly Revenue = $150,000

Customer Cohort Analysis:
├─ Existing customers (Month 0):     $120,000/month
│  └─ Churn rate: 3%/month
│     = $120K × 0.97^12 = $94K by year 2
│
├─ New customers (Month 1+):         $30,000/month
│  └─ New customer acquisition: +$5K/month
│     = $30K + ($5K × 12) = $90K by year 2
│
└─ Total Year 2 Revenue Projection: $94K + $90K = $184K/month

Forecast:
Month 1-3:  $150K (baseline, no growth yet)
Month 4-6:  $160K (new customer growth kicks in)
Month 7-12: $170K-$175K (compound growth from chorts)
Month 13-24: $185K-$200K (full 2-year projection)
```

**Approach B: Top-Down (Simpler)**

```
Current Revenue:  $150,000/month
Growth Assumption: 15% month-over-month

Month 1:  $150,000
Month 2:  $172,500 (15% growth)
Month 3:  $198,375 (15% growth)
...
Month 12: $816,000 (15% × 12 months)

Year 1 Total: $3,200,000
Year 2 Total: $6,800,000+
```

#### Keys to a Credible Forecast:

1. **Be Conservative**: Investors assume your forecast is 50% optimistic
2. **Show Your Math**: Revenue = [Customers] × [ARPU] × [Churn Adjustment]
3. **Segment by Revenue Stream**: SaaS, products, services, partners
4. **Document Your Assumptions**: "I expect 20% new customer growth based on..."
5. **Provide Multiple Scenarios**:
   - **Conservative Case** (50% of base)
   - **Base Case** (your expected outcome)
   - **Upside Case** (if everything goes well)

**Investor Expectations by Business Type:**

| Business Type | Typical Growth | Notes |
|--------------|----------------|-------|
| **SaaS** | 10-20% MoM | Stable, predictable |
| **E-commerce** | 15-30% MoM | Seasonal, variable |
| **Services** | 5-15% MoM | Dependent on team |
| **Marketplace** | 20-40% MoM | Network effects |
| **Mobile App** | 30-50% MoM | Fast scaling, risky |

```
Total Timeline: 6-12 weeks (4-8 weeks typical)
```

## Pitch & Due Diligence

### What Investors Look For

**1. Predictable Revenue** (Most Important)
- Investors want revenue that is **stable and growing**
- Week-to-week or month-to-month volatility is a red flag
- Examples of good revenue:
  - SaaS subscriptions: same customers each month
  - E-commerce: growing orders, predictable churn
  - Services: retainer clients, recurring projects

**2. Growth Trajectory**
- Consistent month-over-month growth preferred
- 10%+ monthly growth is attractive
- Even 5% growth is okay if revenue is stable
- Declining or flat revenue is a major concern

**3. Unit Economics**
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC) < 12 month lifetime value (LTV)
- Healthy gross margins (60%+)
- Positive unit economics = sustainable growth

**4. Founder Capability**
- Have you run a business before?
- Do you have relevant industry experience?
- Can you execute the growth plan?
- Are you coachable and reasonable?

**5. Financial Controls**
- Accurate, timely financial reporting
- Clear revenue recognition policy
- Professional accounting (not spreadsheets)
- Auditable records

### Red Flags That Kill RBF Deals

**Investors RUN from:**

1. **Revenue Declining** ✗
   - If showing downward trend, forget RBF
   - Investors know declining revenue = extended payback

2. **One Customer >50% of Revenue** ✗
   - Customer concentration risk is huge
   - If that customer leaves, deal is broken

3. **Cannot Explain Revenue**
   - "I don't actually know how much we made last month" 🚩
   - Numbers don't reconcile with bank deposits 🚩
   - Founder seems uncertain about metrics 🚩

4. **Messy Financial Records** ✗
   - Shoebox accounting
   - Personal and business expenses mixed
   - Inconsistent revenue recognition

5. **Previous Investor Bad Relationships** ✗
   - Don't trash talk past investors
   - If you fought with previous investors, RBF investor worries you'll fight with them too

6. **Unrealistic Growth Projections** ✗
   - "I'm going to 10x revenue in 12 months with no customers yet"
   - Investor immediately discounts your numbers by 50%+

### Due Diligence Questions to Expect

#### Revenue & Business Model

1. **"Tell me about your revenue streams."**
   - Be specific: "65% from SaaS subscriptions, 25% product sales, 10% services"
   - Show trend: "Growing 20% month-over-month for last 6 months"

2. **"Walk me through your last 12 months of revenue."**
   - Be ready with: monthly breakdown, major changes, reasons for dips
   - Prepared chart: Month-by-month revenue with narrative

3. **"What's your customer retention rate?"**
   - If SaaS: "95% monthly retention, 85% annual"
   - If e-commerce: "60% repeat customer rate"
   - Investor is checking if revenue is really sustainable

4. **"How much concentration risk do you have?"**
   - Answer: "Top 10 customers are 25% of revenue"
   - Red flag if: "Top 3 customers are 50%+"

5. **"What changed from [month X] to [month Y] in your revenue?"**
   - Be honest about seasonal fluctuations, one-time deals, etc.
   - Show understanding of what drives your numbers

#### Metrics & Unit Economics

6. **"What's your gross margin?"**
   - Should be: 50%+ (varies by business)
   - Calculate: (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue

7. **"What's your customer acquisition cost (CAC)?"**
   - Calculate: Total sales & marketing spend / New customers acquired
   - Should be recovered within 12 months of revenue

8. **"What's your lifetime value (LTV)?"**
   - LTV = Average customer value × Average customer lifetime
   - Should be: LTV > 3x CAC (at minimum)

9. **"How many customers do you have and is that growing?"**
   - Matters less than revenue growth, but shows trajectory
   - Investor wants to see customer count increasing

#### Forecast & Use of Funds

10. **"Walk me through your 24-month forecast."**
    - Show the math: [Customers] × [ARPU] × [Retention]
    - Explain assumptions: "20% new customer growth based on current pipeline"
    - Show sensitivity: "This assumes 15% growth - here's what happens if it's 10%"

11. **"What are you using the capital for?"**
    - Have a detailed use of funds breakdown
    - Example: "40% for sales & marketing ($[AMOUNT]), 30% for engineering ($[AMOUNT]), 20% operations"
    - Investor wants to see you use capital to ACCELERATE revenue growth

12. **"Why do you think you'll hit these numbers?"**
    - Be confident but grounded
    - "We have a proven sales process that acquired [X] customers at [Y] cost"
    - "We have [Z] customers in pipeline"

#### Founder & Team

13. **"Why are you the right person to build this?"**
    - Your relevant experience
    - Why you're well-positioned to succeed
    - What have you accomplished before?

14. **"Who else is on your team and what are they great at?"**
    - Investor evaluates founder + team capability
    - Strong team is a massive plus

#### Financial Controls & Reporting

15. **"How do you track revenue? Can you show me?"**
    - Demo your accounting system
    - Show how you track month-by-month
    - Can you pull a report right now?

16. **"How often do you review your financials?"**
    - Should be: Weekly or monthly
    - If: "I don't really look at them" = red flag

### How to Prepare for Due Diligence

**1 Month Before Closing (Typical Timeline):**

```
Week 1-2: Prepare Core Documents
├─ 12 months of bank statements (clean, organized)
├─ Accounting records showing revenue calculation
├─ Customer contracts (top 10 customers)
├─ Clear invoice/order documentation
└─ Verification of major claims (awards, press coverage)

Week 3: Prepare Supporting Materials
├─ Customer references (2-3 people investor can call)
├─ Tax returns (personal and business)
├─ Proof of business ownership and authority to make this decision
├─ Insurance documentation
└─ Any legal agreements (loans, partnerships, etc.)

Week 4: Prepare for Verification
├─ Bank reconciliation (ensure all revenue deposits match reported numbers)
├─ Customer count verification (list of customers and their monthly spend)
├─ Churn analysis (which customers left, when, why)
├─ Sample invoices showing your revenue recognition
└─ Forecast model (detailed assumptions, not just numbers)
```

**Pro Tips:**
- **Make it easy**: Organize documents in a folder, clearly labeled
- **Be transparent**: If there are issues (unusual refunds, one-time deals), explain upfront
- **Have context**: "In April, we had an unusual $50K sale to [customer]. Here's the contract."
- **Don't hide**: Investor will find discrepancies - better to explain proactively

## Negotiating Terms

### Key Terms to Understand

#### 1. Revenue Share Percentage

**What It Means**: Percentage of your monthly revenue that goes to investor

**Typical Range**: 2%-8% per month (median: 4%)

**How It's Set**:
```
Larger investment or riskier business     → Higher % (6-8%)
Smaller investment or proven revenue      → Lower % (2-4%)
B2B SaaS                                  → Lower % (2-4%)
E-commerce or service-based              → Higher % (4-6%)
```

**Your Negotiation Strategy**:

```
Ideal: Get 3% or lower
Acceptable: 3-4%
Challenging: 5-6%
Too High: >6%

If investor wants 6%, counter with:
├─ "Can we do 4% with growth milestones?"
├─ "What if we do 5% first 12 months, then 3% after?"
└─ "I saw comparable deals at 4% - can you meet there?"
```

#### 2. Repayment Cap Multiple

**What It Means**: Maximum total amount investor receives

**Typical Range**: 1.3x - 3.0x of investment (median: 2.0x)

**Examples**:
```
$250K investment at 1.5x cap = $375K total return
$250K investment at 2.0x cap = $500K total return
$250K investment at 2.5x cap = $625K total return
```

**Why Cap Matters to You**:
- Lower cap = you stop paying sooner
- Your upside: all revenue after cap is 100% yours
- Example: If you hit $500K/month revenue by month 24, cap ending means you keep $500K going forward

**Your Negotiation Strategy**:

```
If investor wants 2.5x cap, counter with:
├─ "Can we do 2.0x with 4% revenue share?" (adjust both terms)
├─ "Let's do 2.5x but with declining revenue share (5% → 3%)"
└─ "I need 2.0x to make the math work for growth"

Rationale to give:
- Lower cap = faster return to investor (not all risk on you)
- Growth after cap = mutual benefit (you grow, they're done)
```

#### 3. Term Length

**What It Means**: How long the revenue sharing continues

**Typical Options**:
- **Perpetual** (until cap reached): Continues indefinitely
- **Fixed Term** (24-48 months): Obligation ends at date, regardless of cap

**Your Negotiation Strategy**:

```
Perpetual is BETTER for you:
├─ If revenue growth slows, you're only paying until cap
├─ If revenue declines sharply, you're not locked in
└─ Aligns incentives (investor benefits from YOUR success)

Fixed 48-month term is RISKY:
├─ If revenue plateaus, you pay for full 4 years
├─ Even if cap not reached, you stop getting capital benefit
└─ Ask for: "Perpetual or maximum 36-month term"
```

### Sample Negotiation Scenarios

#### Scenario 1: You Have Multiple Offers

```
OFFER A (Clearco):
├─ Investment: $300,000
├─ Revenue Share: 5%
├─ Cap: 2.0x ($600,000)
└─ Term: Perpetual

OFFER B (Local Angel):
├─ Investment: $300,000
├─ Revenue Share: 4%
├─ Cap: 2.5x ($750,000)
└─ Term: 36 months

YOUR COUNTER TO CLEARCO:
"Thanks for the offer. I have interest from another investor at 4% revenue share
with a 2.0x cap. Can you match the 4% rate?"

CLEARCO's response options:
├─ "Yes, we'll do 4%" (you win!)
├─ "We'll do 4.5%" (compromise)
└─ "We can't budge on 5%" (walk away to other investor)
```

#### Scenario 2: Revenue Share is Too High

```
Investor insists: 6% revenue share
You project: $200,000/month revenue by month 12

Impact on you:
├─ Month 12 payment: $12,000/month (6% of $200K)
├─ Year 2 payments (assuming growth): $15,000/month
└─ This is too much - reduces growth capital available

COUNTER OFFER 1: Variable Rate
├─ Months 1-12: 5%
├─ Months 13-24: 4%
├─ Months 25+: 3% or cap reached
├─ Rationale: "Investor gets paid faster early, then it eases"

COUNTER OFFER 2: Revenue Milestones
├─ Under $100K/month: 6%
├─ $100K-$200K/month: 4%
├─ Over $200K/month: 2%
├─ Rationale: "This incentivizes our growth and success"

COUNTER OFFER 3: Increase Cap Instead
├─ 5% revenue share (instead of 6%)
├─ 2.5x cap instead of 2.0x
├─ Rationale: "Investor gets more total return, I pay lower monthly %"
```

#### Scenario 3: You Have Declining Revenue

```
Your Current Situation:
├─ Last 3 months: $150K → $120K → $100K (declining)
├─ Investor is concerned about payback
└─ Offers: 6% revenue share, 2.5x cap

REALISTIC NEGOTIATION:
You accept: 5% revenue share, 2.5x cap
Rationale to investor: "Here's my turnaround plan...
├─ (1) New product launching next month [show demo]
├─ (2) Sales pipeline has [X] prospects]
├─ (3) I cut costs by $20K/month

This capital will help me execute turnaround. 5% is fair given current metrics."

What investors respect:
├─ Honesty about decline
├─ Clear plan to fix it
├─ Realistic execution steps
└─ Acceptance of higher rates (acknowledging risk)
```

### Negotiation Tactics That Work

#### Tactic 1: Have Alternatives

```
Best Position:
"I have two offers. I prefer working with you, but the other investor
offered 4% revenue share. Can you match?"

This is powerful because:
├─ Shows you're desirable (others want to invest in you)
├─ Gives you real leverage
├─ Investor knows you'll leave if not matched
└─ Often results in them improving terms

If you don't have real alternatives:
└─ Don't mention fake ones (investors will call your bluff)
```

#### Tactic 2: Find Creative Solutions

```
Instead of arguing, solve together:

INVESTOR: "I need 5% revenue share to hit my return targets"
YOU: "What if we structure it differently?"

OPTIONS:
├─ Lower rate if you hit milestones (3% if revenue hits $200K/month)
├─ Higher cap to offset lower rate (2.5x instead of 2.0x)
├─ Add warrant coverage (investor gets 0.5% equity option)
├─ Acceleration clause (higher rate if cap not hit by month 30)
└─ Declining rate (5% → 3% over time)

This works because:
├─ You're not just "pushing back"
├─ You're showing creativity
├─ Investor sees you as a problem-solver
└─ Often leads to better final terms
```

#### Tactic 3: Show Financial Impact

```
DON'T SAY: "5% is too high"
SAY: "Let me show you the impact on our business..."

Visual your cash flow:
├─ "At 5%, I'm paying $15,000/month by month 12"
├─ "That's money I can't use to hire engineers or market"
├─ "At 4%, I can hire one more engineer, accelerating product"
├─ "Faster product = higher revenue = faster cap payback for you"

Investor now sees:
├─ They're not just debating numbers
├─ Your financial situation is real
├─ Lower rate = faster return to investor (through faster growth)
└─ Often persuades investor to budge
```

#### Tactic 4: Use Market Data

```
"I've talked to [X] founders who raised RBF. Here's what they got:
├─ [Founder A]: 3% revenue share with 2.0x cap
├─ [Founder B]: 4% revenue share with 2.5x cap
├─ [Founder C]: 4% revenue share with 2.0x cap

Can we align with market rates?"

This works because:
├─ Shows you've done homework
├─ Establishes what's "normal" in market
├─ Investor knows these terms are achievable
└─ Harder to defend unreasonable asks
```

### When to Walk Away

**You should WALK if:**

```
1. Revenue share > 7% + cap > 2.5x
   ├─ This means paying more than 5.5% average
   └─ Not viable long-term

2. Fixed term of 60+ months
   ├─ Too long if revenue declines
   └─ Too much control to investor

3. Investor is pushy/aggressive
   ├─ "Take this deal NOW or offer goes away"
   └─ Red flag for future relationship

4. Investor wants control they shouldn't have
   ├─ "We need a board seat"
   ├─ "You can't hire without our approval"
   └─ That's equity territory, not RBF

5. Terms worse than your next best alternative
   ├─ Use SAFE, equity, or venture debt instead
   └─ Don't take bad RBF just because it's "available"
```

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Mistake 1: Taking RBF Before Product-Market Fit

**The Problem:**
```
You: "I'm taking RBF to find product-market fit"
Investor: "I'm betting on your proven revenue being sustainable"

These don't match.
```

**Why This Fails:**
- RBF investors want **predictable revenue**
- Pre-PMF = unpredictable, volatile
- If you pivot, revenue might drop 80%
- Investor gets angry, relationship breaks

**The Right Approach:**
```
Timeline:
Month 0: Achieve product-market fit
├─ Real customers paying
├─ Retention metrics look good
└─ Growth is consistent

Month 1-6: Prove revenue stability
├─ 6 months of consistent/growing revenue
├─ Show unit economics work
└─ Demonstrate you can forecast

Month 7: Raise RBF
└─ Now you have proven track record
```

### Mistake 2: Using RBF When You Need Runway

**The Problem:**
```
Your Situation:
├─ Burning $50K/month
├─ 6 months of runway left
├─ Revenue is only $30K/month (not growing)

You think: "RBF will extend runway"
Reality: "RBF will accelerate runway depletion"

Why:
├─ You're not paying revenue share (no revenue to share!)
├─ RBF investors want predictable POSITIVE revenue
└─ They won't fund you if you're burning cash
```

**What to Do Instead:**
- If burning cash: Use **equity** (investors more patient with burn)
- If zero revenue: Use **grants** or **revenue-based debt** (if available)
- If declining revenue: **Fix the business first**, then raise RBF

**RBF Only Works If:**
- [ ] Revenue > Operating costs (positive unit economics)
- [ ] Revenue is growing (not declining)
- [ ] You can cover RBF payments from revenue

### Mistake 3: Overstating Revenue Growth

**The Problem:**
```
You project: 50% monthly growth
Investor plans: 24-month payback based on 50% growth
Reality: You grow 8% monthly

Result:
├─ Investor expected to be paid off in 24 months
├─ Now won't be paid off until month 60+
├─ Investor is angry, files complaints, restricts your business
└─ Relationship destroyed
```

**How to Avoid:**
1. **Be conservative**: Show 10% monthly growth (even if you expect 20%)
2. **Provide scenarios**: Conservative (10%), Base (15%), Optimistic (25%)
3. **Show your work**: "Here's why I think 15% is realistic..."
4. **Update regularly**: If growth slower than forecast, tell investor ASAP

**Example Honest Forecast:**
```
Conservative Case (40% prob): $1.2M revenue Year 1
Base Case (50% prob): $1.8M revenue Year 1
Optimistic Case (10% prob): $2.5M revenue Year 1

"My best guess is Base Case, but I wanted you to see downside too."
```

### Mistake 4: Not Budgeting for RBF Payments

**The Problem:**
```
You raise $300K in RBF at 4% revenue share

Month 6: Revenue = $200K
RBF Payment Due: $8,000

You realize: "I didn't plan for this $8K/month payment"
┣ Can't afford it from operations
└─ Breaches contract, damages relationship
```

**Financial Planning 101:**

```
Monthly Budget (Example):
├─ Revenue:              $200,000
├─ Costs of Goods Sold:  -$80,000 (COGS)
├─ Operating Expenses:   -$100,000 (payroll, rent, etc.)
├─ RBF Payment:          -$8,000 (4% of revenue)
└─ Remaining Cash:       $12,000 (for growth, reinvestment)

This needs to work BEFORE signing.
```

**How to Check:**
- [ ] Do I have cash on hand to cover first 3 months of RBF payments?
- [ ] Will RBF payment + operating costs < revenue?
- [ ] Can I grow business while making payments?
- [ ] What happens if revenue drops 20%?

### Mistake 5: Poor Financial Record-Keeping

**The Problem:**
```
Investor: "Can you show me your revenue from March?"
You: "Um... I think it was around $180K? Let me check my emails..."

Investor immediately:
├─ Loses confidence in you
├─ Assumes you're hiding something
└─ May walk away from deal
```

**Financial Systems You Need:**

```
MINIMUM (not acceptable, but common for startups):
├─ Accounting software (QuickBooks, FreshBooks, Stripe)
├─ Monthly P&L
└─ Monthly revenue reconciliation

GOOD (what investors expect):
├─ All of above PLUS
├─ Detailed revenue by category/customer
├─ Monthly cash flow projection
└─ Quarterly board presentation

EXCELLENT (what you should aim for):
├─ All of above PLUS
├─ Customer cohort analysis
├─ Unit economics tracking
├─ Annual audited financials
└─ Predictive revenue model
```

**What to Do Right Now:**
1. Set up QuickBooks or FreshBooks (if not already)
2. Create monthly P&L template
3. Run your first official monthly close
4. Know your numbers cold (can't rely on "I think...")
5. Reconcile accounts monthly (revenue = deposits)

### Mistake 6: Not Reading the Agreement

**The Problem:**
```
You sign RBF agreement without reviewing:
├─ Revenue Share escalates to 6% after year 1 (you didn't know!)
├─ You can't raise another RBF without consent (restriction!)
├─ If revenue drops, you still owe minimum $5K/month (trap!)
└─ You're stuck in a bad deal for 3 years
```

**What You MUST Read:**

```
Before signing, review these sections carefully:

1. Revenue Definition (Section 2.2)
   ├─ Does it match how you calculate revenue?
   └─ Are refunds/returns handled correctly?

2. Payment Terms (Section 2.5)
   ├─ How much? How often? By what date?
   └─ Is there a minimum even if revenue is $0?

3. Covenants (Section 5)
   ├─ What are you restricted from doing?
   ├─ Can you raise other RBF?
   └─ Can you incur other debt?

4. Termination (Section 4)
   ├─ When does this agreement end?
   ├─ What happens if you're acquired?
   └─ Can you pay it off early?

5. Reporting (Section 3)
   ├─ How often must you report?
   └─ What documents must you provide?
```

**Process:**
1. Investor sends draft agreement
2. **YOU READ IT CAREFULLY** (don't skip this!)
3. Mark up anything you don't understand
4. Have a lawyer review (budget $2K-5K for RBF agreement review)
5. Negotiate any problematic terms
6. THEN sign

## Paying Off RBF Early

### Why Pay Off Early?

**Reasons to Pay Off RBF:**

```
1. Reached Your Cap Early
   ├─ If you hit $500K repayment cap in month 20
   └─ You can optionally stop paying (per agreement)

2. Business Accelerated Beyond Expectations
   ├─ Your revenue is growing 30% monthly (way faster than forecast)
   ├─ You'll hit cap anyway, might as well be done
   └─ Frees up cash flow for growth

3. Next Funding Round Coming
   ├─ Getting Series A equity funding next month
   ├─ Use part of proceeds to pay off RBF
   └─ Cleans up cap table for VC investors

4. Want to Reduce Overhead
   ├─ Monthly reporting is getting burdensome
   ├─ You have strong cash position
   └─ Want to simplify financial obligations

5. Relationship Not Working
   ├─ Investor is problematic (demanding, uncooperative)
   ├─ You have cash to pay them off
   └─ Better to do it than deal with friction
```

### How to Calculate Early Payoff Amount

**From Your Agreement:**

```
Remaining Cap = Cap Amount - Cumulative Payments to Date

Example:
├─ Repayment Cap: $500,000
├─ Cumulative Payments to Date: $380,000
└─ Early Payoff Amount: $120,000 (pay this to be done)

Alternative (if defined in your agreement):
├─ Remaining Balance Calculation Per Agreement
└─ May be different formula (verify your agreement)
```

### Process for Early Payoff

```
Step 1: Review Your Agreement
├─ Section on Early Repayment
├─ Confirm amount you need to pay
└─ Check if there are any penalties

Step 2: Calculate Amount
├─ Get most recent statement from investor
├─ Confirm cumulative payments to date
├─ Calculate remaining: Cap - Cumulative
└─ Add any accrued interest/fees (if applicable)

Step 3: Get Written Confirmation
├─ Email investor: "We'd like to pay off RBF early"
├─ Investor confirms payoff amount
├─ Investor confirms wire instructions
└─ Get written confirmation in email

Step 4: Execute Wire
├─ Wire remaining amount to investor
├─ Confirm receipt
├─ Keep documentation

Step 5: Formal Termination
├─ Investor provides "Certificate of Satisfaction"
├─ Or both parties sign termination letter
├─ Agreement officially ends
└─ You're done!

Timeline: 5-10 business days typical
```

### Example Early Payoff Scenario

```
SITUATION:
├─ Raised $250K on 4% revenue share, 2.0x cap ($500K)
├─ Raised capital 18 months ago
├─ Total payments to date: $420,000
├─ Company just closed Series A round for $2M
└─ Want to use part of proceeds to pay off RBF

CALCULATION:
├─ Repayment Cap: $500,000
├─ Cumulative Payments: $420,000
├─ Early Payoff Amount: $80,000
└─ You pay final $80K, RBF ends

BENEFIT:
├─ No more monthly revenue share payments
├─ Cleaner cap table for Series A investors
├─ Removed reporting burden
├─ $250K+ cash/month freed up (4% of $500K+ revenue)
└─ Total cost: $500K total (2x initial investment) - market standard
```

```
Wait, why so long?
├─ Revenue not growing fast (only 12% MoM)
├─ At current growth rate: ~24-month doubling time
├─ To go from $150K → $300K takes 2 years
├─ Even if doubles twice, still slow path to $600K

├─ If revenue grows to $200K (1 year): Cap reached in ~41 months
├─ If revenue grows to $300K (2 years): Cap reached in ~27 months
├─ Still longer than venture debt (which is 3 years anyway)
```

```

├─ Company is profitable (not trying to grow unprofitably)
├─ Want to scale team and maintain margins
├─ Revenue growth is stable but moderate (12% MoM)
├─ Venture debt gives predictable obligation ($15K/month)
├─ RBF would pay more than venture debt with slow revenue growth
├─ Better to have fixed payment and plan around it

Math Comparison (36 months):
├─ Venture Debt: $540K total (interest + principal)
├─ RBF (slow growth scenario): $720K total (if paying until month 40)
└─ Venture Debt WINS by $180K

Venture Debt Winner Because:
├─ Fixed payment ($15K vs $6K but increasing as revenue grows)
├─ Shorter payback (36 months vs 40+)
├─ Lower risk of renegotiation
├─ Better cash flow predictability
```

```
✓ Later raised equity when ready (on founder's terms)

aligned better with our growth trajectory and cash flow."
```

- [ ] I'm prepared for due diligence (financial documents organized)
- [ ] I've reviewed sample RBF agreements (know what's standard)
- [ ] I have lawyer ready to review final agreement

### After You Close RBF

- [ ] Monthly reporting system is set up
- [ ] Payment mechanism is automated (or calendar reminder)
- [ ] Investor contact info and payment instructions documented
- [ ] First monthly report is ready to send
- [ ] Financial forecast updated (based on actual vs projected)
- [ ] Team is trained on reporting requirements
- [ ] I understand when cap will be reached (tracking cumulative payments)
- [ ] I have plan for what comes next (Series A? More RBF? Stay independent?)

## Final Thoughts for Founders

**RBF is a powerful tool, but only for the right businesses.**

### When RBF is Perfect:
- You have proven, growing revenue
- You want to maintain control
- You need capital within 60 days
- You don't want equity dilution
- You're comfortable with transparency

### When RBF is Wrong:
- You don't have revenue yet
- Your revenue is declining
- You're burning cash faster than you can raise it
- You need strategic mentorship (equity investor better)
- You have inconsistent/unpredictable revenue

**Key Takeaway:**

RBF is not "free money" and it's not debt. It's a partnership where the investor shares in your success. If you grow fast, RBF is incredible (you only pay while you're succeeding). If you grow slowly or decline, RBF becomes a burden.

Think of it as:
```
✓ Growth Tool (use it to scale, not survive)
✓ Speed Tool (fast capital when needed)
✓ Independence Tool (avoid equity dilution)
✗ Survival Tool (don't use for unprofitable runway)
✗ Strategic Tool (don't expect mentorship)
```

**Make the right choice for YOUR business.**
