# Investor Perspective on Priced Equity Rounds

## Overview: What Investors Need to Know

Priced equity rounds are where venture capital funds deploy capital in hopes of 10-100x returns. As an investor evaluating a priced round opportunity, you're balancing risk, return potential, control, and portfolio management.

This guide covers what investors should focus on, watch out for, and negotiate during priced equity rounds.

---

## The Investor's Challenge: The Math Doesn't Always Work

### The Return Math Reality

**To achieve target returns, you need**:

- **Seed-stage investor**: 50x+ return targets (invest $500K, expect $25M exit)
- **Series A investor**: 20-30x return targets (invest $10M, expect $200M+ exit)
- **Series B investor**: 10-15x return targets (invest $50M, expect $500M+ exit)
- **Series C+ investor**: 5-10x return targets (invest $100M+, expect unicorn exit)

**Reality check**: Most companies don't hit these targets. 90% of portfolio companies deliver <5x returns.

### Why Valuation Risk Matters Most

**If you overpay at entry, you're underwater immediately**:

- **Seed at $10M pre** that should have been $5M
- Series A investors won't pay $20M pre (would show lack of progress)
- You're diluted in Series A at lower valuation
- Your position shrinks in relative value
- Return targets become mathematically impossible to reach

**This is the biggest investor risk**: Entry valuation determines the ceiling for potential returns.

## Key Terms That Matter Most to Investors

### 1. Valuation & Entry Price (Your Biggest Risk)

**Why it matters**: Entry price determines your ownership % and potential return multiple.

**Simple math**:
- Invest $5M at $20M pre-money = $25M post-money
- Your ownership = $5M / $25M = 20%
- If company exits at $200M, you get $40M (20% × $200M) = 8x return
- If company exits at $100M, you get $20M (20% × $100M) = 4x return (miss target)

**If you overpaid and pre-money was really $10M**:
- You should own 33.3% but only own 20%
- At $200M exit, you'd have $66.6M (if entry was right)
- You got $40M instead (lost $26.6M)

**How to validate valuation**:
- Post-revenue multiple: 2-5x annual revenue for Seed, 5-10x for Series A
- Growth multiple: Company growing at 100% = should trade at 100x revenue (rough rule of 40 adjusted)
- Comparable companies: similar metrics at similar stage
- Usage-based metrics: 10-30x annual contract value for SaaS
- Pre-revenue metrics: Users, engagement, growth rate
- Benchmarks: Use PitchBook, Carta, or CB Insights data

**Reality**: You'll pay premium for best teams/markets, discount for risky bets.

**Risks to watch**:
- Founder claims valuation based on "investor interest" (not binding)
- Valuation inflated by prior SAFE/convertible note that was too generous
- Market conditions changing rapidly (your previous comps no longer valid)

### 2. Type of Liquidation Preference (Participating vs. Non-Participating)

**Non-Participating Preferred** (Founder-friendly):
- You get your $5M back **OR** your pro-rata share of proceeds
- Whichever is greater
- In massive exits, you convert and get pro-rata (good for you)
- In modest exits, you take preference and get less (bad for you)

**Participating Preferred** (Investor-friendly):
- You get your $5M back **PLUS** your pro-rata of remaining proceeds
- "Double-dipping" - get back investment AND extra return
- In all exit scenarios, you get your preference plus additional share

**Example Impact at $50M exit with $5M Series A investment, 20% ownership**:

**Non-Participating**:
- Exit proceeds = $50M
- Your preference = $5M
- Since $50M > $5M, you convert and get 20% of $50M = $10M
- Return = 2x

**Participating**:
- You get $5M back (preference satisfied)
- You get additional 20% of remaining $45M = $9M
- You get $5M + $9M = $14M total
- Return = 2.8x

**Impact**: You gain $4M in participating scenario.

**What to negotiate**:
- Non-participating for Seed (founders will push back hard)
- Participating is increasingly standard for Series A+
- Participating with caps (e.g., max 1.5x total return) as compromise
- Be willing to trade participating preference for lower valuation

### 3. Anti-Dilution Protection (Minimizing Down-Round Risk)

**Why it matters**: In down rounds, anti-dilution protects your ownership by adjusting conversion price downward.

**Example down round scenario**:
- You invest at Series A at $10/share
- Series B happens at $8/share (down round - 20% decrease)
- Anti-dilution trigger: Your conversion price adjusts downward
- Your ownership % adjusts upward (to compensate for down round)

**Broad-Based Weighted Average** (Moderate investor protection):
- Formula: New Price = Old Price × √(Old Price × New Price / Current Stock Price)
- Less aggressive repricing
- Shares down-round pain across all shareholders
- Market standard (most investors accept this)

**Narrow-Based Weighted Average** (Stronger investor protection):
- Formula uses only preferred shares (larger denominator)
- More aggressive repricing
- Better protection for investors in down rounds
- Harder to negotiate (founders resist)

**Full Ratchet** (Maximum investor protection - rare):
- Your conversion price fully adjusts to new down round price
- Maximum investor protection
- Devastating for founders
- Rarely used (too harsh, kills companies)

**What to negotiate**:
- Broad-based weighted average (market standard)
- Narrow-based if you have strong negotiating power
- Anti-dilution exemptions: exclude employee options from calculation
- Carve-outs: Doesn't trigger on strategic issuances or employee grants

**Critical insight**: Down-round anti-dilution looks good on paper but often backfires:
- Massive founder dilution triggers attrition
- Founder leaves (key person risk)
- Company value collapses
- Your anti-dilution protection is worthless in failed company

### 4. Board Representation (Your Governance Rights)

**Seed (3-person board)**:
- 1 founder (CEO)
- 1 investor (you, if this is lead investor)
- 1 independent director
- **You have blocking minority power** on most decisions

**Series A (5-person board)**:
- 1 founder (CEO)
- 1 Series A investor (you, if lead)
- 1 Series Seed investor (if still holding significant %)
- 1-2 independent directors
- **You have board control** with Series A + 1 independent

**Series B (5-7 person board)**:
- 1 founder (CEO)
- 1 Series A investor
- 1 Series B investor (you, if lead)
- 1-2 independent directors
- 1 Series Seed observer (declining power)
- **You control decisions** but must manage founder relationship

**Series C+ (7+ person board)**:
- Founder becomes minority voice
- Multiple investor directors
- Multiple independent directors
- Investor consortium makes decisions
- **You're part of investor majority**

**What to negotiate**:
- Board observer rights for non-lead investments
- Information rights (monthly reporting, board materials)
- Right to bring in independent board member of your choice
- Right to designate co-investor board seats (if co-investing)

**Critical insight**: Board seats are double-edged sword:
- Gives you operational oversight (good)
- Takes time (bad for large portfolios)
- Makes you liable (you're a director, have fiduciary duties)
- Creates founder tension if you vote against them

### 5. Protective Provisions (Your Veto Rights)

**Why they matter**: Protective provisions give you veto power over founder decisions that threaten your investment.

**Common protective provisions (what you'd want)**:
- Hiring/firing CEO (founder exit risk)
- Sale of company (exit control)
- Merger or consolidation (changes deal structure)
- Liquidation or winding down (protect capital)
- Changing business (pivot risk)
- Additional financing (dilution risk)
- Related-party transactions (self-dealing risk)
- Issuance of senior securities (preference stacking)
- Changes to board composition (governance control)
- Changes to stock preferences (dilution of your rights)

**Typical investor package**: 8-12 protective provisions standard

**What to negotiate**:
- Protective provisions drop at key milestones (revenue, ARR)
- Some provisions require supermajority instead of single investor veto
- Exceptions for employee options, strategic grants
- Defined thresholds for materiality (e.g., "material" = >$500K in related party)

**Critical insight**: Protective provisions create friction:
- Founder sees them as loss of control
- You see them as downside protection
- Every founder decision requires investor approval
- Company moves slowly under heavy provisions
- Causes founder attrition if too restrictive

## Risk Scenarios Investors Face

### 1. Valuation Decline Risk

**Scenario**:
- You invest $10M at $50M pre-money (Series A)
- You own 16.7% of company
- Series B fundraising happens 18 months later
- Market conditions deteriorated
- Series B offered at $40M pre-money (down round)

**Impact on your position**:
- Your shares valued at $8.3M in down round (down from $10M invested)
- You're underwater immediately
- Expected return requires company to reach higher exit value
- Down round signals company didn't execute on milestones

**What you should have done**:
- Better valuation diligence at entry
- Conservative valuation given market risk
- Milestone-based follow-on investment structure

### 2. Dilution in Follow-On Rounds

**Scenario**:
- You invest $5M at Series A, own 20%
- Company raises Series B with new lead investor
- Series B investor owns 25%
- Company raises Series C with new lead investor
- Series C investor owns 30%
- Your ownership declines from 20% to 10.5% to 7.4%

**Impact**:
- Each round dilutes your position
- If you can't participate pro-rata, you're underwater in relative terms
- Expected return depends on participating in upside rounds
- If you run out of follow-on capital, you get diluted out

**What you should have done**:
- Reserve capital for pro-rata follow-on rounds (20-50% of fund capacity)
- Negotiate participation rights in your SPA
- Build relationship with founder for Series B inclusion

### 3. Preference Stacking Paradox

**Scenario - Three rounds with 1x participating preferences**:
- Seed: $2M at $8M pre (Seed owns 20%)
- Series A: $10M at $20M pre (Series A owns 33%)
- Series B: $20M at $60M pre (Series B owns 25%)
- Exit at $70M (good outcome, but not massive)

**Waterfall with 1x participating**:
- Seed: $2M preference
- Series A: $10M preference
- Series B: $20M preference
- Total preferences: $32M
- Remaining after preferences: $38M

**Distribution**:
- Seed investors: $2M (1x return)
- Series A investors: $10M + (33% of $38M) = $10M + $12.5M = $22.5M (2.25x return)
- Series B investors: $20M + (25% of $16.5M remaining) = $20M + $4.1M = $24.1M (1.2x return)
- Common shareholders (founders): remainder

**Issue**:
- Seed investors disappointed (1x return)
- Series A investors okay (2.25x)
- Series B investors disappointed (1.2x)
- Founder gets minimal return despite owning majority
- Overall returns suffer from preference stacking

**What you should have done**:
- Negotiate non-participating preference in earlier rounds
- Don't invest in company with stacked preferences
- Consider this scenario when evaluating opportunity

### 4. Founder Attrition Risk

**Scenario**:
- Founder losing control via board/protective provisions
- Founder frustrated with investor veto on decisions
- Founder 1-year cliff vests out (no acceleration post-investment)
- Founder considers leaving or getting fired
- Company loses key person with deep product knowledge

**Impact on your investment**:
- Company culture disrupted
- Product development stalls
- Investor confidence drops
- Series B doesn't happen
- Company acquires at basement price
- Your return = loss

**What you should have done**:
- Balance governance rights with founder motivation
- Don't strangle founder autonomy
- Ensure founder has equity acceleration
- Build relationship, not adversarial position

### 5. Down Round Triggering Anti-Dilution

**Scenario**:
- Series A investors own 30%
- Series B at lower price triggers anti-dilution repricing
- Series A conversion price adjusts downward
- Series A ownership increases from 30% to 35%
- Founder dilution increases from 40% to 35%
- Founder is now severely diluted

**Investor perspective**:
- Your anti-dilution protection helps (30% becomes 35%)
- But it's Pyrrhic victory:
  - Founder attrition likely (now owns less)
  - Company momentum lost
  - Series C funding becomes harder
  - Your 35% of failing company worth less than 30% of healthy company

**What you should have done**:
- Broad-based anti-dilution (less aggressive repricing)
- Avoid full ratchet or narrow-based (too harsh)
- Remember anti-dilution is downside protection, not upside creator

## What Investors Look For in Term Negotiations

### 1. Founder Receptiveness

**Red flags**:
- Founder overly adversarial about investor protections
- Founder resistant to basic governance provisions
- Founder wants to sign only 1 pager with no legal docs (unrealistic)
- Founder dismissive of investor concerns about dilution/control

**Green flags**:
- Founder understands investor perspective
- Founder willing to negotiate in good faith
- Founder wants terms that work for both parties
- Founder gets lawyer but focuses on relationship

**Why it matters**: Founders who fight every term are going to create problems during scaling.

### 2. Realistic Financial Projections

**Red flags**:
- Founder projecting 500% YoY growth (unsustainable)
- Founder with no financial modeling experience
- Founder unaware of metrics that matter (CAC, LTV, burn rate)
- Founder unable to explain path to profitability

**Green flags**:
- Founder has detailed financial model (12-24 months out)
- Founder understands unit economics
- Founder knows how capital will extend runway
- Founder has conservative revenue projections

**Why it matters**: Bad projections mean founder doesn't understand business, won't manage capital well.

### 3. Market Size & TAM

**Red flags**:
- Founder claims TAM is "everyone"
- Founder unable to articulate total addressable market
- Founder doesn't know market size (just knows it's big)
- Founder pursuing niche in shrinking market

**Green flags**:
- Founder can clearly define target customer
- Founder knows market size (top-down and bottom-up TAM)
- Founder explains how to expand TAM
- Founder articulates why market is attractive

**Why it matters**: Unsizable markets cap potential returns.

### 4. Competitive Moat

**Red flags**:
- Founder's only moat is speed to market
- Founder hasn't thought about competition
- Founder thinks they're in "blue ocean" (usually not true)
- Founder has no defensible advantage

**Green flags**:
- Founder articulates clear competitive advantage (network effects, data, switching costs)
- Founder has product-market fit and growing usage
- Founder understands competitive landscape
- Founder explains why they'll win despite competition

**Why it matters**: Without moat, company becomes commodity, hard to exit.

## Term Sheet Negotiation Strategy for Investors

### Your Negotiation Priorities (In Order)

1. **Valuation** (entry price determines return ceiling) - Most important
2. **Liquidation preference** (participating vs non-participating)
3. **Anti-dilution mechanism** (broad vs narrow)
4. **Board seat** (governance control)
5. **Protective provisions** (veto rights)

### Valuation Negotiation Tactics

**For lead investors** (you're setting valuation):
- Come with comps from similar companies
- Use benchmarks from industry reports
- Be ready with data (Carta, PitchBook, etc.)
- Offer valuation you're comfortable with, not what market might support
- Be willing to walk if founder overvalued

**For follow-on investors** (valuation already set):
- Accept valuation, negotiate other terms
- Negotiate pro-rata participation rights instead
- Get board observer rights
- Push for information rights

**Founder pushback you'll hear**:
- "Previous investors valued us at $20M pre"
- "Comparable company raised at $30M pre"
- "We're growing 200% YoY"

**Your response**:
- "Previous valuations don't matter, I need to feel good about this entry"
- "Show me those comps, I'll validate or explain why they don't apply"
- "Growth rates are great, but I need to model exit scenarios"

### Liquidation Preference Negotiation

**Your goal**: Participating preferred, but open to negotiation

**Negotiation ladder**:
1. **Ask**: 1x participating preferred
2. **Middle ground**: 1x participating with cap (e.g., max 2x total return)
3. **Fallback**: 1x non-participating (common for Seed)
4. **Last resort**: 0.5x participating preferred

**Example compromise**:
- 1x non-participating for Seed
- 1x non-participating with $X cap on participation for Series A
- 1x participating for Series B+

### Anti-Dilution Negotiation

**Your goal**: At least broad-based weighted average

**Negotiation ladder**:
1. **Ask**: Narrow-based weighted average
2. **Middle ground**: Broad-based weighted average
3. **Fallback**: Broad-based with carve-outs for employee options
4. **Last resort**: Broad-based with higher down-round threshold (e.g., >30% down)

### Board Seat Negotiation

**If you're lead investor**:
- Demand board seat (non-negotiable for significant check size)
- Negotiate for founder to be CEO (you want operational oversight)
- Get cast vote in tie situations

**If you're follow-on investor**:
- Negotiate board observer rights
- Request board seat if hitting minimum ownership threshold
- Co-investor rights to bring in joint board seat

### Protective Provisions Negotiation

**Your goal**: Comprehensive protective provisions

**Negotiation ladder**:
1. **Ask**: Full set of 12+ protective provisions
2. **Middle ground**: 8-10 most critical provisions
3. **Fallback**: 6-8 provisions focused on exit and financing

**Most critical provisions to keep**:
- Sale of company
- Additional financing (debt/equity)
- Liquidation
- Change in business

## Investor Red Flags & Deal Breakers

### Red Flag Scenarios

1. **Founder is inexperienced** - No prior startup experience, first-time founder with no mentor
2. **Founder is unrealistic** - Projects 10x growth every year, dismisses competition
3. **Team missing functional expertise** - All engineers, no sales or business person
4. **Market size unclear** - Can't articulate who their customer is
5. **Traction is declining** - Growth was strong, but last 3 months flattened
6. **Founder-investor misalignment** - Founder wants different things than investor

### Deal Breaker Terms

1. **Founder keeps 100% voting control** (with your preferred shares)
2. **Founder gets to hire/fire/pay themselves unlimited**
3. **No liquidation preferences** (preferred stock with no rights)
4. **Founder gets to sell shares** (can cash out while you're locked in)
5. **No veto on future financings** (founder can raise at ridiculous valuation)

### Red Flags in Founder Negotiation Style

1. **Founder is adversarial** - Views every term as a battle
2. **Founder won't show financials** - Refuses to share cap table or projections
3. **Founder keeps changing story** - Different narrative each meeting
4. **Founder pressures for quick decision** - "We have other investors interested" (bluff)

## Follow-On Investment Strategy

### Pro-Rata Participation Rights

**What you should negotiate**:
- Right to invest pro-rata in follow-on rounds
- Protects against dilution if you can afford to participate
- Gives you priority over new investors

**Example**:
- You own 20% after Series A
- Series B happens, you have right to invest 20% of Series B round
- If you exercise, you maintain 20% ownership
- If you don't, you get diluted to 16.7% (rough estimate)

### Capital Reserves Strategy

**Professional VC funds reserve capital**:
- Typical VC fund reserves 25-50% of capital for follow-on investments
- Allows them to support winners without dilution
- Gives them option to pass on bad follow-on opportunities

**As angel or early-stage investor**:
- You might not have capital to follow on
- Negotiate pro-rata rights even if you can't fund them
- Right to sell your shares to incoming investor in Series B

### When to Deploy Follow-On Capital

**Only follow on if**:
1. **Company is tracking to milestones** - Revenue/growth on plan
2. **You have conviction** - Still believe in founder and market
3. **You have capital available** - Won't hurt your portfolio
4. **Terms are reasonable** - Not paying silly valuation

**Never follow on if**:
1. **Company is underperforming** - Missed targets for 2 consecutive quarters
2. **Founder has left or is struggling** - Key person risk
3. **You're out of capital** - Don't stretch your fund
4. **Terms are ridiculous** - Don't throw good money after bad

## When to Walk Away

### Deal Breakers for Investors

1. **Unsustainable unit economics** - CAC > LTV, negative margin structure
2. **Founder misalignment** - Founder's vision doesn't match business model
3. **Fake traction** - Growth is paid acquisition, not organic
4. **Regulatory risk** - Business model might become illegal
5. **Weak team** - Key hires missing or unrealistic hiring plan

### Walk-Away Scenarios

- Founder refuses basic governance (you need protection)
- Valuation is clearly too high (math doesn't work at any exit)
- Comps show this business should be half the valuation
- You lose conviction in market or founder
- Better opportunity in your pipeline

### Negotiation Walkaway

- You've been negotiating for 3+ months with no progress
- Founder keeps changing terms after handshake
- Founder is being hostile/adversarial
- You're compromising core terms just to do deal
- Deal smells wrong (trust your gut)

## Summary: What Investors Care About

**In priority order**:

1. **Return on Investment** - Does the math work? Can we get 10-20x?
2. **Risk Mitigation** - Can we protect downside with governance/preferences?
3. **Founder Quality** - Can they execute? Do they listen? Are they realistic?
4. **Market Size** - Is TAM large enough to justify valuation?
5. **Growth Trajectory** - Is traction accelerating? Are metrics improving?
6. **Competitive Position** - Why will they win vs. competitors?
7. **Team** - Do they have right people? Can they hire great talent?
8. **Governance** - Can we work together? Will they take our input?

**Your negotiating position depends on**:
- How many other investors are interested
- How much cash you're deploying
- Your reputation and value-add
- Founder's desperation to close

## Investor Checklist Before Signing Term Sheet

- [ ] Valuation feels fair based on comps and metrics
- [ ] Liquidation preference aligns with risk (participating vs. non-participating)
- [ ] Anti-dilution mechanism is broad-based weighted average at minimum
- [ ] You have board seat or observer rights
- [ ] Protective provisions protect against major downside scenarios
- [ ] You have pro-rata participation rights for future rounds
- [ ] Information rights give you transparency into financials
- [ ] You've validated traction (revenue, user growth, etc.)
- [ ] Founder is realistic about projections and market
- [ ] You have conviction in founder and team
- [ ] You've reviewed cap table (no weird securities outstanding)
- [ ] You've thought through exit scenarios and return modeling
