# Term Sheet Negotiation Guide for Priced Equity Rounds

## Overview

This guide covers practical negotiation strategies for founders and investors during priced equity round term sheets. The negotiation phase typically lasts 4-8 weeks from initial term sheet to final close.

---

## Pre-Negotiation Preparation

### For Both Parties

**Before negotiations start**:

1. **Define your priorities** - What matters most? (Valuation, control, terms?)
2. **Understand your BATNA** - Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement
3. **Research comparables** - What did similar companies get?
4. **Get legal counsel** - Startup lawyer is non-negotiable
5. **Understand the documents** - What is a SPA? IRA? Voting agreement?
6. **Create fallback positions** - What would you accept if ideal terms not achievable?

### For Founders

**Prepare for investor perspective**:
- Investor's return targets (10-30x depending on stage)
- Investor's need for downside protection
- Investor's governance requirements
- Investor's follow-on investment capacity
- Investor's track record with companies in your space

**Gather competitive data**:
- Recent seed rounds in your market (what valuations?)
- Series A rounds by similar companies
- Investor thesis documents (what they care about)
- NVCA model documents (what's standard?)

**Know your leverage**:
- Do you have competing investor interest?
- Are you in high-demand category (AI, fintech)?
- Do you have strong metrics/traction?
- Can you fund yourself longer (reduce urgency)?

### For Investors

**Prepare for founder perspective**:
- Founder's expectations vs. realistic valuation
- Founder's concerns about control/dilution
- Founder's prior financing history
- Founder's negotiation style (reasonable vs. difficult?)
- Founder's alternatives (other investors interested?)

**Gather competitive data**:
- Recent funding in this vertical
- Founder's negotiating style with prior investors
- Investor reputation (founder concerns about investor governance?)
- Market for this investment (is it hot?)

**Know your leverage**:
- Are you the lead investor or follow-on?
- Does founder have other term sheets?
- Is market hot or cold for this space?
- How many companies are you evaluating?

## The Negotiation Timeline

### Week 1-2: Initial Term Sheet

**Investor presents initial term sheet**:
- Covers major economic terms (valuation, investment amount, preferences)
- Often 2-3 pages for seed, 4-5 pages for later stage
- Usually marked "non-binding except for exclusivity"

**Founder's initial response**:
- Usually aggressive (wants higher valuation, fewer investor rights)
- Comes back with redlined version
- Investor reviews comments

**Typical first redlines by founder**:
- Higher valuation by 20-50%
- Change to non-participating preferred
- Fewer protective provisions
- No investor board seat (observer only)
- Shorter exclusivity period

### Week 2-3: Main Economic Terms

**Focus**: Valuation, liquidation preference, anti-dilution

**Typical progression**:
- Investor offers $8M pre-money
- Founder wants $12M pre-money
- They negotiate to $10M pre-money (compromise in middle)

**Liquidation preference dance**:
- Investor wants 1x participating
- Founder wants 1x non-participating
- They settle on 1x non-participating (common for seed)
- Or 1x participating with 2x cap (compromise)

**Anti-dilution negotiation**:
- Investor wants narrow-based weighted average
- Founder wants no anti-dilution
- They settle on broad-based weighted average (market standard)

**Board composition**:
- If seed: Investor gets 1 of 3 seats
- If Series A: Investor gets 1 of 5 seats (might negotiate 2)
- If Series B+: Multiple investor seats becoming standard

### Week 3-4: Protective Provisions & Rights

**Focus**: What can investor veto? What are information rights?

**Investor wants to block**:
- Sale of company (want to control exit)
- Additional financing (don't want surprise dilution)
- Liquidation (want to stay invested)
- CEO replacement (founder continuity)
- Material business changes (want oversight)

**Founder wants to minimize**:
- Founder wants ability to hire/fire without approval
- Founder wants flexibility to pivot if needed
- Founder wants ability to raise debt without approval
- Founder wants ability to hire/compensate team

**Typical compromise**:
- Investor gets veto on 8-10 most critical items
- Founder gets autonomy on operations
- Both agree on materiality thresholds ($500K+ for related party, etc.)

**Information rights negotiation**:
- Investor wants monthly financials + quarterly board meetings
- Founder says that's too burdensome (true, for small company)
- They settle on quarterly financials + quarterly board meetings
- Founder gets carve-out for first 12 months (get systems in place first)

### Week 4-5: SPA & Ancillary Docs

**Focus**: Stock Purchase Agreement, Investors' Rights Agreement, Voting Agreement

**Stock Purchase Agreement** (Main closing document):
- Representations & warranties by founder/company
- Conditions to closing
- Indemnification provisions
- Escrow for reps & warranties

**Investor's Rights Agreement**:
- Information rights (monthly/quarterly reporting)
- Registration rights (if IPO, investor gets piggyback)
- Participation rights (pro-rata in future rounds)
- Drag-along rights (investor can force sale)
- Tag-along rights (investor sells with founder)

**Voting Agreement**:
- How board votes structured
- Cumulative voting elimination
- Board election procedures
- Redemption rights (if any)

**Right of First Refusal & Co-Sale**:
- Company ROFR on founder stock sales
- Investor ROFR on founder stock sales
- Co-sale rights (investor sells with founder)
- Drag-along rights (investor forces minority to sell)

**Typical negotiation points**:
- **Representation periods**: How long are founder reps good for?
  - Investor wants 18-24 months
  - Founder wants 12 months
  - Settle on 12-18 months
- **Escrow for reps**: Investor wants holdback of 10-15% for 12 months
  - Founder hates this (wants all cash at close)
  - Typical compromise: 10% held for 12 months, released quarterly
- **Registration rights**: Founder wants unlimited, investor wants limited
  - Typical: 2-3 demand registration rights for investor
- **Pro-rata rights**: Investor wants full pro-rata, founder wants to limit
  - Standard: Pro-rata based on ownership %
- **Drag-along**: Investor wants full drag-along (force minority to sell)
  - Founder resists, wants to protect themselves
  - Typical: Drag-along allowed for sale that 75%+ shareholders approve

### Week 5-6: Final Redlines & Closing

**Focus**: Resolving remaining issues, preparing for close

**By week 5**:
- Major terms agreed
- Docs almost final
- Both sides negotiating over commas and semicolons

**Common late-stage negotiations**:
- **Cap table accuracy**: Make sure fully diluted shares calculated correctly
- **Founder equity vesting**: Ensure it matches expectations
- **Employee option pool**: What size? Reserved shares for future hires?
- **Bring-down conditions**: What represents closing conditions?
- **Closing conditions**: What must be true at close?

**Founder checklist before signing**:
- [ ] You understand all terms (not just valuation)
- [ ] Your lawyer has reviewed everything
- [ ] You've modeled dilution across future rounds
- [ ] Board composition makes sense to you
- [ ] You're comfortable with investor's protective provisions
- [ ] You have clear understanding of use of proceeds
- [ ] You have fund amount in writing (not contingent on other things)
- [ ] Exclusivity period is reasonable (60-90 days typical)

**Investor checklist before signing**:
- [ ] Valuation feels fair based on metrics
- [ ] You have sufficient governance rights
- [ ] Founder is realistic about business
- [ ] Cap table is clean (no weird securities)
- [ ] You've validated traction
- [ ] Legal docs are clean and standard
- [ ] You have board seat or observer rights
- [ ] You have information rights
- [ ] Follow-on investment strategy is clear

## Key Negotiation Tactics

### For Founders

#### Tactic 1: Valuation Anchoring

**How it works**:
- Anchor the negotiation high (ask for higher valuation)
- Investor anchors low
- Meet in middle, which is founder's target

**Example**:
- Founder's target: $10M pre-money
- Founder asks: $15M pre-money (anchor high)
- Investor offers: $7M pre-money (anchor low)
- They settle: $10M pre-money (founder wins)

**When it works**:
- You have multiple investor interest (valuation tension)
- You have strong metrics/traction
- You're in hot market (AI, fintech)

**When it backfires**:
- You anchor too high (sounds unrealistic)
- You have weak metrics (investor dismisses you)
- You're in cold market (investor confident walking away)

#### Tactic 2: Competitive Tension

**How it works**:
- Mention you're talking to other investors
- Creates urgency for investor to close
- Investor more willing to concede on terms

**Example**:
- "We're closing term sheet with [INVESTOR_B] next week"
- "We have [3_OTHER_INVESTORS] interested in this round"
- "We're hoping to announce funding by [DATE]"

**When it works**:
- You actually have other investor interest (must be true)
- Investor fears losing deal
- You're in hot category

**When it backfires**:
- You lie about other investors (investors talk, they find out)
- Other investors don't actually exist (founder loses credibility)
- Investor calls your bluff and walks
- Investor negotiates harder (tries to beat other terms)

**Pro tip**: Always be truthful about other interest. Investor will respect honesty more than lies.

#### Tactic 3: Bundling Issues

**How it works**:
- Don't negotiate one term at a time
- Bundle multiple issues together
- Trade terms: "If you give me lower valuation, I'll give you participating preference"

**Example**:
- Founder: "We want $12M pre-money AND non-participating preference"
- Investor: "Can't do both. I'll do $10M pre if you do 1x participating"
- Founder: "Okay, we'll do that"

**Benefits**:
- Creates flexibility (both sides can trade)
- Feels more collaborative
- Easier to find win-win solutions
- Reduces adversarial tone

#### Tactic 4: Walk Away When Needed

**How it works**:
- Be willing to pass on deal
- Gives you leverage (negotiates harder for terms)
- Investor knows you're not desperate

**When to threaten walking away**:
- Investor asking for unreasonable protective provisions
- Valuation is too low (math doesn't work)
- Investor is being difficult/unreasonable
- You have other investor interest

**When NOT to walk away**:
- You're desperate for capital (reduces leverage)
- No other investors interested
- You can't fund yourself longer
- This is your only option

**Pro tip**: Only threaten walk-away if you mean it. Bluffing destroys credibility.

#### Tactic 5: Valuation Trade-Offs

**How it works**:
- Accept lower valuation in exchange for better terms
- Example: Accept $9M pre instead of $12M, but get non-participating preference

**Scenario**:
- Investor won't budge on valuation ($8M pre)
- You want $12M pre (unrealistic)
- You can:
  - Keep pushing on valuation (endless negotiation)
  - Accept $8M pre, push for non-participating preference (founder-friendly)
  - Accept $8M pre, push for lower board seat veto rights
  - Accept $8M pre, push for investor to commit follow-on rounds

**When it makes sense**:
- You have acceptable valuation + better terms = better outcome
- Terms matter more than pure dilution
- Non-participating preference might save you more in bad exit

### For Investors

#### Tactic 1: Valuation Anchoring

**How it works**:
- Anchor the negotiation low (offer lower valuation)
- Founder anchors high
- Meet in middle, which is investor's target

**Example**:
- Investor's target: $10M pre-money
- Investor offers: $7M pre-money (anchor low)
- Founder wants: $15M pre-money (anchor high)
- They settle: $10M pre-money (investor wins)

**When it works**:
- Founder has weak metrics
- Founder is desperate for capital
- Market is cold (many companies need funding)

**When it backfires**:
- You anchor too low (sounds insulting)
- Founder has strong metrics (investor sees it)
- Founder walks to other investors
- You lose deal over 20% valuation difference

#### Tactic 2: Conditional Follow-On

**How it works**:
- Commit follow-on investment conditional on hitting milestones
- Founder gets capital now + committed follow-on later
- Investor limits risk (only follow if company performs)

**Example**:
- "We'll do $5M at $15M pre-money now"
- "Plus we'll do $10M Series A at $40M pre-money if you hit $1M ARR by Q4"
- Founder gets capital today + Series A reserved if execution good

**Benefits**:
- Founder gets higher valuation on Series A (if they perform)
- Investor limits downside (only double-down if metrics good)
- Aligns incentives (both want company to hit milestones)

#### Tactic 3: Governance Trading

**How it works**:
- Accept lower valuation, demand more governance
- Example: "Okay, $10M pre-money if we get 2 board seats + protective provisions"

**Scenario**:
- Founder wants $12M pre-money (unrealistic)
- Investor won't budge past $10M pre-money
- Investor offers:
  - "We'll do $10M pre if we get..."
  - "...1 board seat + all protective provisions"
  - "...or $11M pre with observer rights only + fewer protections"
- Founder chooses based on what matters more (valuation vs. governance)

**Benefits**:
- Investor gets governance rights that matter
- Founder feels like they won on valuation
- Both parties feel satisfied

#### Tactic 4: Risk-Based Valuation

**How it works**:
- Justify valuation based on risk factors
- Lower valuation for higher risk, higher valuation for lower risk
- Ties valuation to company metrics

**Example**:
- Pre-seed: $2-5M pre-money (high risk, early stage)
- Seed: $5-10M pre-money (some traction, team proven)
- Series A: $20-50M pre-money (strong metrics, PMF)
- Series B: $100M+ pre-money (proven business, scaling)

**When it works**:
- You have data/benchmarks showing this risk profile
- Founder can't credibly argue their company is lower risk
- Founder accepts logic (fair framework)

#### Tactic 5: Carve-Out Strategy

**How it works**:
- Carve out specific things from broad rights
- Example: "We want anti-dilution protection, except for employee options"
- Narrows scope of investor rights

**Scenario on anti-dilution**:
- Investor wants broad-based weighted average
- Founder resists (too punitive)
- Investor offers:
  - "Broad-based weighted average, except..."
  - "...doesn't trigger on employee option grants up to 15% of fully diluted"
  - "...doesn't trigger on strategic issuances under $500K"
- Both parties happy (investor still protected, founder has flexibility)

**Benefits**:
- Makes terms more founder-friendly
- Investor still gets core protection
- Reduces adversarial tone

## Common Negotiation Impasses & Resolutions

### Impasse 1: Valuation Gap

**Situation**:
- Founder wants $15M pre-money
- Investor offers $8M pre-money
- Gap of 87.5% (huge)

**Root causes**:
- Founder unrealistic about metrics
- Investor undervaluing company
- Lack of comparable data
- Market conditions different

**Resolution options**:

1. **Bring in independent valuation**:
   - Both agree on valuation expert
   - Expert does independent analysis
   - Both agree to accept their valuation
   - Cost: $3-5K, but saves deal

2. **Milestone-based pricing**:
   - Do round at $8M pre-money now
   - Do follow-on at higher valuation ($15M pre) if company hits metrics
   - Both get what they want (investor at lower entry, founder at higher follow-on)
   - Example: "Series A at $15M pre if you hit $500K MRR by Q4"

3. **Data-driven approach**:
   - Both agree on valuation methodology
   - Build bottom-up valuation model
   - Benchmark against comparables
   - Base valuation on agreed-upon inputs
   - This reduces emotion, increases objectivity

4. **Bring in mutual advisor**:
   - Founder's advisor + investor's advisor meet
   - They recommend fair valuation
   - Both sides trust advisors more than each other
   - Usually splits the difference

5. **Walk away**:
   - Deal isn't meant to be
   - Better to pass than do bad deal
   - Founder finds investor at their valuation
   - Investor finds company at theirs

### Impasse 2: Liquidation Preference Disagreement

**Situation**:
- Investor insists on 1x participating preferred
- Founder insists on 1x non-participating
- Both say it's non-negotiable

**Root causes**:
- Founder sees participating as "double-dipping"
- Investor sees it as necessary downside protection
- Emotional attachment to position

**Resolution options**:

1. **Participating with cap**:
   - Investor gets 1x participating preference
   - BUT capped at 2-2.5x total return
   - Example: "1x participating, but max total return is 2.5x"
   - Investor still gets downside protection, less aggressive than uncapped

2. **Tiered participating**:
   - 1x participating for first outcomes ($20-50M exit)
   - 0.5x participating for larger outcomes (>$100M exit)
   - Aligns incentives (investor & founder both win on upside)

3. **Founder discount trigger**:
   - 1x non-participating normally
   - Converts to 1x participating if down round happens
   - Gives investor protection only when needed

4. **Combination with valuation**:
   - Investor: "We'll do non-participating if valuation is $12M pre instead of $15M"
   - Founder: "Deal, let's do non-participating at $12M"
   - Both get something

5. **Explain the math**:
   - Model out actual scenarios ($20M exit, $50M exit, $100M exit)
   - Show what each preference means in actual dollars
   - Often de-escalates emotional debate

### Impasse 3: Protective Provisions Stalemate

**Situation**:
- Investor wants 12 protective provisions
- Founder wants none (or minimal)
- Both see this as core issue

**Root causes**:
- Founder fears loss of control
- Investor fears founder bad decisions
- Trust not established

**Resolution options**:

1. **Prioritize & carve down**:
   - List all investor requests
   - Both prioritize top 5-7 must-haves
   - Agree to include only must-haves
   - Example mutual musts:
     - Sale of company (both need veto)
     - Additional financing (both need consent)
     - Liquidation (investor needs veto)
     - CEO replacement (investor wants veto)

2. **Materiality thresholds**:
   - Investor: "Can't do related-party transaction without approval"
   - Founder: "That's too much control"
   - Compromise: "Can't do related-party transaction >$500K without approval"
   - Gives investor protection without paralyzing company

3. **Sunset provisions**:
   - Protective provisions apply for 24 months
   - Drop off after company hits milestones (e.g., $1M ARR)
   - Founder gets autonomy once company proven
   - Investor gets protection during risky early stage

4. **Supermajority instead of single veto**:
   - Investor: "Want single veto on sale"
   - Founder: "That's too much control"
   - Compromise: "Need supermajority (75%) of preferred holders to approve"
   - If multiple investors, requires consensus (not single veto)

5. **Operational autonomy guarantee**:
   - Investor: "Our protective provisions give us veto"
   - Founder: "But I need to run day-to-day business"
   - Compromise: "Protective provisions for major decisions only. Founder has autonomy on operations"
   - Define "major" = issues >$250K, >10% business impact, etc.

### Impasse 4: Board Seat/Observer Disagreement

**Situation**:
- Investor demands board seat
- Founder wants observer only (or no seat)
- Tension over control

**Root causes**:
- Investor fears founder bad decisions (needs board seat)
- Founder fears investor interference (wants independence)

**Resolution options**:

1. **Observer rights for now, board seat after Series A**:
   - Investor: "Be observer now, get board seat in Series A"
   - Founder: "Fine, you've earned trust by then"
   - Both get timeline they want

2. **Co-investor model**:
   - Multiple investors share single board seat (rotate)
   - Gives board access without founder being outnumbered
   - Only one investor voice on board

3. **Independent director compromise**:
   - Investor doesn't get board seat
   - But investor gets to nominate independent director
   - Gives investor influence without direct control

4. **CEO controls board**:
   - Founder (as CEO) still calls board meetings
   - Founder controls meeting cadence, agenda
   - Investor has seat but limited power over process
   - Gives investor access but founder autonomy

### Impasse 5: Information Rights Burden

**Situation**:
- Investor wants monthly financials + weekly updates
- Founder says it's too burdensome
- Founder has 3-person team, no finance person

**Root causes**:
- Investor needs transparency
- Founder lacks systems/resources for reporting

**Resolution options**:

1. **Tiered approach**:
   - Months 1-6: Bi-weekly updates
   - Months 6-12: Monthly financials + quarterly meetings
   - Year 2+: Quarterly financials + quarterly meetings
   - Ramps up as company matures

2. **Key metrics only**:
   - Don't require full P&L
   - Just ask for: MRR/ARR, customer count, burn rate, runway
   - Can be simple spreadsheet
   - Takes 30 minutes vs. 4 hours

3. **Quarterly board meetings**:
   - Investor gets formal updates 4x/year
   - Covers financial results + strategic updates
   - Sufficient for governance
   - Not monthly burden

4. **Accounting support**:
   - Investor recommends bookkeeper/accountant
   - Investor pays for them (as value-add)
   - Founder gets professional systems
   - Investor gets reliable data

## Tactics to Avoid

### 1. Bait & Switch
**Don't do this**: Agree on terms, then change during legal docs
- Shows bad faith
- Investor will walk away
- Destroys relationship
- Hurts your reputation in ecosystem

### 2. Lies About Traction
**Don't do this**: Fake metrics, misleading numbers, exaggerate growth
- Investor will discover truth during diligence
- Deal dies
- Your reputation gets damaged
- Word spreads in investor community

### 3. Unrealistic Anchoring
**Don't do this**: Ask for $100M pre-money when you have no revenue
- Shows you're unrealistic
- Investor dismisses you
- Wastes everyone's time
- Hurts your credibility

### 4. Ultimatums
**Don't do this**: "This is my final number, take it or leave it"
- Locks you in
- Investor will leave
- Kills negotiation possibility
- Seems arrogant

### 5. Playing Investors Against Each Other
**Don't do this**: Tell investor A you're closing with investor B (false)
- Investors talk to each other
- They'll discover the lie
- You lose both investors
- Reputation destroyed

### 6. Hiding Bad News
**Don't do this**: Delay telling investor about product issues, lost customers, missed milestones
- Investor discovers during diligence
- They feel deceived
- Deal dies
- You lose negotiating credibility

## Negotiation Psychology & Best Practices

### 1. Build Trust First

**Why it matters**:
- Negotiations are easier with trust
- Trusted parties make concessions
- Adversarial negotiations take longer
- Best outcomes from collaborative mindset

**How to build trust**:
- Be honest about challenges
- Admit what you don't know
- Follow through on commitments
- Listen to investor concerns (not just pushing your agenda)
- Show you're reasonable (willing to negotiate)

### 2. Separate Person from Problem

**Concept from "Getting to Yes"**:
- Don't attack investor personally
- Instead, focus on solving the problem together
- Investor isn't enemy, they're solving business puzzle with you

**Example**:
- **Bad**: "You're being unreasonable on valuation"
- **Good**: "We need to find a valuation that works for both of us. Here's what makes sense..."

### 3. Focus on Interests, Not Positions

**Concept**: People negotiate positions, but underlying interests matter more

**Example**:
- **Position**: "I want 1x non-participating preferred"
- **Interest**: "I want to protect founders if exit is disappointing"

Once you understand interest, you can find creative solutions:
- Non-participating preferred ✓ (best solution)
- Lower valuation + participating (if combined with other terms)
- Participating with cap ✓ (partial solution)

### 4. Create Win-Win Scenarios

**How it works**:
- Don't think of negotiation as pie-cutting (fixed pie)
- Think of it as pie-growing (both can win)

**Example - traditional**:
- Higher valuation = founder wins, investor loses
- Lower valuation = investor wins, founder loses

**Example - creative**:
- Lower valuation now + higher valuation in Series A if metrics hit
- Investor wins on entry risk, founder wins on Series A upside
- Both win

### 5. Use Objective Criteria

**Why it works**:
- Hard to argue with data
- Reduces emotion
- Focuses on facts

**Examples**:
- "NVCA says broad-based weighted average is market standard"
- "Comparable companies raised at $15-20M pre for your metrics"
- "Your burn rate means 18 months runway at this capital level"

### 6. Make First Offers Strategically

**Research shows**: First offer influences final number (anchoring effect)

**For founder**:
- If you have leverage (strong metrics), make first offer
- Ask for high valuation (anchor high)
- You'll likely get closer to your target

**For investor**:
- If founder is making unrealistic asks, make counter-offer low
- Anchors negotiation lower
- You'll likely get better terms

**When NOT to make first offer**:
- You're desperate (offer will be low, locks you in)
- You don't have leverage (ask will be unrealistic)
- Better to let other party anchor first

### 7. Silence is Powerful

**Why it works**:
- After you make offer, STOP TALKING
- Let investor respond in silence
- Fills silence by conceding
- Don't explain, don't defend, just wait

### 8. Never Split the Difference

**Example**:
- Founder wants $12M pre-money
- Investor offers $8M pre-money
- Splitting difference = $10M pre-money

**Why not**:
- Rewards unrealistic anchoring
- Both sides learn to anchor more extreme
- Never solves underlying problem (why the gap?)
- You end up in middle ground that doesn't satisfy anyone

**Better approach**:
- Understand interests underneath positions
- Find creative solution that addresses interests
- May result in $11M pre OR different terms that work

## Red Flags During Negotiation

### Investor Red Flags

1. **Constantly changing terms**:
   - Agree on something, then it changes next meeting
   - Shows bad faith
   - Pass on this investor

2. **Bringing in lawyers too early**:
   - Before you've agreed on commercial terms
   - Creates adversarial tone
   - Slows everything down
   - Deal often dies

3. **Asking for terms no one gets**:
   - Wants 2x participating with broad anti-dilution
   - Wants 3 board seats in 3-person board
   - Wants protective provisions that paralyze company
   - Shows they're either inexperienced or predatory

4. **Moving goalposts**:
   - You negotiate on valuation, reach agreement
   - Then investor says they want additional things
   - Bad faith negotiation
   - Walk away

5. **Involving corporate counsel way too early**:
   - Before you've agreed on terms
   - Creates lawyers, not businesspeople, driving negotiation
   - Deal takes 6 months instead of 6 weeks
   - Costs skyrocket

### Founder Red Flags

1. **Refusing to show financials**:
   - Won't share cap table
   - Won't show revenue/burn
   - Red flag on transparency
   - Investors will walk

2. **Constantly changing story**:
   - Different narrative each meeting
   - Can't remember what they said last time
   - Shows bad memory or lying
   - Investor loses trust

3. **Demanding unrealistic valuation**:
   - No revenue, asking for $50M pre-money
   - Pre-revenue company with no traction
   - Shows lack of realism
   - Investor walks

4. **Being adversarial**:
   - Treats investor as enemy
   - Sees every term as battle
   - Won't listen to investor concerns
   - Investor walks (doesn't want to work with difficult founder)

5. **Playing investors against each other**:
   - Tells investor A they're closing with investor B (false)
   - Investors compare notes, discover lie
   - Lose both investors
   - Reputation damaged

## Post-Negotiation: Closing Phase

### Legal Document Review (2-3 weeks)

**Your lawyer's jobs**:
1. Review SPA (Stock Purchase Agreement)
2. Review IRA (Investors' Rights Agreement)
3. Review Voting Agreement
4. Review ROFR/Co-Sale Agreement
5. Check cap table accuracy
6. Ensure all terms negotiated are reflected in docs
7. Identify any new issues that arose

**Typical issues that come up**:
- **Reps & warranties period**: How long are founder reps binding?
  - Investor wants 18-24 months
  - Founder wants 12 months
  - Typical: 12-18 months

- **Escrow holdback**: Is there holdback for reps & warranties?
  - Investor wants 10-15% held for 12 months
  - Founder wants full cash at close
  - Typical: 10% held for 12 months

- **Conditions to closing**: What must be true at close?
  - Financial audits, customer confirmations, etc.
  - Must be achievable by founder

- **Post-close adjustments**: Any adjustments to cap table post-close?
  - Employee options issued
  - Cashless exercises of warrants
  - Make sure all accounted for

### Due Diligence Phase (1-2 weeks)

**What investor will verify**:
- Cap table accuracy (all shares accounted for)
- Founder background checks
- Customer/market verification
- Technology due diligence
- IP ownership (founder owns what they claim)
- Legal issues (any pending lawsuits?)

**What founder needs to prepare**:
- Organize cap table documentation
- Customer references on call
- Prepare data room access
- Organize IP documentation (patents, code ownership)
- Disclose any legal issues proactively

### Closing Logistics (1 week)

**Final items**:
- [ ] Execute all documents
- [ ] Wire funds to designated account
- [ ] Update stock certificates
- [ ] Update records with state
- [ ] Announce funding (press release, etc.)
- [ ] Update 409A valuation for options

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Mistakes by Founders

1. **Not negotiating at all**:
   - Accept first term sheet as-is
   - Lose opportunity to improve terms
   - Likely to regret later

2. **Negotiating for 6+ months**:
   - Endless back-and-forth
   - Both sides exhausted
   - Deal often dies
   - Investor moves to other companies

3. **Skipping lawyer**:
   - Save $10K in legal fees
   - Miss critical issues in docs
   - End up with bad terms
   - Issues surface later (too late to fix)

4. **Not understanding liquidation preference impact**:
   - 1x participating vs. non-participating is huge
   - Can cost you millions in modest exits
   - Must understand scenarios before signing

5. **Not thinking about future rounds**:
   - Negotiating Seed without thinking about Series A
   - Some terms make Series A fundraising hard
   - Stacked preferences eventually trap company

6. **Letting emotions drive negotiation**:
   - Getting angry at investor terms
   - Refusing to negotiate reasonably
   - Walking away from good deals over ego
   - Missing tree for the forest

### Mistakes by Investors

1. **Valuation anchoring too aggressively**:
   - Offer way below realistic
   - Founder dismisses you as unreasonable
   - Miss opportunity
   - Founder does deal with other investor

2. **Asking for unreasonable governance**:
   - Want 2 board seats in 3-seat board
   - Want veto on every decision
   - Founder refuses
   - Deal dies

3. **Playing hardball unnecessarily**:
   - Nickel-and-diming on small terms
   - Creating adversarial tone
   - Founder more defensive in future
   - Relationship sours before even starting

4. **Not following up after term sheet**:
   - Take 2-3 months to finalize documents
   - Founder gets frustrated
   - Founder takes other offer
   - Miss deal

5. **Changing terms after handshake**:
   - Agree on valuation, then want it lower
   - Destroy founder trust
   - Deal dies
   - Reputation damaged

6. **Doing deal with wrong founder**:
   - Founder is difficult/adversarial
   - Won't take investor input
   - Future governance will be painful
   - Should have walked away during negotiation

## Summary

Successful term sheet negotiations require:

1. **Preparation** - Know your numbers, leverage, and walkaway point
2. **Trust** - Build relationship before and during negotiation
3. **Flexibility** - Be willing to trade terms
4. **Data** - Use objective criteria to justify positions
5. **Patience** - 4-8 weeks is typical, rushing leads to mistakes
6. **Good faith** - Be honest, follow through, respect other party
7. **Lawyer** - Use experienced startup lawyer, not courtroom fighter

The best outcomes come from collaborative negotiation where both parties feel they won something. The worst outcomes come from adversarial negotiation where one party "wins" and the other resents the deal forever.
