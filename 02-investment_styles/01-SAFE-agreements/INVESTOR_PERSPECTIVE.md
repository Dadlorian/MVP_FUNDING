# SAFE AGREEMENTS FROM THE INVESTOR PERSPECTIVE
## Risks, Concerns, and Best Practices

---

## INTRODUCTION: WHY INVEST VIA SAFE?

### Why Investors Love SAFEs

| Benefit | Value |
|---------|-------|
| **Quick Deployment** | Capital deployed in days, not months |
| **Simplicity** | 2-3 pages, easy to understand terms |
| **Standardized** | Y Combinator forms, market standard |
| **Founder Alignment** | No interest burden on founders |
| **Downside Protection** | Cap and/or discount protect your ownership |
| **Portfolio Approach** | Invest in many startups quickly |
| **Low Legal Cost** | Minimal legal fees (~$2k vs $20k) |
| **Clean Conversion** | Automatic conversion at Series A |

### When SAFEs Make Sense for Investors

**Best Case:**
- Early-stage company with strong team
- Post-MVP, with initial traction
- Raising $500k-$2M for seed round
- Clear Series A path in 12-18 months
- SAFE terms include cap + discount + pro-rata + MFN

**Result:** SAFEs are efficient way to get downside protection with upside optionality

**Problematic Cases:**
- Company has no clear Series A path (capital trapped)
- SAFE has no valuation cap (no protection in up round)
- No MFN clause (you could be left with worst terms)
- No information rights (can't track progress)
- Founder is evasive about Series A timeline

**Result:** You end up with trapped capital and no protection

## INVESTOR RISK ASSESSMENT

### Risk #1: No Maturity Date / Unlimited Duration

**The Core Problem:**

```
Your Investment Scenario:
- Invest $100k in SAFE
- Expect Series A in 18 months
- Plan for 3-5x return in 5-7 years

Reality Check:
Year 1: Company making progress, Series A expected in 6 months
Year 2: Series A delayed, expected in 6 more months
Year 3: Series A still delayed, company still operating but no raise
Year 4: Company pivoting, Series A timeline unclear
Year 5: Company still operating, SAFE still unconverted
Year 6: Your $100k invested 6 years ago, still no conversion, no liquidity

Capital Opportunity Cost:
- $100k for 6 years = $600k+ opportunity cost
- Could have invested in 6 other startups
- Could have earned stock market returns (~10% annually)
- Stuck waiting for eventual conversion or company exit

SAFE Holder Dilemma:
- Can't force liquidation (not a debt holder)
- Can't force conversion (no maturity trigger)
- Can't force company to raise Series A
- Can't exit your position (non-transferable)
- Capital is stuck indefinitely
```

**Why SAFEs Are Designed This Way:**
- Founder-friendly: no forced conversion, no pressure
- But investor-unfavorable: no guaranteed outcome

**Investor Protection Strategy:**

```
Negotiation Points:

1. Require Conversion Trigger:
   "If no Qualifying Equity Financing by Month 24, SAFE converts to [X]% common stock"
   → Prevents indefinite limbo
   → Forces outcome by specific date

2. Alternative: Expiration + Return:
   "If no Qualifying Financing by Month 20, Company returns investment + 5% annual interest"
   → Investor gets return of capital
   → Interest compensates for time/uncertainty
   → Or investor converts to common stock at that point

3. Request Board Observation:
   "I get monthly board updates and quarterly in-person meetings"
   → Track company progress
   → Assess Series A likelihood
   → Get early warning if timeline slipping

4. Quarterly Updates:
   "Company provides quarterly financial statements and KPI updates"
   → See if company progressing toward Series A
   → Can assess if capital being used effectively
```

**Typical Language to Negotiate:**

```
"If the Company has not conducted a Qualifying Equity Financing by [DATE],
one of the following shall occur at Investor's election:

(a) SAFE automatically converts to [X]% of Common Stock on fully-diluted basis, OR
(b) Company returns original investment amount plus [5]% annual interest, OR
(c) Investor receives board observation rights and quarterly meetings until Series A

This ensures capital is deployed with clear timeline and prevents indefinite holding."
```

### Risk #2: No Interest or Return Guarantee

**The Time Value Problem:**

```
Investor Economics Scenario:
- Invest: $100,000 today (Year 0)
- SAFE: No interest, no guaranteed return
- Expected outcome: Converts to equity in 18 months (Year 1.5)

Wait, That's Fine. But Consider This:

Scenario A: Company Exits Mediocrely
- Year 1: Series A doesn't happen, company slower than expected
- Year 2: Series A at $5M valuation, smaller than anticipated
- SAFE converts to 2% equity (capped because of smaller raise)
- Year 5: Company acquired for $50M total
- Your 2% = $1M return on $100k
- 5-year return: $1M vs. $100k = 10x... sounds good!

But Wait (Time Adjustment):
- Invested $100k in Year 0
- Got $1M in Year 5
- Nominal return: 10x
- Real return (accounting for 3% inflation over 5 years): ~8x
- Annualized return: ~58% per year (actually good!)
- But...

What If You'd Invested in Index Fund Instead?
- $100k in S&P 500, 5 years
- 10% annual return = $160k final value
- Vs. startup: $1M
- Startup much better!

The Problem: What If You Lose?

Scenario B: Company Fails
- Year 1-2: Company runs well, Series A expected
- Year 2-3: Market changes, Series A doesn't materialize
- Year 3-4: Company struggles with capital efficiency
- Year 4-5: Company liquidates
- Your SAFE: Paid back $50k (less than investments, operational costs)
- Your loss: $50k on $100k = 50% loss
- Time: 5 years of capital tied up for 50% loss

The Risk:
- With 20-30 SAFE investments, maybe 5-10 are home runs (10x+)
- Maybe 5-10 are moderate successes (2-5x)
- Maybe 5-10 are breaks (return of capital)
- Maybe 5-10 are losses (50% loss)
- Your portfolio needs the home runs to cover the losses

But If Interest Was Included:
- Invest $100k at 5% annual interest (convertible notes standard)
- Year 5 interest: $25k + return of capital = $125k minimum
- Vs. SAFE: Could be $0 if company fails with no conversion

The Real Problem:
"I'm taking illiquid startup risk without interest compensation.
If this SAFE never converts and company fails, I lose everything.
At minimum, SAFEs should have maturity date + return option."
```

**Negotiation Points:**

```
Ask for one of these:

Option 1: Maturity Date + Return
"If no Qualifying Financing by [Month X], Company returns investment + [5]% annual interest"
→ Investor protected against indefinite holding period

Option 2: Enhanced Discount
"20% discount on Series A + no valuation cap"
→ Investor gets price break to offset time value
→ If Series A successful, discount provides upside

Option 3: Higher Valuation Cap
"$2M cap instead of $3M"
→ Lower cap = higher ownership %
→ Ensures better return if Series A at expected valuation
→ Compensates for lost time value

Option 4: Combination
"18-month maturity date, 20% discount, $3M cap, MFN, pro-rata"
→ Multi-layer protection
→ Covers maturity (time), conversion (discount + cap), follow-on (pro-rata), fairness (MFN)
```

### Risk #3: Subordinate to All Debt

**The Liquidation Waterfall Problem:**

```
Investment Scenario:
- You invest $500k SAFE
- Company also has:
  - $200k bank loan
  - $100k vendor credit lines
  - $50k equipment financing

Total Obligations: $750k debt + $500k your SAFE

Company Fails, Liquidates:
- Liquidation proceeds: $400k (company sold for $400k, all assets + IP)

Payment Order:
1. Bank loan ($200k) - paid in full ✓
2. Vendor credits ($100k) - paid in full ✓
3. Equipment financing ($50k) - paid in full ✓
4. YOUR SAFE ($500k) - gets $50k (split with other equity holders)

Your Loss:
- Invested: $500k
- Received: $50k
- Loss: $450k (90% loss)

While Bank: Fully protected despite much later funding

The Unfairness:
- Debt came later, but paid first
- You took more risk (earlier stage), but paid last
- Bank had collateral (actual assets), you had equity promise
- Equity is residual interest (last in line)
```

**Why This Happens:**
- SAFEs technically become equity upon conversion
- But unconverted SAFEs can be ambiguous (debt-like but equity-promised)
- In liquidation, courts often treat unconverted SAFEs as junior creditors
- All actual debt holders paid before SAFE holders

**Investor Protection Strategy:**

```
Negotiation Points:

1. Understand Debt Level:
   Ask founder: "What is your current debt outstanding?"
   - Total bank debt
   - Vendor credit lines
   - Equipment financing
   - Other obligations

   Red line: Total debt shouldn't exceed ≥ your equity SAFE amount
   Example: If you're investing $500k, debt should be <$500k

2. Request Debt Cap:
   "Company will not incur more than $[X] in debt without investor consent"
   → Prevents founder from taking on debt that makes SAFE junior
   → Protects your liquidation priority
   → Ensures you're not subordinate to excessive debt

3. Request Convertible Debt Instead:
   If amount is large enough, ask for convertible note instead of SAFE
   - Convertible notes typically senior to equity SAFEs
   - Have interest accruing, maturity date
   - Better creditor treatment in bankruptcy
   - More protection than SAFEs

4. Board Observation:
   "I observe board meetings where capital structure discussed"
   → See if new debt being taken on
   → Object if debt threatens your position
   → Early warning if company struggling (more debt = more risk)

5. Information Rights:
   "Quarterly updates include debt obligations"
   → Track if company accumulating risky debt
   → Can raise concerns if debt becoming excessive
```

**When to Walk Away:**
```
Red Lines - Don't Invest:
- Company has debt > 50% of your intended investment
- Founder refuses to discuss debt obligations
- Founder plans to take on more debt post-your-SAFE
- Company in debt distress (vendor credits, not paying on time)
- Previous SAFEs/equity investors subordinate to debt (bad signal)

These situations mean your SAFE will be deeply subordinate in any downside scenario.
```

### Risk #4: No Governance or Information Rights (If Not Negotiated)

**The Transparency Problem:**

```
Your Investment Scenario:
- Invest $500k SAFE
- No voting rights until conversion
- No board seat or observation
- No information rights
- Company is a black box to you

What Happens:
Month 0-3: Founder emails you with progress updates
Month 3-6: Updates become sporadic
Month 6-9: You send email, get brief response
Month 9-12: Silence, you have no idea what's happening
Month 12+: Founder finally emails: "Series A fell through, pivoting business"

Your Position:
- You have $500k at risk
- You have zero visibility into company
- You don't know if capital being used effectively
- You don't know if Series A timeline realistic
- You're powerless to influence decisions
- You can't even get information

What Should Have Been Different:
- Monthly updates, with key metrics
- Quarterly board-style meetings
- Real-time notice of material changes
- Access to cap table, payroll, burn rate

The Investor's Frustration:
"I put significant capital at risk and I can't even track company progress?
This is not acceptable. I should have negotiated information rights."
```

**Negotiation Points:**

```
Minimum Information Rights:

1. Quarterly Reporting (due within 45 days of quarter close):
   - Unaudited financial statements (P&L, balance sheet, cash flow)
   - KPI updates (users, revenue, engagement, etc.)
   - Written update on business progress
   - Material changes since last report

2. Annual Reporting (due within 120 days of year end):
   - Audited or reviewed financials (if company revenue > $1M)
   - Updated fully-diluted cap table
   - Annual operating plan and budget
   - Board minutes and resolutions

3. Real-Time Notification:
   - Material legal actions (lawsuits, regulatory issues)
   - Significant departures (key employees, founders leaving)
   - Major financing events (new debt, SAFEs, conversions)
   - Change of control discussions
   - Within 10 days of occurrence

4. Board Access:
   "Investor has right to observe all Board of Directors meetings"
   - Monthly or quarterly meetings
   - Access to board materials
   - 5-day advance notice of meeting
   - Bound by confidentiality
   - Cannot vote on conflicted matters (rare)

5. Cap Table Review:
   "Company will provide fully-diluted cap table"
   - Updated cap table within 30 days of any financing
   - Shows all issued shares, SAFEs, options, warrants
   - Updated valuations and share prices
   - Helps investor track ownership % over time
```

**Typical Language:**

```
"Company shall provide Investor with:

(a) Within 45 days of each quarter-end: Unaudited financial statements
    and operating metrics

(b) Within 120 days of fiscal year-end: Audited financial statements (if
    Company revenue exceeds $1M)

(c) Within 10 business days of announcement: Notice of Series A/B term sheet,
    debt financing, or change of control discussions

(d) Right to observe all board meetings upon 5 days' notice

(e) Within 30 days of any financing event: Updated fully-diluted cap table"
```

**Why to Push for This:**
- Reduces information asymmetry
- Lets you track if Series A on track
- Lets you see if founders executing or failing
- Gives you basis to decide if follow-on investment needed
- Shows founder transparency (good sign) vs. evasiveness (bad sign)

### Risk #5: No Pro-Rata Rights (Initially)

**The Dilution Problem:**

```
Your Investment Scenario:
- Invest $200k SAFE, converts to 5% Series A
- Series A: Company raises $5M additional Series A capital
- You receive: Nothing (no pro-rata rights in SAFE)

What Happens:
- Series A creates: 25% new ownership (5% for lead, 20% for other investors)
- Your 5% is now: 5% / (100% + 25%) = 4% post-Series A
- You've been diluted 1% despite not having opportunity to follow
- Other investors now own more than you

What If You Could Have Participated:
- Series A $5M round, you have 5% pro-rata
- Your pro-rata = 5% of $5M = $250k
- You could have invested $250k to maintain 5% ownership
- But SAFE gives no such right

Compounding Problem:
- Series B: You still not in, more dilution
- Series C: You still not in, more dilution
- By Series D, your 5% may be 2% due to dilution
- While lead investors maintained/increased ownership through pro-rata

The Founder's Perspective:
"SAFEs don't include pro-rata. You can negotiate it into your SAFE,
but most early SAFEs don't have pro-rata rights."

The Series A Investor's Perspective:
"We'll offer pro-rata rights to new Series A investors, but not to SAFEs
that converted at lower valuations. You had your chance to follow."

Your Frustration:
"I invested first, took more risk, but now have no right to maintain
ownership. This is backwards."
```

**Negotiation Points:**

```
Ask for Pro-Rata Rights:

"Upon conversion to Series A Preferred Stock, Investor shall have the
right to participate pro-rata in all future equity financings conducted
by the Company, for a period of [7] years or until [Company reaches
Series D/IPO], whichever is earlier.

Investor's pro-rata percentage = Investor's post-conversion ownership %

Example:
- Your SAFE converts to 5% ownership in Series A
- Series B: You can invest 5% of Series B round at same price as other Series B investors
- You invest: You maintain 5% ownership
- You don't invest: Your ownership dilutes per your ownership %"

Why This Matters:
- Lets you maintain your ownership % if you want
- Gives you visibility into all future rounds
- Prevents surprise dilution from rounds you don't know about
- More valuable for follow-on investors in seed than for seed investors

Reality Check:
- Most SAFEs don't include pro-rata rights
- Pro-rata more common in Series A Preferred Stock
- As SAFE investor, you get pro-rata only if you negotiate it
- Most early investors accept lack of pro-rata as limitation of SAFE
```

### Risk #6: Unfavorable Conversion Terms

**The Math Problem:**

```
Scenario A: Uncapped SAFE with No Discount
Your SAFE: $100k, no cap, no discount

Series A Outcome A (Huge Success):
- Series A at $50M pre-money, pricing $10/share
- Your conversion: $100k / $10 = 10,000 shares
- Post-SAFE total shares: 5M (roughly)
- Your ownership: 10,000 / 5M = 0.2%
- Investor feeling: "I took all the early risk for 0.2%?"

Series A Outcome B (Normal Success):
- Series A at $5M pre-money, pricing $2/share
- Your conversion: $100k / $2 = 50,000 shares
- Post-SAFE total shares: 2.5M
- Your ownership: 50,000 / 2.5M = 2%
- Investor feeling: "Okay, 2% is reasonable"

Series A Outcome C (Failure):
- Series A at $1M pre-money, pricing $0.50/share
- Your conversion: $100k / $0.50 = 200,000 shares
- Post-SAFE total shares: 2M
- Your ownership: 200,000 / 2M = 10%
- Investor feeling: "I own 10% of a dying company"

The Problem:
- Your upside is capped by Series A success (can't own more than % of company)
- Your downside is unlimited (own more %, but company worth less)
- In success case, you're left with minimal ownership
- In failure case, ownership % is irrelevant (company worthless)

Uncapped SAFE is Worst Case:
- Successful company: minimal ownership, co-founder got diluted
- Failing company: large ownership %, but worthless

Investor Conclusion:
"Uncapped SAFE is bad for investor. Always negotiate cap."
```

**Better Conversion Terms:**

```
Scenario B: SAFE with $5M Post-Money Cap + 20% Discount

Series A Outcome A (Huge Success):
- Series A at $50M pre-money, pricing $10/share
- Cap applies: Max you own = $100k / $5M = 2%
- Your shares: 2% of fully-diluted
- Your ownership: 2% (capped, protected)
- Investor feeling: "Cap protected me, I got reasonable ownership"

Series A Outcome B (Normal Success):
- Series A at $5M pre-money, pricing $2/share
- Cap doesn't apply (Series A more favorable than cap)
- Discount applies: $2 × (1 - 20%) = $1.60
- Your conversion: $100k / $1.60 = 62,500 shares
- Your ownership: 2.5% (better than uncapped, due to discount)
- Investor feeling: "Discount gave me extra shares, I'm happy"

Series A Outcome C (Failure):
- Series A at $1M pre-money, pricing $0.50/share
- Discount applies: $0.50 × (1 - 20%) = $0.40
- Your conversion: $100k / $0.40 = 250,000 shares
- Your ownership: 12.5% (even worse case, at least you got more shares via discount)
- Investor feeling: "Discount helped in down case too, at least I have some ownership"

Compare To Uncapped:
- Success case: Uncapped 0.2% vs. Capped 2% = Cap is 10x better!
- Normal case: Uncapped 2% vs. Discount 2.5% = Discount slightly better
- Failure case: Uncapped 10% vs. Discount 12.5% = Discount slightly better

Conclusion:
"CAP + DISCOUNT is clearly superior to uncapped SAFE"
```

## INVESTOR BEST PRACTICES

### Best Practice #1: Negotiate for Maximum Protections

**The Protection Stack:**

```
Must-Have Protections:

1. Valuation Cap (Non-Negotiable)
   - Post-money cap of [X]% of expected Series A valuation
   - Example: Expect Series A at $8M pre, request $4M post-money cap
   - Cap protects you in up rounds
   - Shows founder confidence (willing to cap)

2. Discount Rate (Strongly Preferred)
   - 15-25% depending on risk
   - Pre-seed higher discount (25%), seed lower (15%)
   - Discount protects you across all scenarios
   - Shows early investor reward

3. MFN Clause (Essential)
   - "If any investor gets better terms, I get them too"
   - Ensures you're never left with worst terms
   - 12-month time limit is reasonable
   - Scope: cap, discount, investor rights

4. Pro-Rata Rights (Strongly Preferred)
   - Right to maintain ownership % in future rounds
   - Only applies after conversion
   - Especially valuable for large investments
   - Shows investor treated fairly

5. Information Rights (Essential)
   - Quarterly financials
   - Annual board access
   - Notification of major events
   - Cap table visibility

Nice-to-Have Protections:

6. Board Observation (Preferred)
   - Attend board meetings, receive materials
   - See company decisions firsthand
   - Build relationships with management
   - Valuable for follow-on decisions

7. Anti-Dilution (Negotiable)
   - In down rounds, adjust conversion price
   - Broad-based weighted average standard
   - Protects your ownership in bad scenarios

8. Maturity/Expiration (Important)
   - If no Series A by Date X, SAFE converts or returns
   - Prevents indefinite limbo
   - Creates forcing function for company
```

### Best Practice #2: Understand the Cap and Discount Interplay

**The Protection Matrix:**

```
Understanding Conversion:

If Cap Applies (Company succeeds, Series A valued > Cap):
- You get the CAP benefit
- Discount doesn't help (Series A price is already good)
- Result: You own % = Investment / Cap

If Discount Applies (Series A valued < Cap implies):
- You get the DISCOUNT benefit
- Cap doesn't help (Series A price is already good)
- Result: You pay series A price × (1 - discount)

If Neither Applies:
- You convert at Series A price with no benefit
- Company valued between cap and discount implications
- Rare scenario

Investor Implication:
"Both cap and discount exist because in different scenarios, each protects you.
Having both ensures you get protection regardless of Series A outcome."

Example Scenarios:

Company has HUGE success:
- Series A at $30M pre-money
- Your $4M cap is crushed by huge success
- But cap still gives you 4% max ownership
- Without cap, you'd own 0.1% (uncapped)
- Cap saved you from dilution
- You're happy despite huge upside (you own 4%, not 0.1%)

Company has moderate success:
- Series A at $5M pre-money (expected)
- Your $4M cap doesn't apply
- Your 20% discount applies
- You get discount benefit (lower price than other Series A investors)
- You're happy with discount protection

Company has down round:
- Series A at $2M pre-money (down from expectations)
- Your $4M cap doesn't apply (down round benefits from discount)
- Your 20% discount applies (lower price than other investors)
- You're happy with discount protection (own more shares)
- Cap doesn't help in down round (discount better)

Conclusion:
"Both cap and discount are useful in different scenarios.
Having both provides maximum protection."
```

### Best Practice #3: Analyze the Cap Relative to Series A Expectations

**The Cap Setting Art:**

```
Framework for Cap Setting:

Step 1: Estimate expected Series A valuation
"Based on company stage, market, and comparables, Series A likely at:
  - Conservative: $3M pre-money
  - Base case: $7M pre-money
  - Optimistic: $12M pre-money"

Step 2: Determine what post-money cap implies
"If I want [X]% ownership in Series A:
  - Investment: $100k
  - Post-money cap should be: $100k / [X]% = [Cap]"

Example:
"I want to own at least 2% in Series A:
  - Investment: $100k
  - Cap required: $100k / 2% = $5M post-money cap"

Step 3: Pressure-test cap against Series A scenarios

Conservative Series A ($3M pre):
- If cap applies ($5M post-money), you own: 2%
- Company values at $3M pre, so total post = $3M pre + new capital
- (This is complex math, depends on Series A size)
- Rough: You own desired 2%

Base Case Series A ($7M pre):
- Series A at $7M pre, your cap at $5M post
- Cap applies, you own: $100k / $5M = 2%
- Capping up round benefit, but getting 2%

Optimistic Series A ($12M pre):
- Series A at $12M pre, your cap at $5M post
- Cap applies, you own: $100k / $5M = 2%
- Missing out on upside, but 2% secured
- Without cap, you'd own 0.3%
- Cap saved you 1.7% of ownership

Step 4: Assess if cap is reasonable
"$5M post-money cap is fair given:
  - $100k investment
  - Early stage company
  - Expected Series A at $6-8M pre
  - Ensures I own 2-3% minimum"

Step 5: Consider discount instead of cap
"If founder won't accept $5M cap, ask for:
  - Higher cap: $6-7M, OR
  - Higher discount: 25% instead of 20%, OR
  - Both but lower: $6M cap + 20% discount"
```

### Best Practice #4: Build Founder Relationships

**Why This Matters:**

```
As SAFE investor, you have:
- NO voting rights
- NO board seat
- NO veto power
- NO formal input on decisions

So your only leverage is:
- Relationship with founder
- Ability to provide strategic advice
- Network and connections
- Potential to follow-on invest

Founder, in turn, benefits from:
- Your strategic input (informal)
- Your network introductions
- Your credibility for Series A
- Your potential follow-on investment

The dynamic:
"You're betting on founder's execution.
Founder is betting on your advice and follow-on support.
Relationship is the currency here."

How to Build Relationships:

1. Post-investment communication:
   "Let's schedule monthly 1-on-1s (not emails, actual calls)"
   "I want to understand your challenges and help where I can"

2. Provide value beyond capital:
   "Here are 3 people who might be important to know"
   "Have you thought about partnering with company X?"
   "I've seen similar companies struggle with Y, here's how they solved it"

3. Show genuine interest in success:
   "I want you to win, not just make money on this round"
   "What's the biggest challenge you're facing right now?"

4. Be helpful without being intrusive:
   "I've had the opportunity to observe many startups"
   "Here's what I've learned works, here's what doesn't"
   "You're the expert on your company, I defer to you"

5. Show patience and realism:
   "Building a company takes longer than expected"
   "I'm committed to supporting you through the ups and downs"
   "Celebrate wins together when they happen"

Long-term benefit:
- Founder gives you early notice of Series A
- Founder asks for follow-on advice
- Founder prioritizes your participation in Series A
- Founder introduces you to other founders
- Your network becomes more valuable
```

### Best Practice #5: Due Diligence Before Investing

**The Investor's Checklist:**

```
Before You Write a Check:

1. Team Assessment:
   [ ] Have I met the founder(s) in person?
   [ ] Do they have relevant experience?
   [ ] Do they have complementary skills?
   [ ] Are they coachable and take feedback?
   [ ] Can they execute in difficult times?
   [ ] Have I checked references (prior employers, investors)?

2. Product Assessment:
   [ ] Does product exist and work?
   [ ] Have I used the product myself?
   [ ] Is there customer traction?
   [ ] Do customers love it, or just use it?
   [ ] Are there paying customers?
   [ ] Is product defensible (technical, brand, network)?

3. Market Assessment:
   [ ] Is the market large ($1B+)?
   [ ] Is the market growing?
   [ ] Who are the competitors?
   [ ] What's the competitive advantage?
   [ ] Is founder's analysis credible?

4. Business Model Assessment:
   [ ] Is there a clear path to revenue?
   [ ] Are unit economics showing promise?
   [ ] Is the burn rate reasonable?
   [ ] Do they have capital efficiency story?
   [ ] Is Series A timeline realistic?

5. Cap Table Assessment:
   [ ] How much capital already raised?
   [ ] How many other investors?
   [ ] What are cap table terms?
   [ ] Is cap table clean or fragmented?
   [ ] Are previous SAFEs/convertibles appropriately terms?
   [ ] Are founders still aligned (equity stakes sensible)?

6. Financial Assessment:
   [ ] Have I reviewed financial statements (if any)?
   [ ] Do KPIs align with founder narrative?
   [ ] Is burn rate sustainable?
   [ ] Do they have runway to Series A?
   [ ] Are financial projections realistic?

7. SAFE-Specific Assessment:
   [ ] Are SAFE terms favorable for investor?
   [ ] Does cap align with Series A expectations?
   [ ] Is discount reasonable for stage?
   [ ] Do I have information rights?
   [ ] Do I have board observation?
   [ ] Is MFN clause included?
   [ ] Is pro-rata included (if I want it)?
   [ ] Can I actually exit this position (liquidation scenario)?

8. Risk Assessment:
   [ ] What could go wrong with this company?
   [ ] Is founder aware of risks?
   [ ] What's my realistic return scenario (3x, 10x, loss)?
   [ ] Can I afford to lose this $100k?
   [ ] Would this be in my top 10% of investments?

9. Trust Assessment:
   [ ] Do I trust the founder to execute?
   [ ] Do I trust the founder to be honest about challenges?
   [ ] Do I trust the founder with my capital?
   [ ] Would I invest again in this founder's next company?
   [ ] Is the founder's story compelling?

Red Flags - Don't Invest:
✗ Founder won't answer direct questions or is evasive
✗ Cap table is messy or fragmented
✗ Founder doesn't have clear vision
✗ Team doesn't have relevant experience
✗ No product or only vaporware
✗ Financials don't support founder narrative
✗ Founder is unwilling to accept reasonable SAFE terms
✗ Series A timeline is vague ("maybe in 18-24 months")
✗ Company has significant debt (making SAFE junior)
✗ Founder is unwilling to provide information rights
```

## INVESTOR NEGOTIATION TACTICS

### Tactic #1: Anchor on Cap Size

```
Founder: "We're raising at $4M post-money cap"
You: "I need $3M post-money cap"
Founder: "That's too low, we're confident in our valuation"

Your response:
"Let me explain my reasoning. You're expecting Series A at:
- Raise: $2-3M
- Pre-money valuation: $5-8M
- That implies post-money of $7-11M

If I have a $4M post-money cap on my $100k investment:
- My ownership: $100k / $4M = 2.5%
- If Series A at $7M pre (base case), my ownership post-Series A: ~2%
- If Series A at $10M pre (optimistic), my ownership post-Series A: ~1.5%

I'm getting diluted in success case. I need $3M cap to ensure:
- Base case ownership: ~3%
- Optimistic case ownership: ~2%

This is industry standard for seed rounds. Let me show you comparables..."
```

**Why This Works:**
- Grounds your ask in Series A expectations
- Shows you've done analysis
- Makes ask seem logical, not arbitrary
- Gives founder option to defend their Series A expectations

### Tactic #2: Trade Discount for Cap

```
Founder: "We can do $3M cap or 25% discount, not both"
You: "I need cap AND discount for downside protection"

Your response:
"Let me understand. You're saying:
- Option A: $3M cap, 15% discount
- Option B: $4M cap, 25% discount
- Or one or the other?

My concern: In failure scenario, I want protection.
- Down round at $1M pre: Discount saves me more shares
- But huge success at $15M pre: Cap saves me ownership

Here's my proposal: $3.5M cap + 20% discount
- This is middle ground
- I get cap protection in upside
- I get discount protection in downside
- You're comfortable with both

Or, if you can only do one:
- I'd prefer cap ($3M) with higher discount (25%) instead
- Downside protection via discount is good, cap is better"
```

**Why This Works:**
- Treats cap and discount as substitutes, tradeable
- Shows you're reasonable (middle ground offer)
- If founder says "can't do both," you know which one to choose (cap preferred)

### Tactic #3: Use MFN to Secure Better Terms

```
Founder: "I'm offering $4M cap, take it or leave it"
You: "I'll invest with MFN clause"

What you're doing:
- Accepting their $4M cap for now
- But securing MFN protection
- If they give better terms to next investor, you automatically get them

Founder reaction:
- Initially: "Okay, investor accepts $4M cap with MFN"
- Later, Month 6: Another investor negotiates $3M cap
- That investor gets $3M cap
- Your MFN triggers
- You automatically get $3M cap

Result:
- You got $3M cap without the negotiation battle
- Because MFN forced founder to give you the better term
- Founder more willing to give slightly better terms to later investors if MFN applies

Why this matters:
- MFN converts "late-mover disadvantage" into advantage
- Forces founder to consider implications of giving better terms
- Other investors see MFN, know it won't help them specifically
- You win by being first investor with MFN
```

### Tactic #4: Request Board Observation as Sweetener

```
Founder: "I won't accept $3M cap, sticking with $4M"
You: "Okay, I'll accept $4M cap, but I need board observation rights"

Why this works:
- You gave up cap demand
- But got board observation instead
- Board observation gives you:
  - Monthly visibility into company
  - Early warning if Series A slipping
  - Relationships with management
  - Basis for follow-on investment decision
  - Access to early information

Founder perspective:
- "Investor gave up on cap demand"
- "Board observation is information sharing, I can manage"
- "Better than having investor always complaining about cap"
- "Investor can help advise board"

Result:
- Win-win: You get visibility, founder keeps higher cap
```

### Tactic #5: Walk Away if Terms Too Unfavorable

```
Scenario: Founder unwilling to negotiate
- "I'm offering SAFEs with no cap, no discount"
- "Take it or leave it"
- "Other investors are fine with it"

Your response:
"I appreciate the opportunity, but I can't accept uncapped SAFE without
discount protection. The risk/reward doesn't make sense for my portfolio.

If you change your mind, I'm happy to discuss:
- $4M post-money cap, OR
- 20% discount with no cap, OR
- Some combination

If not, I'll pass and I wish you luck. Great team, interesting market."

Why this matters:
- Shows you have standards
- Demonstrates you're not desperate
- Sometimes founders will reconsider after you walk
- Better to pass than accept terrible terms
- Your capital is valuable, deploy it only for good terms
```

## INVESTOR RED FLAGS

### Red Flag #1: Uncapped SAFEs

**Red Line:** "I'm offering SAFEs with no cap"

**Why It's Bad:**
- No protection in up-round scenarios
- In success case, you could own 0.5% on early risk
- Uncompensated for founder-favorable structure

**Your Response:**
"I can't invest in uncapped SAFE. I need cap protection OR higher discount. Would you accept $4M cap?"

### Red Flag #2: No Information Rights

**Red Line:** "I don't provide quarterly reports to investors"

**Why It's Bad:**
- Can't track capital use
- Can't assess Series A timeline
- No visibility into company health
- Founder could be hiding problems

**Your Response:**
"Quarterly reporting is standard. I need:
- Quarterly financial updates
- Annual board access
- Notice of material events

If you won't provide this, I'll pass."

### Red Flag #3: Evasive About Series A Timeline

**Red Line:** Founder says "Series A timeline is 18-24 months, maybe longer"

**Why It's Bad:**
- SAFE has no maturity, could be indefinite
- "Maybe longer" means Series A might never happen
- Capital could be stuck 5+ years

**Your Response:**
"I need clarity on Series A timeline. I'll invest if:
- Series A target is Month 15-18, with Plan B by Month 24
- If no Series A by Month 24, SAFE converts to [X]% common stock
- Or SAFE returns investment plus interest

Can you commit to these terms?"

### Red Flag #4: Multiple SAFEs with Conflicting Terms

**Red Line:** You discover company already issued 7 SAFEs with different caps and discounts

**Why It's Bad:**
- MFN clauses will cascade
- Cap table becomes complex
- Series A investor will be concerned
- Your SAFE could have worse relative terms if new investor gets better deal

**Your Response:**
"I see you've already issued 7 SAFEs with different terms. This is complex.
How have you managed MFN obligations? Can you show me cap table analysis?

For this to work, I need:
- Clear MFN policy (which SAFEs have it, scope, timing)
- Aggregated cap table impact
- Series A timeline (to avoid extended SAFE period)

Otherwise, I'm concerned about cap table becoming unmanageable."

### Red Flag #5: Founder Won't Discuss Capitalization

**Red Line:** Founder says "You don't need to know details about other SAFEs"

**Why It's Bad:**
- You can't assess cap table impact
- You can't calculate your real dilution
- Founder is hiding something
- Red flag for transparency

**Your Response:**
"I need to understand the full cap table to assess my investment:
- Total SAFE capital raised
- Cap and discount terms for each SAFE
- Which SAFEs have MFN, pro-rata, information rights
- Aggregate dilution impact

This is standard due diligence. If you're unwilling to share, I'll pass."
