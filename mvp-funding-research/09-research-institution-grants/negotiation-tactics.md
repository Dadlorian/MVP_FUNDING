# Negotiation Tactics: Faculty and TTO Discussions

## Overview

This document provides tactical guidance for navigating conversations with faculty partners and Technology Transfer Offices (TTOs). It outlines specific negotiation sequences, leverage points, and communication strategies that have proven effective in closing research partnerships.

---

## Part 1: Faculty Negotiation Dynamics

### Understanding Faculty Motivations

Before any negotiation, understand what drives faculty decision-making:

| Driver | Strength | How to Leverage |
|--------|----------|-----------------|
| Research advancement | Very High | Show how partnership accelerates their research agenda |
| Funding | High | Emphasize grant funding will flow to their lab |
| Student/postdoc opportunities | High | Highlight opportunities for mentees |
| Publication potential | High | Promise publication opportunities and data |
| Commercialization interest | Medium-High | Frame as research contribution to society |
| Career advancement | Medium | Positioning as high-impact research enhances tenure case |
| Industry connections | Medium | Offer future introductions to other industry partners |
| Equity/financial upside | Low-Medium | Some faculty interested; depends on culture |

**Key Insight**: Faculty are not motivated by company success or profit. They're motivated by research advancement, funding, and career impact. Frame all discussions through that lens.

## Part 2: The Faculty Negotiation Sequence

### Phase 1: The Initial Contact (Weeks 1-2)

**Objective**: Get a meeting. Nothing more.

**Email Template:**

```
Subject: Research Partnership Opportunity in [Area]

Dear Dr. [Name],

I recently read your paper on [specific paper], and your findings regarding
[specific insight] directly align with technology we're developing.

Our company, [Company Name], is working on [specific technology], and I believe
your expertise in [specific domain] could be instrumental in validating our approach.

Would you have 20 minutes next week for a brief call to explore whether a
partnership might be mutually beneficial? No pressure—I'm happy to work around
your schedule.

Best regards,
[Name]
[Title]
[Company]
[Email/Phone]
```

**Tactical Notes**:
- Keep under 150 words
- Reference specific paper (shows genuine interest)
- Mention his/her area of expertise (ego boost)
- Make ask concrete (20 minutes, not "let's explore partnership")
- Timing: Send Tuesday-Wednesday (not Monday morning spam rush, not Friday)
- Response rate target: 20-30% (expect 1 response per 3-5 outreaches)

**If No Response After 1 Week**:
- Send one follow-up email (don't exceed 2 emails)
- Alternative: Call department secretary, ask for best time to reach faculty
- Alternative: Attend conference where faculty is presenting; approach in person

**Red Flag**: If faculty doesn't respond to 2 emails, move on. Not everyone is responsive or interested. Preserve bandwidth for faculty who engage.

---

### Phase 2: The Initial Call (Week 2-3)

**Objective**: Determine if faculty sees research value. Explore interest level.

**Call Preparation** (30 minutes before call):
1. Review 5 most recent faculty publications (skim; know main contributions)
2. Prepare 3-5 specific technical questions on their research
3. Prepare 2-3 minute overview of your technology (problem statement + solution)
4. Anticipate objections (unrealistic timeline, too early-stage, competing priorities)

**Call Structure** (20 minutes total):

**Minute 0-1: Introduction**
- "Thanks so much for taking time. I know you're busy, so I'll be efficient."
- Establish rapport (mutual connection, shared interest in research area)

**Minute 1-3: Show You Did Homework**
- "I've been impressed by your recent work on [specific research]."
- Reference specific insight from their recent publication
- Ask one genuine technical question about their research
- **Goal**: Show deep knowledge; demonstrate you're not mass-emailing

**Minute 3-7: Pitch Your Technology** (not company, not business model—just technology)
- "We're developing [technology] to solve [specific problem]."
- Reference faculty member's research: "Your work identifies [gap]; we believe [technology] addresses that"
- 2-3 minute explanation; use simple language
- Don't oversell; be honest about current stage (prototype, validated, etc.)

**Minute 7-12: Explore Faculty Interest**
- "Do you see potential research value in this?"
- Listen. Don't talk. Faculty will tell you if interested.
- If positive: "What would research collaboration look like from your perspective?"
- If lukewarm: "What would need to happen for this to be interesting?"
- If negative: Thank them for time, move on gracefully

**Minute 12-16: Discuss Next Steps (if interested)**
- "I'd love to explore this further. Would it make sense to schedule a follow-up call with our technical team?"
- Or: "Could I introduce you to [co-founder/CTO] who can dive deeper on the technical specifics?"
- Propose: In-person meeting at their university (shows respect, builds relationship)

**Minute 16-20: Closing**
- Confirm next step
- Send thank-you email within 1 hour (recap call, confirm next step, attach anything discussed)

**Critical Tactics**:
- Let them talk 50% of the call. You talk 50%.
- Ask follow-up questions instead of pitching.
- Never discuss business model, funding, or money on first call.
- If they're interested, they'll ask about money/structure.

**Post-Call Evaluation**:
- **Hot**: "I see research value. Let's discuss this further." → Schedule follow-up
- **Warm**: "Interesting. I need to think about it." → Wait 1 week, send data/paper, request another call
- **Cold**: "I'm not sure it fits my research." → Thank them, move on

### Phase 3: The Second Call or In-Person Meeting (Week 3-4)

**Objective** (if hot): Move from "interesting research" to "let's explore partnership"

**For Hot Leads** (schedule in-person):
- Invite to campus, buy lunch/coffee
- Bring co-founder or CTO
- Come prepared with 1-2 page research overview
- Discuss specific research objectives (what would 12-month project look like?)
- Gauge willingness to be lead PI on a grant

**Conversation Focus**:
1. **Research Scope**: "What would 12 months of focused research on [problem] look like?"
   - Faculty typically will sketch outline
   - Listen for their natural research direction
   - Align your needs with their direction (not opposite)

2. **Team & Resources**: "Who would be on the research team? Any postdocs or grad students?"
   - Understanding team size helps budget planning
   - Larger teams = higher budgets = easier to fund
   - Small teams (just faculty) = harder to justify budget

3. **Timeline Compatibility**: "How long would this research take?"
   - Faculty often want 18-24 months for thorough work
   - Push for 12-month initial phase (better for grants)
   - Agree to Phase II if Phase I goes well

4. **Prior Funding Experience**: "Have you led grants in this area before?"
   - You want faculty with grant-writing experience
   - Inexperienced faculty require more hand-holding
   - Adjust your support level accordingly

5. **TTO Familiarity**: "Have you worked with [University] TTO on IP agreements before?"
   - Experienced faculty can navigate TTO faster
   - Inexperienced faculty can have unrealistic IP expectations
   - Offer to manage TTO relationships if helpful

**Key Statement to Gauge Interest**:

> "I'm imagining a partnership where we develop a research agreement for 12 months,
> pursue a federal grant [NSF SBIR, NIH SBIR, etc.], and you lead the research with
> your team. The university would own patents; we'd get an exclusive commercial license.
> Does that model feel workable to you?"

**Listen for**:
- **Positive**: "Yes, that's how I typically structure industry partnerships." → Move forward
- **Cautious**: "I'd want to discuss with my TTO first." → Good sign (they're being responsible)
- **Negative**: "I'm concerned about IP claims" or "I wouldn't give exclusive rights." → Red flag (might not be right partner)

**Decision Point**: If faculty is warm but not hot, ask: "What would need to happen for you to be excited about this?"
- If answer is reasonable (data, publication, meeting team), take action
- If answer is vague ("I'll think about it"), they're likely not the right fit

### Phase 4: The Formal Partnership Discussion (Week 4-6)

**Objective**: Move from "interested" to "committed"

**At This Stage**:
- Faculty has agreed, in principle, to be lead PI
- You're developing research partnership agreement
- You're preparing to engage TTO

**Critical Conversation Topics**:

#### Topic 1: Defining Research Scope

**Start with**: "Let's nail down exactly what research we'd fund for 12 months."

**Frameworks**:
- **Research Objective**: What specific problem are we solving? (1 sentence)
- **Hypothesis**: What are we testing? (1 sentence)
- **Methodology**: How will we test it? (5-10 sentences)
- **Success Metric**: How do we know it worked? (1-3 measurable outcomes)
- **Deliverables**: What are tangible outputs? (papers? code? datasets? prototypes?)

**Example**:
```
Research Objective: Validate AI algorithm for drug screening on pharmaceutical dataset

Hypothesis: ML-based candidate scoring will predict drug efficacy with >85% accuracy
on held-out test set of 5,000 compounds

Methodology: Conduct wet lab screening of top 200 AI-predicted compounds; compare
actual results to model predictions; iterate on model based on discrepancies

Success Metrics: >85% prediction accuracy; identify 10+ novel high-potential candidates
not predicted by traditional screening

Deliverables:
- 1 peer-reviewed publication
- 2-3 patent applications
- Dataset of 5,000 screened compounds + results
- Optimized algorithm suitable for commercialization
```

**Negotiation Tactic**: Frame research so it naturally serves both purposes:
1. Faculty's research interests (science, publications, student training)
2. Your company's commercialization needs (validation, proof-of-concept, IP)

If you can show how research serves both goals equally, faculty buys in completely.

#### Topic 2: Budget Structure and Negotiation

**Approach**: "Let's build a realistic budget for this research."

**Budget Components to Discuss**:
1. **Faculty Time**: "What's your % time on this?"
   - Typical: 10-20% (not 50%+; faculty need time for other responsibilities)
   - Translate to salary: If faculty is $120K/year, 20% = $24K
   - Negotiate: "Would 15% of your time work?" (More achievable)

2. **Team Composition**: "Who else would be on the team?"
   - Postdoc (if needed): $70K-$80K/year
   - Graduate students (# depends on scope): $40K-$50K/year each
   - Undergraduate research assistants: $15K-$25K/year
   - Lab manager (if large project): $50K-$60K/year

3. **Equipment & Supplies**: "What equipment do you need?"
   - Often inflated in initial estimates
   - Negotiate: "Can you use existing lab equipment?"
   - Differentiate: Essential vs. Nice-to-have
   - Budget: $30K-$100K depending on field (biotech = higher)

4. **Travel & Dissemination**: "How much for conferences and collaboration travel?"
   - Typical: $10K-$20K
   - Negotiate: Limit to necessary conferences (not 4+ per year)

5. **Indirect Costs (F&A)**: "What's the university's indirect rate?"
   - Typically 35-50% of total budget
   - This is university cut; non-negotiable typically
   - Exception: Agencies sometimes allow 15-25% reduction for startup partnerships

**Budget Negotiation Strategy**:

1. **Start with faculty's wish list**: "What would the ideal budget be?"
   - Faculty will overestimate (safe approach)
   - Typical: 2x what you'd actually need

2. **Establish constraints**: "Let me check what NSF SBIR Phase I typically funds."
   - NSF SBIR Phase I max: $275K (includes indirect costs)
   - After indirect (40%), direct costs: ~$165K
   - After faculty overhead, actual research budget: ~$100K-$120K

3. **Problem-solve together**: "Given NSF limits, what's most critical to fund?"
   - Faculty often identifies what's essential vs. nice-to-have
   - Respect their expertise
   - Build budget from essentials up

4. **Propose cost-sharing**: "Could your company contribute equipment or in-kind support?"
   - Agencies favor matching funds (shows commitment)
   - You provide equipment, materials, or personnel time
   - Reduces burden on grant budget; increases approval odds

**Example Budget Negotiation**:

Faculty says: "I need $500K to do this right."
You respond: "That's helpful context. NSF SBIR Phase I caps at $275K total. Given that constraint, what's most critical?"
Faculty replies: "Well, absolutely need the postdoc and graduate student. Need some equipment for testing."
You counter: "Perfect. What if we cover equipment from company resources, and you focus grant funding on personnel? That gives us $200K+ for your team."
Faculty: "That could work. We'd need $60K postdoc, $45K grad student, supplies... maybe $150K total direct costs. Plus indirect, that's ~$250K, right in range."
You: "Exactly. And my team will provide testing equipment and access to our data—that's our cost-share."

**Result**: Budget defined, realistic, and lower than original ask.

#### Topic 3: IP and Commercialization Rights

**This is where many partnerships fail. Be explicit.**

**Conversation Starter**: "Let's make sure we're aligned on IP from the start, since that can become complicated."

**Key Questions to Answer**:
1. **What IP existed before partnership?** (Pre-existing IP)
   - Your company retains all your pre-existing IP
   - University retains all its pre-existing IP
   - Clear ownership = smooth process

2. **Who owns IP developed during partnership?** (New IP)
   - **Option A** (Most common): University owns; company exclusively licenses
   - **Option B** (Rare): Company owns; university gets non-exclusive license
   - **Option C** (Complex): Joint ownership
   - **Recommendation**: Option A (standard, lower friction)

3. **What does "exclusive license" mean?**
   - Company has exclusive right to commercialize
   - University can't license to competitors
   - University retains right to use for research
   - Field of use might be limited (e.g., "pharmaceutical industry" not "all industries")

4. **Royalties?**
   - University typically gets 3-7% of net sales
   - Lower for early-stage companies (5% typical)
   - Only triggered when company commercializes and generates revenue
   - Not upfront cost

5. **Patent prosecution costs?**
   - Typically: Company pays for filing and prosecution
   - Company chooses counsel (with university review)
   - Cost: $2K-$5K per patent to file

6. **Publication and confidentiality?**
   - Typical: 60-day delay before publication (to file patents)
   - Company can request trade secret protection
   - Academic culture values publication; balance is key

**Negotiation Tactic - Frame as Win-Win**:

"Here's why this structure works well: The university retains IP ownership, so from a compliance perspective, everything flows through your TTO normally. We get exclusive commercial rights, which is what we need to build a company. When we commercialize and generate revenue, you get royalties. And if we don't commercialize, the university still owns patents and can pursue other opportunities. Everyone's incentivized correctly."

**Red Flags to Watch**:
- Faculty wanting company to own all IP (suggests misaligned incentives)
- Faculty wanting non-exclusive license to competitors (defeats purpose of partnership)
- Faculty wanting high royalty rates (>7%) upfront (unsustainable for startup)
- Faculty unwilling to delay publication (suggests lower commercialization commitment)

## Part 3: Technology Transfer Office (TTO) Negotiations

### Understanding TTO Dynamics

TTOs are institutional gatekeepers. You need their approval.

**Their Constraints**:
- Fiduciary duty to university (maximize long-term IP value)
- Regulatory compliance (federal funding rules, IP disclosure)
- Standardized processes (apply to all partnerships, not just yours)
- Risk aversion (don't want deals that blow up)

**What TTOs Want**:
- Clear IP ownership (prevents future disputes)
- Standard terms (reduces negotiation/legal work)
- Realistic commercialization timelines
- Financial upside (royalties, milestone payments)
- Academic freedom (publication, research independence)

**What TTOs Resist**:
- Company ownership of IP (threatens university's role)
- Unreasonably short exclusivity periods (<7 years)
- High-risk commercialization plans
- Exclusive territory limitations (prefer worldwide)
- Complex equity arrangements

---

### The TTO Engagement Sequence

#### Step 1: Identify the Right TTO Contact (Week 1)

**Goal**: Find the TTO manager who handles your research area.

**Approach**:
- Search university website for "Technology Licensing" or "Innovation Office"
- Call main number, ask: "Who handles [your industry] licensing agreements?"
- Get name, email, phone
- Alternative: Ask faculty to introduce you

**Initial Email to TTO**:

```
Subject: Introduction – Research Partnership Opportunity

Dear [TTO Director],

[Faculty Name] and I are exploring a research partnership between [University]
and [Company Name] in [research area].

I would like to schedule a brief call to discuss how [University] typically
structures research partnerships with industry, and what IP framework would
work for your office.

The partnership would be structured as: [brief description]

Could we find 15-20 minutes next week?

Best regards,
[Name]
[Company]
[Phone/Email]
```

**Tone**: Professional, respectful, brief. You're approaching them as institutional stakeholder, not vendor.

#### Step 2: Initial TTO Meeting (Week 2-3)

**Objective**: Understand TTO's standard approach; identify points of flexibility.

**Preparation**:
- Review university's standard IP agreement (if available online)
- Prepare 1-page partnership summary
- List 3-5 specific questions for TTO
- Bring faculty member if possible (their presence legitimizes request)

**Meeting Agenda** (20 minutes):

1. **Introduction** (2 min)
   - "Thanks for making time. We're excited about partnering with [Faculty]"

2. **Partnership Overview** (5 min)
   - Describe research focus
   - Timeline (12-24 months)
   - Funding source (NSF SBIR, NIH SBIR, etc.)
   - Budget range ($300K-$500K example)

3. **TTO Questions** (10 min)
   - "What does [University]'s standard research partnership agreement look like?"
   - "What's your typical IP ownership structure?"
   - "Are there any flexibility points based on startup status?"
   - "What's the timeline for TTO approval of agreements?"
   - "Who needs to sign off (faculty, department chair, provost)?"

4. **Next Steps** (3 min)
   - Confirm TTO will send standard agreement template
   - Set timeline for review and feedback
   - Identify any concerns TTO has

**Critical Tactic**: Listen more than talk.
- TTO will explain their process
- Take notes on their constraints
- Don't negotiate in this meeting
- Just gather information

**Post-Meeting Actions**:
- Send thank-you email within 1 hour
- Recap what you heard (confirm understanding)
- Request template agreement
- Ask for timeline for TTO approval (target: 2-3 weeks)

---

#### Step 3: Review Standard Agreement and Propose Changes (Week 3-5)

**After TTO sends template agreement**:

1. **Have startup counsel review** ($2K-$5K investment, worth it)
2. **Identify must-haves vs. nice-to-haves**:
   - **Must-have**: Exclusive commercialization license
   - **Must-have**: 7+ year exclusive period
   - **Must-have**: Company right to sublicense to partners
   - **Nice-to-have**: Reduced royalty rate (try for 5% vs. 6%)
   - **Nice-to-have**: Limited field of use (negotiable, often not core issue)

3. **Prepare redline document** (track changes)
   - Use Word "Track Changes" or Google Docs suggestions
   - Only change what's necessary
   - Explain rationale for each change

4. **Email TTO with redlines**:

```
Subject: Research Partnership Agreement – Comments and Proposed Revisions

Dear [TTO Contact],

Thank you for sending the standard agreement template. We've reviewed it with
our counsel and find most terms very reasonable.

We've proposed revisions in the attached redline document (changes tracked).
The main items:

1. Exclusive license term: We propose 7 years (vs. 5-year standard). Given
startup commercialization timeline, this provides more certainty for R&D investment.

2. Royalty rate: Given our early-stage status, we propose 5% net sales
(vs. 6% standard). This reflects typical startup-university agreements and
leaves capital for continued development.

3. Sublicense rights: We propose ability to sublicense with university approval.
Strategic partnerships may require this for market development.

All other terms are acceptable as written. We're happy to discuss these three
points on a call if helpful.

What's your availability for a brief call next week?

Best regards,
[Name]
```

**Key Tactic**: Frame changes as industry-standard for startup partnerships, not as special asks.

---

#### Step 4: TTO Negotiation Call (Week 5-6)

**Objective**: Resolve redlines and get TTO agreement in principle.

**Pre-Call Strategy**:
- Rank your requests (exclusive license > royalty > sublicense)
- Prepare to concede on lower-priority items
- Have fallback positions ready

**Call Structure** (30 minutes):

1. **Opening** (2 min):
   - "Thanks for reviewing our redlines. We appreciate your time."

2. **Walk Through Redlines** (20 min):
   - Start with least contentious item
   - Move to more contentious
   - Listen to TTO's concerns on each
   - Negotiate one item at a time

3. **Example Negotiation**:
   - **You**: "We proposed 7-year exclusive term vs. your 5-year standard."
   - **TTO**: "5 years is our standard. We worry about blocking other opportunities."
   - **You**: "Understood. How about 7 years exclusive, then non-exclusive after (or university can license competitors)?"
   - **TTO**: "That's workable. We can do exclusive for 7, non-exclusive for 3 after."
   - **You**: "Perfect. That works for us." [Move to next item]

4. **Closing** (5 min):
   - Summarize agreements reached
   - Identify any remaining open items
   - Next step: "We'll have counsel prepare clean version reflecting our agreements."
   - Timeline: "Can you turn around review in 1-2 weeks?"

**Red Flag - If TTO Won't Move on Core Terms**:
- Exclusive license is non-negotiable (you must have this)
- If TTO insists on non-exclusive license, consider different university
- Escalation path: Ask to meet with TTO director or university counsel if contact won't budge

**Signs of TTO Openness**:
- Acknowledges startup context (understands early-stage constraints)
- Willing to consider terms variant from standard
- Responsive to amendments and calls
- Suggests compromises (split-the-difference approach)

---

#### Step 5: Final Agreement Execution (Week 6-7)

**Once TTO Agrees in Principle**:
1. Counsel prepares clean document with all amendments
2. TTO reviews clean document (usually 5-7 days)
3. Both parties sign (typically: Company CEO, Faculty Member, TTO Director)
4. Executed agreement goes to university legal for counter-signature if needed
5. Final signed copy goes to all parties

**Timeline**: Figure 3-5 business days from agreed clean document to fully executed.

## Part 4: Advanced Negotiation Tactics

### Tactic 1: Leverage Competition

**Situation**: Faculty has multiple companies interested in partnership.

**Your Approach**:
- Don't ignore it; acknowledge it
- "I know other companies have reached out. Here's why we're the right fit: [specific reasons]"
- Show commitment: "We're willing to fund X additional research areas if it helps your program"
- Speed: Move faster than competitors (submit proposal first, show follow-through)

**Example**: Two biotech companies want to partner with same Stanford professor for drug discovery research.
- Company A: Waits; proposes complex partnership with 3 universities
- Company B: You; propose simple 12-month NSF SBIR with Stanford, moving quickly
- Result: Professor picks Company B because momentum and clarity beat complexity

---

### Tactic 2: Highlight Non-Monetary Benefits to Faculty

**Problem**: Faculty focused on money/time commitment.

**Solution**: Emphasize non-monetary benefits.

**Examples**:
- "Your students will have access to industry data most researchers never see"
- "This research could support 2-3 high-impact publications in [Journal]"
- "Your group will be cited in our product marketing—industry recognition"
- "We can facilitate introductions to [other companies/researchers] working on related problems"
- "This positions you for larger follow-on grants from [Agency] in year 2-3"

**Key Point**: Faculty care about career impact and student opportunities. Emphasize those, not just money.

---

### Tactic 3: Escalation Path

**Situation**: Faculty or TTO is inflexible on non-negotiable terms.

**Escalation Sequence**:
1. **Faculty resists**: Ask "What would need to happen for you to see this differently?" Often there's underlying concern you can address.
2. **Faculty won't move**: Ask faculty to introduce you to department chair or grad director. Often they have broader perspective.
3. **TTO won't move**: Ask for meeting with TTO director (not just associate). Director often more flexible and forward-thinking.
4. **Director won't move**: Consider different institution. Not all partnerships are worth pursuing.

**Red Flag**: If you're escalating frequently, partnership likely isn't right fit. Better to find aligned partner than force misaligned one.

---

### Tactic 4: The "Trial Period" Proposal

**Situation**: Faculty uncertain about partnership; TTO wants proof of concept.

**Your Offer**:
"What if we start with a small pilot research agreement (3-6 months, $50K-$75K) to prove out the partnership? If it goes well, we expand to Phase I grant in next cycle. This reduces risk for everyone."

**Benefits**:
- Lower commitment for faculty (easier to say yes)
- Proves execution capability
- TTO sees success before larger IP/legal work
- Often Phase I funding approved for Phase II if Phase I succeeds

---

### Tactic 5: The "Faculty-Founder" Option

**Situation**: Exceptional faculty interested in commercialization.

**Your Pitch**:
"Rather than research partnership, would you consider co-founder role? 10% equity, part-time during initial research phase."

**Advantages**:
- Faculty incentivized (personal upside)
- Cleaner IP (founder owns, not separate agreements)
- Stronger university support (university has equity stake)
- Faster decision-making (faculty is insider)

**Downsides**:
- Complex (faculty employment, leave of absence, etc.)
- Not for all faculty
- Requires deeper commitment

**Best Used**: When faculty is exceptional and already thinking about commercialization.

## Part 5: Red Flags and When to Walk Away

### Faculty Red Flags

| Red Flag | What It Means | Action |
|----------|---------------|--------|
| Doesn't respond to emails | Low engagement; won't prioritize | Move on; don't pursue |
| No publications in 5 years | Might not be active researcher | Move on |
| "I'll need to clear with my TTO" on every question | TTO has tight control; slow decision | Acceptable (expected); just manage expectations |
| "I own my IP separately" | Conflicts with university IP policies; complexity | Escalate to TTO; might still work |
| Wants 50%+ time commitment | Unrealistic for faculty with other obligations | Negotiate down to 15-20% |
| Wants large upfront payment (equity in company) | Misaligned incentives; thinks you're well-funded | Clarify you're pre-revenue startup; renegotiate |
| Won't discuss IP framework | Suggests vague thinking about partnership | Red flag; clarity needed |
| No grant experience | Slow grant development process; requires hand-holding | Acceptable if you manage it; plan 2-3 weeks extra |

### TTO Red Flags

| Red Flag | What It Means | Action |
|----------|---------------|--------|
| "We require company ownership of IP" | Unusual; suggests TTO doesn't understand startups | Escalate; very few startups can do this |
| "Non-exclusive license only" | Defeats purpose of partnership | Consider different institution |
| "20%+ royalty rate" | Unsustainable for startup; shows unrealistic view | Escalate; this is not market standard |
| "5-year exclusive then university can shop IP" | Higher risk; suggests TTO doesn't trust partnership | Negotiate; try for non-exclusive after exclusivity |
| Months-long negotiation on simple terms | Process bottleneck; suggests inflexibility | Try to escalate to director |
| "We need equity in your company" | Not typical; suggests TTO views itself as investor | Unusual; discuss carefully; most TTOs avoid this |

**Walking Away**: If you see 2+ red flags, seriously consider different faculty/university partnership.

## Part 6: Negotiation Template Language

### Email: Proposing Partnership to Faculty

```
Subject: Research Partnership Opportunity in [Area]

Dear Dr. [Name],

I've been following your work on [specific paper/topic], and I believe your
research in [specific area] is directly relevant to [your technology area].

[Company Name] is developing [one-sentence technology description]. Specifically,
your insight about [faculty's key finding] aligns with a core challenge we're
solving: [your problem].

**What I'm Proposing**: A research partnership where you lead a 12-month research
project on [research area]. This would be funded through an [NSF SBIR / NIH SBIR /
DOE ARPA-E] grant. Your lab would own the research; we'd get an exclusive
commercial license for the results.

**Why This Makes Sense**:
- Your expertise in [area] is essential to validate our approach
- The research advances your agenda in [specific direction]
- Funding would support your grad students and postdocs
- Results would generate publications in [relevant journals]

Would you be open to exploring this? I'm happy to schedule a brief call to discuss
further. No pressure—I understand you have competing priorities.

Best regards,
[Name]
[Company]
```

```
**Partnership Overview**:

1. What's your standard IP ownership structure for research partnerships?

I'd like to schedule a 20-minute call next week to discuss. Happy to work around
your schedule.

Best regards,
[Name]
[Company]
```
