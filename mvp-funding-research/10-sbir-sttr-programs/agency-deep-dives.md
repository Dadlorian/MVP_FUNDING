# Agency-Specific SBIR/STTR Program Deep Dives

## Overview
Each federal agency administers SBIR/STTR programs with distinct priorities, submission processes, funding levels, and technical focus areas. This guide provides detailed strategies for succeeding with DOD, NIH, NSF, and DOE—the four largest SBIR funders.

---

## Part 1: Department of Defense (DoD) SBIR

### Program Overview
- **Total Annual Funding**: $2.2B+ (largest SBIR funder)
- **Success Rate**: 18-22% for Phase I
- **Phase I Funding**: $175K-200K (varies by service)
- **Phase II Funding**: $1.0M-1.5M (24-month program)
- **Phase III Funding**: $10M+ (commercialization and production)
- **Military Departments**: Air Force, Army, Navy, Marines

### Agency Philosophy
DoD SBIR is technology-focused, mission-driven, and operationally pragmatic. They fund innovations that solve real military needs and can transition to use within 3-5 years. DoD values:
- **Operational relevance**: Will this solve an actual soldier/sailor problem?
- **Technical maturity**: Ready for rapid prototyping and testing
- **Manufacturability**: Can it be produced at scale in US?
- **Phase III transition**: Clear path to government contracts or license to prime contractors

### Key Solicitation Guidance

**DoD SBIR Solicitations** (published Spring and Fall each year):
- Air Force: [AFSBIR.com](https://afsbir.com/)
- Army: [Army SBIR Portal](https://sbir.army.mil/)
- Navy: [Navy SBIR Portal](https://www.navysbir.navy.mil/)
- Strategic: [DefenseURG](https://sbir.defenseurge.org/) - Cross-military topics

**Typical Topics** (30-50 per solicitation):
- Autonomous systems and robotics
- Cyber security and information warfare
- Directed energy weapons
- Armor and materials science
- AI and machine learning for defense
- Sustainable energy/power systems
- Medical and field health technologies
- Supply chain optimization
- Human performance enhancement

### Success Strategy for DoD SBIR

#### 1. Find the Right Topic Match
**Critical**: Choose topic with highest relevance to your technology

- Read topic descriptions carefully (not just titles)
- Look at "deliverables" section—what does DoD actually want?
- Review prior year winners in your domain (SBA database)
- Email topic author (PM listed in solicitation) before writing

**Example Email to PM**:
```
Subject: SBIR Topic [X.1.03] - Technical Feasibility Question

Dear [PM Name],

We're exploring an SBIR proposal for Topic X.1.03 (Edge Computing for
Tactical Networks). Our solution uses [brief tech description].

Quick questions to ensure fit:
1. Would you envision this running on existing military platforms?
2. What's the priority for battery life vs. computational power?

Looking forward to potentially proposing in this area.

Best,
[Your Name]
```

**Why**: PMs appreciate engagement, often provide guidance, and remember applicants who asked good questions.

#### 2. Understand Phase III Transition Path
**This is what DoD cares most about**

- What military program would use your technology?
- Which prime contractor might integrate it?
- What are existing contract vehicles (JEDI, IDIQ, etc.)?
- Is there a Phase II contract already pending?

**Example Phase III pathway**:
> "Phase III: Technology will be integrated into Raytheon's air defense radar system
> (program valued at $8.5B annually). Raytheon has indicated interest in Phase III
> partnership for integration and production. Production volume: 50-100 units annually
> at $500K per system = $25M+ annual market."

#### 3. Demonstrate Manufacturing Readiness
DoD cares about whether you can actually produce at scale.

**Include in proposal**:
- Supply chain analysis (especially for rare materials)
- Manufacturing process (not just design)
- Quality assurance plan
- Timeline to production (showing understanding of QA process)
- Cost modeling for production volumes

**Example**:
> "Manufacturing readiness: GaN die fabricated by supplier (Transphorm, confirmed
> capacity for 10K units annually). Hybrid circuit assembly performed by Sanmina-SCI
> (ISO 9001 certified, aerospace qualified). Full environmental testing by TRL7 end
> of Phase II (thermal shock, vibration per MIL-STD-810H)"

#### 4. Address ITAR Considerations
**Critical for defense contractors and weapons systems**

ITAR (International Traffic in Arms Regulations) controls export of defense-related technology.

**Questions to answer**:
- Will your technology be subject to ITAR?
- If yes, do you have ITAR facility and procedures?
- Can you hire foreign nationals? (Usually not for ITAR work)
- Are any components/materials ITAR-controlled?

**If ITAR uncertainty**:
- Email to DoD PM: "Is [technology area] likely ITAR-controlled?"
- Contact ITAR compliance lawyer ($1K-3K)
- Don't assume—incorrect ITAR classification can kill deal

**Strategy**:
- If ITAR-free, emphasize it (commercial advantage)
- If ITAR-subject, have procedures in place (shows sophistication)
- Factor ITAR costs into budget if applicable

#### 5. Emphasize TRL (Technology Readiness Level)
DoD thinks in terms of TRL (1-9 scale):
- **TRL 1-3**: Basic research
- **TRL 4-6**: Development and testing
- **TRL 7-8**: Prototype ready for field testing
- **TRL 9**: Production deployment

**Strategy**:
- Start at lowest realistic TRL in Phase I (demonstrates honesty)
- Target Phase I exit at TRL 5-6
- Plan Phase II to reach TRL 7-8
- Phase III bridges to TRL 9

**Example**: "Current TRL: 4 (prototype in lab). Phase I goal: TRL 5 (prototype with
representative components). Phase II goal: TRL 7 (field-ready prototype tested by
military users)."

#### 6. Build Team with Military Expertise
**Strongest advantage**: Prior DoD program experience

- If no prior DoD experience, hire consultant or advisor
- Prior government workers with security clearance are valuable
- Prime contractor relationships crucial (they're your Phase III partner)

**Example advisor language**:
> "Dr. [Name], retired Air Force Major with 20 years in [specific domain], joined
> as technical advisor. He ensures our development aligns with operational needs and
> identifies integration pathways within existing USAF systems."

#### 7. Craft Proposal for DoD Reviewers
**DoD reviewers are often government program managers—pragmatists, not academics**

**Writing approach**:
- Start with problem statement (military pain point)
- Show why existing solutions fall short (operational gap)
- Explain how your solution addresses specific gap
- Include military user feedback/quotes where possible
- End with Phase III transition plan

**Avoid**:
- Heavy theoretical foundations (DoD doesn't fund basic research)
- Overly ambitious R&D (too long to transition)
- Unclear transition path (Phase III is make-or-break)

#### 8. Leverage Prime Contractor Relationships

**Prime contractors** (Lockheed, Raytheon, Boeing, General Dynamics, etc.) often license or acquire SBIR technologies.

**Strategy**:
- Research which primes work on relevant programs
- Contact their innovation or business development teams
- Get letters of interest for Phase III
- Make prime contractor your Phase III anchor customer

**Email to prime contractor**:
```
Subject: SBIR Technology Opportunity - [Technology Area]

Dear [Business Development Contact],

We're developing [brief tech description] to address [military need].
This is directly applicable to [specific program you researched].

We're pursuing SBIR funding and would like to discuss potential Phase III
partnership where [Company] could integrate this into [specific program].

Would you be open to a brief conversation?

Best,
[Your Name]
```

### DoD SBIR Phase Timelines and Milestones

**Phase I** (6 months, ~$175K):
- Month 1: Requirements definition and test plan
- Months 2-4: Core R&D and experimentation
- Months 5-6: Prototype demonstration and risk assessment
- Deliverable: Phase I final report + prototype

**Phase II** (24 months, ~$1.0M):
- Year 1: Full development, prototype maturation
- Year 2: Field testing, manufacturability assessment
- Quarterly progress reviews (government monitors closely)
- Deliverables: Quarterly reports + final prototype

**Phase III** (indefinite, $10M+):
- Government contracts or prime contractor licensing
- Production and deployment
- Continuous improvement based on operational feedback

### Common DoD SBIR Mistakes
1. **No Phase III plan** → Rejected immediately
2. **TRL misalignment** → "This is too early stage for DoD"
3. **Prime contractor cold shoulder** → No industry credibility
4. **No military user input** → Doesn't solve real problem
5. **ITAR confusion** → Disqualified if handled incorrectly
6. **Manufacturing timeline underestimated** → Unrealistic
7. **Unclear problem statement** → Hard to evaluate relevance

## Part 2: National Institutes of Health (NIH) SBIR

### Program Overview
- **Total Annual Funding**: $1.2B+ (second-largest SBIR funder)
- **Success Rate**: 10-15% for Phase I (most competitive)
- **Phase I Funding**: $50K-225K (varies by institute)
- **Phase II Funding**: $750K-1.5M (24-month program)
- **Participating Institutes**: 27 (NCI, NHLBI, NINDS, NIAID, etc.)
- **Research Areas**: All biomedical and health research topics

### Agency Philosophy
NIH SBIR is innovation-driven and mission-aligned with institute focus areas. They fund high-risk, high-impact innovations with strong scientific foundation. NIH values:
- **Scientific innovation**: Novel approach to health problem
- **Unmet medical need**: Addresses gap in current treatments/diagnostics
- **Feasibility**: Sound science and realistic plan
- **Commercial potential**: Credible path to healthcare market
- **Team expertise**: Strong scientific credentials

### Institutes and Focus Areas

**Major NIH SBIR funders** (pick best match for your technology):

| Institute | Focus | Example Technologies |
|-----------|-------|---------------------|
| NCI | Cancer | Diagnostics, targeted therapies, immunotherapy |
| NHLBI | Heart/Lung | Cardiovascular devices, heart failure monitoring |
| NINDS | Neurological | Neurostimulation, brain imaging, Alzheimer's research |
| NIAID | Infectious Disease | Diagnostics, vaccines, antimicrobial resistance |
| NIBIB | Biomedical Engineering | Imaging, robotics, biosensors |
| NIDDK | Diabetes/Kidney | Glucose monitoring, dialysis alternatives |
| NEI | Eye | Vision restoration, retinal implants |
| NICHD | Child Health | Pediatric diagnostics, maternal health |

**Strategy**: Match technology to institute with most relevant disease focus. Small institute = less competition (NIDCD, NCCIH).

### Key Solicitation Guidance

**NIH SBIR Solicitation Structure**:
- **Parent R43/R44 SBIR**: Open to all health-related innovations
- **Institute-Specific Funding Opportunity Announcements** (FOAs): Targeted opportunities
- **Program Announcements**: Specific research priorities (precision medicine, AI/ML, etc.)

**Finding best opportunity**:
1. Visit [NIH SBIR website](https://sbir.nih.gov/)
2. Search by disease area or technology type
3. Check institute priorities (5-year plans on institute websites)
4. Email program officer at target institute (they're helpful)

### Success Strategy for NIH SBIR

#### 1. Ground in Unmet Medical Need
**This is paramount for NIH**

- What medical problem are you solving?
- How many patients affected annually?
- What's the current standard of care?
- Why is current care inadequate (cost, efficacy, safety)?
- What would improvement mean for patients?

**Example Problem Statement**:
> "Type 2 diabetes affects 37 million Americans. Current CGM systems cost $2-5K annually,
> with 50% accuracy in glucose readings <140 mg/dL. Patients often make incorrect insulin
> decisions, leading to hypoglycemia (500K emergency room visits/year, $5.3B annual cost).
> We propose a non-enzymatic glucose sensor with 95% accuracy and <$500 annual cost,
> reducing hypoglycemia events by 40%."

#### 2. Demonstrate Scientific Rigor
NIH reviewers are scientists—they scrutinize methodology.

**Proposal must include**:
- Preliminary data showing feasibility
- Specific aims (clear, measurable objectives)
- Research design and methods section
- Expected outcomes and milestones
- Literature citations (20-50 relevant papers)
- Risk assessment and alternative approaches

**Preliminary Data Strategy**:
- If possible, fund initial research before SBIR proposal
- Show lab results proving concept
- Demonstrate technical approach works (at least partially)
- Include figures showing results
- Include founders' publications (if relevant)

#### 3. Leverage Investigator Credentials
**Strong scientific team = higher success**

**Team components**:
- **CEO/PI**: Ideally with PhD and relevant research background
- **Scientific Co-Founder**: If available
- **Scientific Advisors**: 1-3 respected researchers in field
  - May be from academic institutions
  - Should have published in relevant domain
  - NIH grant funding is plus (shows they know process)
- **Medical/Clinical Advisors**: If developing medical device/drug

**Advisor engagement**:
- Document in proposal: "Dr. X committed [specific contribution]"
- Include advisor CVs (2-3 page summary showing expertise)
- Letters of support from advisors confirming commitment
- Realistic effort allocation (not honorary advisors)

**Example**:
> "Dr. Jane Smith, Chief of Cardiology at [Hospital], committed 5% effort to Phase I.
> She will provide patient recruitment strategy, clinical feedback on prototype, and
> guidance on regulatory pathway. Dr. Smith has published 47 papers on heart failure
> diagnosis and served on NIH study section for NHLBI grants."

#### 4. Address Regulatory Pathway
**If medical device or drug: Discuss FDA pathway**

- Which category (501(k), PMA, or drug classification)?
- What studies needed for approval?
- Timeline to regulatory approval?
- What Phase II is designed to accomplish toward approval?

**Example**:
> "Our glucose sensor is a Class II medical device. Regulatory pathway:
> Phase I - preliminary sensor validation (10 patient samples). Phase II -
> prospective multicenter study (100 patients, demonstrating non-inferiority to
> existing CGM, supporting 510(k) submission). Target FDA clearance Month 30."

#### 5. Emphasize Commercialization Viability
**NIH wants technologies that reach patients**

- Market size and growth projection
- Reimbursement strategy (Medicare, insurance codes)
- Distribution pathway
- Competitor analysis
- Commercial timeline

**Example**:
> "Market opportunity: 8.5M type 2 diabetes patients on insulin worldwide. Total
> addressable market (TAM) at $500/patient/year = $4.25B annually. Reimbursement:
> CPT code [X] allows $50 per reading, insurance coverage expanding. Distribution:
> Partnership with [Company] reaches 50K endocrinologists. Year 3 revenue projection: $12M."

#### 6. Use Correct NIH Proposal Format
**NIH has strict format requirements**

- R43 (Phase I): 15 page proposal
- R44 (Phase II): 25 page proposal
- Specific sections required (Aims, Research Design, etc.)
- Font/spacing requirements strictly enforced
- Follow [NIH instructions](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/how-to-apply-application-guide.html)

**Common formatting mistakes**:
- Margins too small (not allowed)
- Font size too small (Courier 10-12, min)
- Page limits exceeded (auto-rejected)
- Missing required sections

**Strategy**: Use [NIH template](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/how-to-apply-application-guide.html), avoid customizing, test PDF carefully.

#### 7. Address Rigor and Feasibility Concerns
NIH reviewers often worry about feasibility.

**Proposal should include**:
- Preliminary data showing approach works
- Realistic timeline with contingencies
- Appropriate budget for work proposed
- Risk mitigation strategies
- Alternative approaches if primary fails

**Example Risk/Contingency**:
```
Risk: Glucose sensor selectivity (interference from other molecules)
Mitigation:
  1. In vitro testing with common interferents (weeks 1-4)
  2. If selectivity <95%: Test alternative coating materials (weeks 5-12)
  3. Worst case: Scale back sensor complexity, target <150 mg/dL range
```

#### 8. Build Relationships with NIH Program Officers
**NIH program officers are incredibly helpful**

**What to do**:
- Identify program officer for your institute/disease area
- Email 2-3 months before deadline
- Ask about current priorities and fit
- Request feedback on pre-proposal concept
- Send draft of specific aims for comments
- Program officer often has inside track on funding priorities

**Example**:
```
Subject: SBIR Pre-proposal Consultation Request

Dear Dr. [Name],

I'm developing an innovative [disease area] diagnostic technology and
exploring SBIR funding through the [Institute]. Would you be available
for a brief call to discuss fit with current NHLBI priorities?

I have a two-page concept summary that outlines our unmet need,
scientific approach, and commercial opportunity. Happy to send ahead
of call if helpful.

Looking forward to speaking.
```

**Why this works**: Program officers want to fund good ideas, will help shape proposal, often remember who pre-consulted.

### NIH SBIR Phase Timelines and Milestones

**Phase I** (6 months, $50K-225K):
- Establish feasibility of scientific/commercial innovation
- Preliminary data supporting key hypotheses
- Risk assessment and mitigation planning
- Regulatory pathway (if applicable) planning
- Deliverable: Final scientific report + publications plan

**Phase II** (24 months, $750K-1.5M):
- Full development and validation
- Clinical studies if applicable (FDA pathway)
- Prototype optimization
- Regulatory submissions as appropriate
- Quarterly or annual progress reports to NIH

**Phase IIB** (available for most institutes):
- Potential extension of Phase II for additional clinical work
- Up to $500K additional funding
- Used when Phase II shows promise but needs more validation
- Similar timeline to Phase II (12-24 months)

### Common NIH SBIR Mistakes
1. **No preliminary data** → "Feasibility unclear"
2. **Weak team credentials** → "Team not qualified for this work"
3. **Unclear unmet medical need** → "Not clear why this matters"
4. **Unrealistic timelines** → "Can't do these studies in Phase I"
5. **Poor regulatory understanding** → "FDA pathway vague"
6. **Overcomplicated technology** → "Likely to fail"
7. **Weak reimbursement model** → "Won't work commercially"

## Part 3: National Science Foundation (NSF) SBIR

### Program Overview
- **Total Annual Funding**: $200M+ (mixed with STTR)
- **Success Rate**: 15-20% for Phase I
- **Phase I Funding**: $50K-225K (varies by program)
- **Phase II Funding**: $750K-1.5M (24-month program)
- **Research Areas**: All non-medical science and engineering
- **Program Philosophy**: High-risk innovation with broad societal impact

### Agency Philosophy
NSF SBIR funds early-stage, high-risk innovations with potential for broad market impact. They value:
- **Technical merit**: Sound science and innovative approach
- **Societal impact**: Solves important problem or creates new market
- **Proof of concept**: Demonstrates feasibility
- **Scalability**: Technology can address large market
- **Commercialization readiness**: Clear path to market

### Technical Focus Areas

NSF organizes SBIR around research directorates. Choose best fit:

| Directorate | Example Technologies |
|-------------|---------------------|
| Engineering (ENG) | Advanced manufacturing, robotics, new materials, construction tech |
| Computer & Information Science (CISE) | AI/ML, cybersecurity, quantum computing, software |
| Geosciences (GEO) | Climate tech, environmental monitoring, geothermal |
| Biological Sciences (BIO) | Biotech tools, agricultural innovation, environmental biotech |
| Mathematical Sciences (MPS) | Materials science, nanotechnology, chemistry applications |
| Social, Behavioral, Economic Sciences (SBE) | Market research, workplace tech, education |

**Strategy**: NSF often funds "harder" technical problems than other agencies. If your technology is cutting-edge/risky, NSF may be good fit.

### Key Solicitation Guidance

**NSF SBIR Solicitations**:
- Published annually (typically January for Phase I/II, Summer for Phase II only)
- Available on [NSF SBIR website](https://www.nsf.gov/sbir/)
- More flexible topics than DoD/NIH (can propose own research)
- Can propose outside formal topics (but lower success rate)

**Two submission pathways**:
1. **Pre-screened topics**: Higher success rate (15-20%)
2. **General SBIR solicitation**: Lower success rate (8-12%), but more flexibility

**Strategy**: Submit to pre-screened topic if good fit exists (20-30% better odds).

### Success Strategy for NSF SBIR

#### 1. Choose Right Program Track
NSF has tiered SBIR program:

**Phase I**: $50-225K (6-12 months)
- Feasibility demonstration
- Technical proof of concept
- Market validation

**Phase II**: $750K-1.5M (24 months)
- Full development
- Prototype optimization
- Phase III preparation

**Phase IIB (Accelerator)**: Up to $1M additional, 12 months
- For companies ready to scale
- Requires Phase II background
- Greater commercial potential

**Strategy**:
- Start with Phase I to prove feasibility (lower risk)
- Phase II for full development
- Phase IIB if commercialization accelerating

#### 2. Ground in Technical Merit
**NSF reviewers are scientists/engineers**—focus on innovation

**Proposal must demonstrate**:
- Specific technical innovation (vs. engineering)
- Why existing approaches insufficient
- Novel approach with theoretical foundation
- Preliminary results supporting approach
- Clear technical milestones and metrics

**Example**:
> "Existing Li-ion batteries hit capacity ceiling due to fundamental limits of
> lithium chemistry. Our approach replaces lithium with sodium (10x more abundant),
> achieving 70% of Li-ion capacity at 20% cost. Preliminary results show 250 Wh/kg
> (vs. standard 150 Wh/kg). Phase I targets 300 Wh/kg prototype with 500+ cycle life."

#### 3. Demonstrate Market Opportunity
**NSF wants innovation addressing real market need**

- Market size and growth trends
- Current solutions and their limitations
- Why companies/consumers would adopt your solution
- Price/performance targets vs. alternatives
- Revenue model

**Example**:
> "Global sodium-ion battery market projected $10B by 2030. Target segments:
> stationary storage (solar/wind backup) and EVs in cost-sensitive markets (India,
> Southeast Asia). Addressable market (stationary): 500M kWh annually at $100/kWh = $50B market."

#### 4. Focus on High-Risk, High-Impact Innovation
**NSF specifically wants bold ideas, not incremental improvements**

- Is this transformational or incremental?
- Could this create new market category?
- Does this significantly outperform existing solutions?
- Is there significant technical risk (NSF expects this)?

**Avoid**: "We're making existing approach 10% better" (Too incremental)
**Aim for**: "We're using novel approach that could revolutionize [domain]" (Even if risky)

**Example**:
> "High risk: Early-stage sodium-ion chemistry with unproven cycle life. However,
> if successful, could eliminate cobalt/nickel supply constraints, reduce battery
> cost by 50%, and unlock massive emerging-market EV adoption. Only NSF-type
> organization funds this risk level."

#### 5. Build Strong Technical Team
**NSF values advanced technical credentials**

- PhDs in relevant field preferred (but not required)
- Peer-reviewed publications showing domain expertise
- Prior startup success or technical achievement
- Academic collaborators/advisors (boost credibility)

**Example bio**:
> "Dr. Michael Chen, Chief Technology Officer: PhD Electrochemistry (MIT),
> 15 publications in sodium-ion chemistry. Previously led battery R&D at [Company],
> developed cathode material achieving 10x charge speed. Co-author on two patents."

#### 6. Include Preliminary Data/Validation
**Essential for NSF funding**

- Lab results demonstrating feasibility
- Bench testing of prototype
- Simulation results
- Market research data
- Prototype demonstration

**Budget line item**: Set aside $5-10K in Phase I for external validation
- University collaboration testing
- Industry expert assessment
- Preliminary market research

#### 7. Propose Rigorous Evaluation Plan
**NSF wants clear metrics for success**

**Include**:
- Specific, measurable technical metrics
- Comparison benchmarks (vs. existing solutions)
- Testing and validation methodology
- Go/no-go decision points
- Risk mitigation testing

**Example Evaluation Plan**:
```
Objective 1: Demonstrate 300 Wh/kg energy density
  Metric: Actual cell testing at 3 independent lab
  Target: Achieve 300±10 Wh/kg
  Risk: If <280 Wh/kg, pivot to alternative cathode material

Objective 2: Demonstrate 500+ cycle life
  Metric: Accelerated life testing per NREL protocol
  Target: 500+ cycles at 80% capacity retention
  Risk: If <400 cycles, investigate aging mechanisms

Objective 3: Demonstrate cost viability
  Metric: Cost modeling with supplier quotes
  Target: <$80/kWh at volume (100K units/year)
  Risk: If cost projections >$100/kWh, revisit material sourcing
```

#### 8. Address Commercial Pathway Clearly
**NSF wants to see path from lab to market**

- Manufacturing strategy (who produces at scale?)
- Supply chain for key materials
- Timeline to commercialization
- Potential Phase III/follow-on funding
- Regulatory requirements (if applicable)

**Example**:
> "Manufacturing: Partner with [Battery Company] (confirmed interest, LOI attached)
> for production in Phase II. Scaled production: Existing Gigafactory in [Location]
> capable of 1 GWh annually by 2026. Time to market: Phase II completion Q4 2025,
> market introduction 2026, first $10M revenue in 2028."

### NSF SBIR Phase Timelines and Milestones

**Phase I** (12 months, $225K):
- Establish technical and commercial feasibility
- Develop preliminary prototype or proof of concept
- Validate market opportunity
- Plan for Phase II development
- Deliverable: Phase I final report with metrics

**Phase II** (24 months, $750K-1.5M):
- Full development toward commercializable product
- Prototype optimization and testing
- Market validation and customer feedback
- Manufacturing pathway planning
- Quarterly progress reports

**Phase IIB** (12 months, up to $1M additional):
- Accelerated path to commercialization
- Earlier Phase II exit may qualify
- Focuses on scaling and market entry
- Requires strong Phase II progress

### NSF SBIR Reviewer Expectations
- **Team capability**: "Can this team execute?"
- **Technical innovation**: "Is this truly novel/risky?"
- **Feasibility**: "Will this work?"
- **Market opportunity**: "Is there commercial potential?"
- **Evaluation plan**: "How will they know if successful?"

### Common NSF SBIR Mistakes
1. **Incremental innovation** → "Not sufficiently novel"
2. **Weak team credentials** → "Team not qualified"
3. **Unclear evaluation plan** → "How will success be measured?"
4. **No preliminary data** → "Feasibility unclear"
5. **Weak market analysis** → "Commercial potential questionable"
6. **Too much market focus, insufficient innovation** → NSF wants both, but tips toward innovation if trade-off needed
7. **Overly complex proposal** → "Unclear if achievable"

## Part 4: Department of Energy (DOE) SBIR

### Program Overview
- **Total Annual Funding**: $200M+ (mixed with STTR)
- **Success Rate**: 15-20% for Phase I
- **Phase I Funding**: $100K-225K (varies by program office)
- **Phase II Funding**: $750K-1.5M (24-month program)
- **Research Areas**: Energy, climate, advanced manufacturing, grid technology
- **Program Philosophy**: Accelerate energy innovation toward commercial deployment

### Agency Philosophy
DoE SBIR funds high-impact energy innovations with clear commercialization pathway. They value:
- **Climate impact**: How much CO2 reduction or energy efficiency?
- **Commercial viability**: Market demand exists
- **Manufacturability**: Can scale to meet market need
- **Technical innovation**: Novel approach to energy problem
- **Team strength**: Proven execution capability

### DoE Program Offices (Choose Best Fit)

**Major funders**:
- **Solar Energy**: Photovoltaic and concentrated solar
- **Wind Energy**: Wind turbine components and systems
- **Energy Efficiency**: Building, industrial, transportation efficiency
- **Geothermal**: Geothermal energy exploration and use
- **Hydrogen and Fuel Cells**: Clean hydrogen production and fuel cells
- **Grid Modernization**: Energy storage, smart grid, resilience
- **Advanced Manufacturing**: Industrial process innovation
- **Sustainable Transportation**: EVs, batteries, alternative fuels

### Key Solicitation Guidance

**DoE SBIR Solicitations**:
- Published annually (typically fall for next year's awards)
- Available on [DoE SBIR website](https://www.energy.gov/sbir/)
- Organized by program office with specific technical areas
- Often research topics, not technology-agnostic

**Research areas rotate**: Review past solicitations to predict future areas

### Success Strategy for DoE SBIR

#### 1. Quantify Energy/Climate Impact
**This is what DoE cares about most**

- How much CO2 reduction? (tons/year?)
- How much energy saved? (MWh/year?)
- Can you model the impact?
- Scale to national deployment (what's potential?)

**Example**:
> "Current industrial furnace efficiency: 65%. Our high-temperature superconducting
> coil design achieves 92% efficiency. Single furnace saves 20 MWh/year. US industrial
> furnaces (500K units) = 10 billion MWh/year potential savings = 5M tons CO2 reduction
> annually = $300M annual value at $60/ton CO2."

#### 2. Understand Manufacturing and Supply Chain
**DoE wants technologies that can scale**

- What's the manufacturing process?
- What materials are required (supply chain constraints)?
- Can you scale to meet market need?
- Are there cost barriers to scaling?
- How do you reduce cost with volume?

**Include in proposal**:
- Manufacturing cost projections (at volume)
- Supplier identification and quotes
- Pathway from lab prototype to production
- Capital required for manufacturing facility (if needed)

#### 3. Address Cost Reduction Targets
**DoE often funds toward cost targets**

- What's current technology cost?
- What's target cost for market adoption?
- How does your technology get there?
- What are the scaling economies?

**Example**:
> "Current lithium-ion battery cost: $125/kWh (2023). Grid storage cost target:
> $70/kWh (DoE goal for 4-hour duration). Our sodium-ion approach:
> - Phase I: Prove technology at lab scale
> - Phase II: Demonstrate cost pathway to $90/kWh
> - Phase III: Scale to $70/kWh with volume manufacturing"

#### 4. Build Team with Energy Industry Experience
**DoE values industry execution experience**

- Prior energy company experience
- Track record in relevant sector (solar, wind, grid, etc.)
- Understanding of energy market dynamics
- Relationships with utilities, manufacturers, integrators

**Advisor/team strategy**:
- Bring on energy industry advisor (utility executive, equipment manufacturer, etc.)
- Demonstrate prior deployments (if any)
- Show understanding of utility business model
- Connect to potential Phase III customers

#### 5. Plan for Utility or Industrial Customer Validation
**Energy innovation needs real-world validation**

- Who will test/deploy your technology?
- Letters of intent from utilities or large industrials?
- Field testing plan in Phase II?
- Cost of field testing?

**Example**:
> "Utility partner: [Large Utility] confirmed interest in Phase II field testing
> of energy storage system (LOI attached). Testing plan: 1 MW / 4 MWh system at
> [Location] substation for 6-month evaluation. Utility will provide site,
> interconnection, and O&M costs. Success criteria: 95% round-trip efficiency,
> 2000+ cycles without degradation."

#### 6. Align with DoE Strategic Priorities
**Research DoE's current 5-year plans and long-term research needs**

- Energy Earthshots (Moon Shots for energy)
- Climate risk and resilience
- Hydrogen as energy carrier
- Energy storage breakthroughs
- Grid resilience and security

**Strategy**: Frame proposal against DoE's stated priorities (shows alignment)

#### 7. Emphasize Domestic Manufacturing
**DoE values US-based manufacturing for strategic tech**

- Where will manufacturing happen?
- Can critical materials be sourced domestically?
- Job creation potential?
- Supply chain resilience?

**Especially important for**: Batteries, semiconductors, rare earth materials, advanced materials

### DoE SBIR Phase Timelines and Milestones

**Phase I** (6 months, $100K-225K):
- Prove technical feasibility
- Develop preliminary cost modeling
- Identify potential utility/industrial partners for Phase II
- Preliminary manufacturing plan
- Deliverable: Phase I final report

**Phase II** (24 months, $750K-1.5M):
- Full prototype development
- Field testing if possible
- Cost reduction validation
- Manufacturing pathway planning
- Quarterly progress reports to DoE

**Phase III**: Not directly funded by DoE, but:
- Can leverage other DoE programs (matching funding)
- Utility/industry partnerships
- Venture capital with DoE validation
- Federal energy procurement (limited)

### DoE SBIR Reviewer Expectations
- **Energy impact**: "How much will this reduce CO2 or improve efficiency?"
- **Technical viability**: "Will this technology work?"
- **Cost viability**: "Can this reach commercial cost target?"
- **Manufacturing**: "Can this be made at scale?"
- **Market**: "Do utilities/industrials actually want this?"

### Common DoE SBIR Mistakes
1. **No energy impact quantification** → "Unclear benefit"
2. **Insufficient cost modeling** → "Not clear how you hit cost targets"
3. **No utility/industry engagement** → "Will anyone actually use this?"
4. **Weak manufacturing plan** → "Can't see how this scales"
5. **Underestimated complexity** → "Timeline unrealistic"
6. **No consideration of grid/system integration** → "Doesn't understand context"
7. **Missing supply chain analysis** → "Critical materials not available"

## Part 6: Multi-Agency Strategy

**Advanced approach**: Submit to multiple agencies in same program year

**Benefits**:
- Increases total funding odds (even with 15% success rate, 2 agencies = ~27% odds at least one funds)
- Learn from rejections at first agency to improve second submission
- Spreads risk across different reviewer pools

**Challenges**:
- Requires 3-6+ months per proposal
- Budget time and resources carefully
- Can't describe same work in multiple proposals (conflict of interest)

**Strategy for multi-agency approach**:
- **Month 0**: Decide which agencies to target (2-3 max)
- **Month 0-1**: Get feedback from program officers at each
- **Month 1-3**: Write Phase I for first agency (easiest fit)
- **Submit first**: Get response in 6-9 months
- **Month 3-5**: Modify/improve proposal for second agency
- **Submit second**: 2-3 months after first
- **Receive feedback**: Learn from first rejection, improve second

**Example timeline**:
- March 2024: Submit NSF SBIR (high technical risk fit)
- May 2024: Submit DoE SBIR (energy market validation fit)
- September 2024: NSF rejects with detailed feedback
- October 2024: Apply feedback, resubmit NSF or pivot to different DoE topic
- December 2024: DoE responds (may fund)
