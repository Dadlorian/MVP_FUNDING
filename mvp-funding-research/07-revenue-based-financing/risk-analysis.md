# RBF Risk Analysis: When NOT to Take Revenue-Based Financing

## Overview

This document provides **honest assessment** of RBF downsides, risks, and situations where RBF is NOT the right funding choice. Read this before you apply.

---

## The Core Problem: RBF Misalignment

### What RBF Actually Does

RBF takes a **percentage of your revenue** every month until you've repaid the multiple.

This sounds simple but creates several problems:

1. **Monthly Cash Flow Reduction**: Revenue goes directly to lender, not your business
2. **Fixed Obligation Regardless of Profit**: You pay even if you're not profitable
3. **Duration Uncertainty**: If growth stalls, repayment extends 2-3 years
4. **Growth Paradox**: Faster growth = faster payoff but harder to sustain costs

### Stress Test Your Situation

**Before reading further, calculate:**

```
Your monthly revenue today: $[X]
Typical RBF repayment (8%): $[X × 0.08]
Your monthly operating costs: $[Y]
Your actual monthly profit: $[X - Y]

If repayment > profit → RBF is RISKY for you
If repayment + profit (and taxes) < runway → RBF creates cash crunch
```

## 10 Situations Where You Should NOT Take RBF

### 1. Revenue is Declining (Current or Projected)

**The Problem**

RBF payments are based on revenue percentage. If revenue declines, the payment percentage stays the same, but actual cash flow gets worse.

**Example: Declining Revenue Disaster**

```
Month 1: $50K revenue
- RBF Payment (8%): $4,000
- Operating Costs: ($30K)
- Net: +$16K ✓ Manageable

Month 2: $48K revenue (4% decline)
- RBF Payment (8%): $3,840
- Operating Costs: ($30K)
- Net: +$15,160 ✓ Still okay

Month 3: $40K revenue (17% decline total)
- RBF Payment (8%): $3,200
- Operating Costs: ($30K)
- Net: +$6,800 ⚠️ Tight

Month 4: $32K revenue (36% decline)
- RBF Payment (8%): $2,560
- Operating Costs: ($30K)
- Net: -$560 ✗ NEGATIVE (funding burning cash!)

Month 5: $28K revenue (44% decline)
- RBF Payment (8%): $2,240
- Operating Costs: ($30K)
- Net: -$4,240 ✗ Default risk (can't pay)
```

**Why This Happens**

You promised investor "we'll grow at X% forever." If growth stalls:
- Committed costs don't reduce (salaries, rent, etc.)
- Revenue becomes insufficient for both costs + RBF
- Cash burns faster with RBF than without it

**Red Flags You're At Risk**

- Declining revenue for 2+ months in a row
- No clear path back to growth
- "We think we'll recover" (without specific plan)
- Market is changing against your business
- Churn is increasing

**Better Choice**: Bootstrap, cut costs, fix business model BEFORE taking RBF

---

### 2. You Haven't Validated Unit Economics

**The Problem**

RBF assumes you have a profitable unit economics (revenue per customer > cost to acquire + service).

If you don't, RBF makes it worse by adding monthly debt obligation.

**Example: Unproven CAC:LTV Ratio**

```
Scenario: Startup with unclear unit economics

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC): $500
Customer Lifetime Value (LTV): ~$2,000?
Ratio: 1:4 (theoretically good)

BUT: You haven't validated this at scale.

Take $100K RBF, spend $80K on acquisition
- Acquire 160 new customers
- Expected LTV: $320K
- Actual LTV (if 40% higher churn): $192K
- You've now spent $100K + RBF repay $135K
- Actual customer value: $192K
- Margin: Positive but risky

What if churn is 60% higher than expected?
- Actual LTV: $128K
- RBF repay: $135K
- You've LOST money on the deal
```

**Warning Signs**

- Can't articulate your CAC in dollars
- LTV is "estimated" not measured
- Different cohorts have wildly different retention
- Payback period >24 months
- Not sure if customers are actually profitable

**Better Choice**: Prove unit economics with bootstrapping for 6-12 months, THEN RBF

---

### 3. Your Business Model is Still in Flux

**The Problem**

RBF works best when **business model is proven**. If you're still testing:
- Pricing model
- Customer segment
- Revenue source
- Distribution channel

Then taking RBF locks you into repayment obligations while you're pivoting, which is dangerous.

**Example: Pivot Spiral**

```
Year 1: SaaS B2B model, raised RBF
- Revenue: $50K/month
- Growing but not as fast as hoped
- Realizing B2B sales cycles = 6 months (too slow)

Year 1.5: Want to pivot to B2C
- B2C would have:
  - Faster sales cycles (1-2 weeks)
  - Lower ARPU ($50 vs $500)
  - Higher volume
- Problem: RBF agreement likely says "can't change business model"
- Even if allowed, unit economics completely different
- RBF payment assumes B2B unit economics
- B2C unit economics might not support same payment

Stuck: Can't pivot easily with RBF obligation
```

**RBF Provider Response to Pivot**

From actual provider terms:
> "Cannot materially change business model, customer segment, or revenue sources without written consent. Breach of this covenant could trigger acceleration of repayment."

**When Pivot Risk is High**

- Experimenting with pricing model (SaaS: standard vs usage-based)
- Testing different customer segments (SMB vs Enterprise)
- Considering marketplace vs SaaS model
- Not confident you've found PMF yet

**Better Choice**: Finalize business model, THEN RBF

---

### 4. Your Profit Margin is Below 20%

**The Problem**

RBF takes 5-10%+ of revenue. If your profit margin is below 20%, RBF eats most/all of your profit.

**Example: Thin-Margin Disaster**

```
Scenario 1: 15% Gross Margin Business
Revenue: $100K
- Cost of goods: ($85K)
Gross profit: $15K
- Operating costs: ($12K)
Net profit: $3K

RBF payment (8%): ($8K)
Result: Net loss of ($5K) per month
→ UNSUSTAINABLE

Scenario 2: Better Scenario with 30% Margin
Revenue: $100K
- Cost of goods: ($70K)
Gross profit: $30K
- Operating costs: ($15K)
Net profit: $15K

RBF payment (8%): ($8K)
Result: Net profit of $7K per month
→ SUSTAINABLE
```

**Businesses at Risk**

- Manufacturing/physical products (20-40% margins typical)
- High-service businesses (consulting, done-for-you, agencies)
- Marketplace (30-50% take rate but high cost)
- Wholesale (thin margins by design)

**Better Choice**: If margin <20%, improve margin structure BEFORE RBF

### 5. You're Not the Operator (Passive Investment)

**The Problem**

RBF works best when the founder is actively managing the business and capital deployment.

If you're:
- Hands-off investor
- Have hired CEO (who might leave)
- Passive income business that runs itself

Then RBF creates misalignment.

**Example: Hired CEO Problem**

```
Scenario: You hired CEO for your SaaS company

You want: CEO will grow business using RBF capital
Reality:
- CEO focuses on near-term metrics (hits own bonus targets)
- RBF capital spent on lowest-risk initiatives
- Not spent on high-risk, high-reward growth
- 12 months later: Growth okay (8% MoM) but not accelerating
- RBF repayment still $6K/month
- You're locked in for 20+ more months
- CEO leaves, new person takes over

Problem: Capital was not optimally deployed.
With personal founder managing capital: Different decisions.
```

**Warning Signs**

- You've stepped back from daily operations
- CEO/manager reports to you (you don't do it yourself)
- Can't articulate how each $1 of capital will be used
- Decisions are made without your input
- You're not reviewing weekly metrics

**Better Choice**: Operators take RBF. Passive investors pass or stay hands-on.

### 6. You Already Have Debt That Consumes >30% of Revenue

**The Problem**

Debt stacking: RBF + existing loan + credit card debt + equipment financing.

If your existing obligations >30% of revenue, adding RBF creates impossible cash flow.

**Example: Debt Stack**

```
Revenue: $80K/month

Existing Debt:
- Equipment loan: $3,000/month (3.75%)
- Previous business loan: $1,500/month (1.87%)
- Credit card payments: $1,000/month (1.25%)
- Total existing: $5,500/month (6.87% of revenue)

Want to take RBF:
- RBF payment (8% of revenue): $6,400/month
- Total debt: $11,900/month (14.87% of revenue)

Still manageable (under 30%)? Yes.
But add operating costs:
- Salaries: ($35K)
- Rent/ops: ($15K)
Total: ($50K)

Cash flow:
- Revenue: $80K
- Debt service: ($11.9K)
- Operating: ($50K)
- Remaining: $18.1K
- But this is GROSS. After taxes? Tight.

If revenue dips even 10% → Can't service all debts
→ Will default on something
```

**Debt-to-Revenue Threshold**

- <10% total debt: Safe
- 10-20%: Okay if revenue growing
- 20-30%: Risky, requires 15%+ growth
- >30%: Avoid RBF, pay down debt first

**Better Choice**: Reduce existing debt to <15% THEN add RBF

### 7. You Need Capital for Survival (Not Growth)

**The Problem**

RBF is **growth capital**, not survival capital.

If you're taking RBF to:
- Pay payroll this month
- Cover operating shortfall
- Keep business from failing

Then RBF is the wrong choice (you need emergency capital or restructuring).

**Example: Survival Trap**

```
Scenario: SaaS company in trouble

Situation:
- Revenue: $50K/month but declining
- Burn rate: $60K/month
- Runway: 2 months
- Problem: Lost major customer, churn accelerating

Founder thinks: "Take $200K RBF, use to survive while we fix churn"

Reality:
- RBF payment: 8% of revenue = $4K/month
- But revenue IS declining (not growing)
- 6 months later: Revenue down to $30K/month
- RBF payment still: $2,400/month (can barely sustain)
- Business still not fixed (just delayed failure)
- Now have $150K+ RBF debt to repay
- If business fails: Personal guarantee means founder personally liable

Better approach:
- Cut costs immediately
- Address churn root cause
- Once stabilized AND growing, THEN RBF
```

**Questions to Ask**

- "If I don't raise this capital, will the business survive 12 months?"
  - Yes → RBF okay
  - No → Don't take RBF (get restructuring, emergency funding, or wind down)

- "Is the capital for growth or survival?"
  - Growth → RBF good
  - Survival → Wrong tool

**Better Choice**: If business is in trouble, RBF is too expensive. Fix problems first.

### 8. You Have Unpredictable/Seasonal Revenue

**The Problem**

RBF works best with **predictable revenue**. If revenue is lumpy, seasonal, or unpredictable:
- Some months have huge payments (cash crunch)
- Other months have tiny payments (overpay)
- Can't plan cash flow accurately
- Risk of default in low months

**Example: Seasonal Agony**

```
Seasonal Service Business: Holiday gift consulting

Revenue by month:
- Jan-Sep: $10K/month (slow season)
- Oct-Nov: $80K/month (peak)
- Dec: $120K/month (holiday rush)

Annual revenue: $350K (average: $29K/month)

Take RBF based on annual average: $10K/month RBF

What happens:
- Jan-Sep: Revenue $10K, but RBF due $10K
  Problem: Revenue = payment, nothing left for ops!
- Oct-Nov: Revenue $80K, payment $10K
  Okay: Plenty of cash to repay
- Dec: Revenue $120K, payment $10K
  Great: Huge cash month

But in Jan-Sep you CANNOT PAY $10K RBF AND RUN BUSINESS

Better: Negotiate seasonal adjustment
- "Can we pay 3% in low season (Jan-Sep) and 12% in peak (Oct-Dec)?"
- This spreads obligation based on revenue
- Much more sustainable

Problem: Not all providers allow seasonal adjustment
- Clearco: No
- Lighter: Yes
- Rapid: Yes (for e-commerce)
```

**If You're Seasonal**

1. Find provider who offers seasonal adjustment (Lighter, Rapid)
2. Negotiate seasonal clause IN ADVANCE
3. Provide 24 months of historical revenue data
4. Get written documentation of seasonal terms
5. If provider won't adjust: Don't take RBF

**Better Choice**: Only take RBF if you can adjust for seasonality OR revenue is stable year-round

### 9. You Can't Articulate How Capital Will Generate 2.5x+ Return

**The Problem**

RBF repayment is 1.3x-1.5x of capital borrowed. For this to work economically:
- Capital must generate 2.5x+ additional revenue
- Over the repayment period (12-24 months)
- To offset repayment + opportunity cost

If you can't map capital → revenue increase, don't take it.

**Example: Blind Capital Deployment**

```
Scenario: Non-RBF founder taking RBF

Founder: "I need $200K to scale my marketing"
Me: "What's the ROI?"
Founder: "I'll grow from $50K to $70K revenue"
Me: "That's +$20K/month = $240K annually"
Me: "But RBF repayment is $270K (1.35x × $200K)"
Me: "You've barely covered the repayment, zero net gain"

Better answer:
"I'll deploy $200K as:
- $100K on paid ads (proven CAC $200, LTV $1,200)
  Expected: 500 customers × $1,200 = $600K value
  ROI: 6x

- $50K on sales team (3 reps)
  Expected: +$80K MRR (new revenue stream)
  12 months = $960K new revenue
  ROI: 19x

- $50K on product (new features)
  Expected: +10% NRR (retention improvement)
  Current MRR: $50K. NRR improvement = +$5K MRR sustainably
  12 months = $60K incremental
  ROI: 1.2x

Total Expected Return: $600K + $960K + $60K = $1.62M
Against RBF repay: $270K
Net gain: $1.35M"
```

**ROI Calculation**

```
Capital deployed: $[X]
Expected incremental revenue: $[Y] (12 months)
Less: RBF repayment: $[X × 1.35]
Less: Cost of incremental revenue: $[Y × COGS %]
─────────────────────────────
Net gain: $[Y - cost - repayment]

If net gain < 0 → Don't take RBF
If net gain < capital borrowed → Marginal
If net gain > 2.5x capital → Worth considering
```

**What NOT to Say**

- "We'll invest it in the business" (too vague)
- "I'll pay back investors and grow" (not answering ROI)
- "We need it to compete" (FOMO, not ROI)
- "It'll help with cash flow" (RBF doesn't improve cash flow, it worsens it)

**Better Choice**: Only take RBF if you can model 2.5x+ ROI on capital deployed

### 10. You're Not Comfortable with Fixed Monthly Obligation

**The Problem**

RBF is a **fixed obligation** every month. Unlike equity (no repayment), RBF requires payment regardless of:
- Profit/loss
- Market conditions
- Personal circumstances
- Business challenges

If fixed obligations stress you out, RBF is not right.

**Emotional/Psychological Reality**

```
Founder Anxiety with RBF:

Month 1: Revenue strong ($60K), RBF payment easy ($4.8K), feeling good
Month 2: Revenue drops to $45K, RBF payment is $3.6K, feeling stressed
  (Payment is lower but cash crunch because of fixed ops costs)
Month 3: Revenue bounces to $58K, RBF payment $4.6K, feeling relieved
Month 4: Market downturn, revenue drops to $35K, RBF payment $2.8K
  (But ops can't scale down, so negative cash flow)

Founder's mental state: Month 4 is STRESSFUL
- Can I make payroll + RBF?
- What if revenue drops again?
- Did I make a mistake taking RBF?

Compare to equity:
Month 1-4: Revenue varies, but no fixed obligation
  Founder can adjust costs, no default risk
  Stress: Lower (more flexibility)

Compare to no capital:
Month 1-4: Revenue varies, costs fixed, natural survival pressure
  Founder adjusts or fails (clear outcome)
  Stress: High but honest
```

**Question to Ask Yourself**

- "How would I feel with $5K/month obligation for 18 months, no matter what?"
- "What if revenue drops 50%? Can I still sleep at night?"
- "Am I comfortable with potential personal liability?"
- "Can I handle feeling like I 'owe' capital to a lender for 18+ months?"

If answer is "No" → Don't take RBF

## RBF Downsides (Even When It's "Right")

### Downside 1: Cash Flow Becomes Tighter

**Reality**: RBF improves business metrics but worsens founder cash flow.

```
Before RBF:
- Revenue: $50K/month
- Costs: $35K/month
- Profit: $15K/month
- Founder draw: $15K/month (or reinvest)

After RBF (taking $100K):
- Revenue: $60K/month (growth from capital)
- Costs: $40K/month (invested in growth)
- Gross Profit: $20K/month
- RBF payment (8%): ($6K)
- Net Profit: $14K/month
- Taxes (est): ($4.5K)
- Founder draw: $9.5K/month (DOWN from $15K)

Founder takes home LESS despite larger business
(money went into growth + repayment)
```

**Why This Matters**

If you're counting on increased personal income from growth, RBF disappoints.

**Better Mindset**: RBF grows the business, not founder income (in the short term)

---

### Downside 2: You Lose Flexibility

**Reality**: RBF agreements include covenants restricting your decisions.

**Common Restrictions**

```
From actual RBF agreements:

1. "Cannot increase debt without written approval"
   → Blocked from raising additional capital if needed

2. "Must notify if revenue declines >30%"
   → Transparency required (good) but also obligation

3. "Cannot change business model materially"
   → Stuck pivoting if you need to

4. "Must maintain minimum bank balance of $X"
   → Can't spend all cash (must reserve for payment)

5. "Cannot acquire company or be acquired without consent"
   → Blocking some strategic options

6. "Must provide monthly financials"
   → Compliance overhead
```

**Impact**

If market changes and you want to pivot, you might:
1. Ask provider for approval (delayed, might be denied)
2. Proceed without approval (breach of covenant, default risk)
3. Stay stuck in original model (bad for business)

---

### Downside 3: Personal Guarantee Risk

**Reality**: Most RBF requires personal guarantee.

If business fails and RBF balance is still $150K:
- Provider can sue you personally
- Can garnish wages, attach personal assets
- Can damage personal credit
- Could lead to personal bankruptcy

**Example: Personal Guarantee Impact**

```
Scenario: Business fails, RBF balance: $150K

Without Personal Guarantee:
- Business fails
- RBF provider gets ~20% recovery (liquidated assets)
- Founder: Loses business, keeps personal assets safe

With Personal Guarantee:
- Business fails
- RBF provider sues you personally
- Garnishes wages, attaches personal assets
- Could lose house (if you put up collateral)
- Personal credit destroyed (7 years impact)
- Personal bankruptcy possible
```

**When Personal Guarantee is Risky**

- You're personally guaranteeing >$100K
- You have personal net worth <$500K
- Business model is unproven
- Market is risky (early-stage)

---

### Downside 4: Longer Repayment = Longer Obligation

**Reality**: If growth slows, repayment extends.

```
Scenario: Projected 14-month repayment, actual 24 months

Initial plan:
- Raise $200K RBF
- Grow revenue 20% MoM
- Repayment completes in 14 months
- Then reinvest profit into business

Actual outcome (slower growth):
- Revenue grows 10% MoM (less than hoped)
- Repayment extends to 24 months
- Founder expected to be "done" with RBF in 14 months
- Reality: 10 more months of payments and restrictions
- By month 20: Founder tired, wants capital out, can't exit

Problem: You're locked in longer than expected
- Can't raise equity (have RBF obligation)
- Can't take more debt (would violate covenants)
- Can't pivot (would violate covenants)
- Just have to keep paying monthly
```

---

### Downside 5: Repayment Multiple is Non-Negotiable

**Reality**: Once growth slows, you're stuck with the repayment multiple.

If you took 1.4x multiple when business was hot:
- You owe $140K on $100K borrowed
- Business growth slowed (not your fault, market)
- Can't renegotiate the 1.4x multiple
- Still owe the full amount
- Provider won't forgive or adjust

**This is Brutal**

```
Scenario: Economic downturn

2024: Took $250K RBF @ 1.35x ($337.5K repay)
2024-2025: Market downturn, revenue flat
2026: Still owe $337.5K
2026: Can't refinance (other providers won't touch you)
2026: Can't take equity (RBF in the way)
2026: Stuck paying $4K-5K/month indefinitely

Better if you'd negotiated:
1. Lower multiple upfront (1.30x instead of 1.35x)
2. Seasonal adjustment clause
3. Revenue decline adjustment
```

---

### Downside 6: Reputation/Privacy Loss

**Reality**: RBF requires deep financial visibility.

**What Lender Knows**

- Your monthly revenue (every month)
- Your bank balance
- Your accounting software data
- Your business model details
- Your founder personal info

**Privacy Concerns**

- Lender could detect business problems before you
- Lender could pressure you (we know your revenue is declining)
- Lender could share data (though agreements say no)
- Public knowledge: Cap table includes RBF debt (if you raise later)
- Founder community: Word gets out (especially with Pipe/marketplace)

---

### Downside 7: Opportunity Cost of Capital Spending

**Reality**: Capital under RBF obligation must be deployed for ROI, not experimentation.

```
With Bootstrap Capital ($100K saved):
- Spend $10K on failed marketing experiment
- No pressure to show ROI
- Learn valuable lesson
- Move on to next experiment

With RBF Capital ($100K borrowed):
- Spend $10K on experiment
- PRESSURE: Must show ROI (lender is watching)
- If fails: Compresses runway further (RBF payment still due)
- Psychology: Can't afford to "waste" money on learning
- Result: More conservative capital deployment
- Outcome: Slower innovation, fewer bets

The cost of capital is not just dollars, but psychological pressure to be "perfect."
```

## RBF Success Pre-Mortem

### What Could Go Wrong?

Before you sign, imagine it's 12 months later and RBF was a mistake.

**Ask Yourself**

1. "What would have to happen for RBF to be a disaster?"
   - Revenue decline >30%
   - Key customer leaves
   - Market shifts against me
   - Team member leaves critical role
   - Unexpected crisis

2. "Could I weather any of these?"
   - If no → Don't take RBF
   - If yes (barely) → Consider smaller RBF
   - If yes (comfortably) → RBF is reasonable

3. "What's my plan if all 5 of these happen?"
   - Business has 6-month contingency plan
   - Know exactly what I'd cut to stay solvent
   - Have alternative revenue sources
   - Can reduce operating costs by 30%+ if needed

## RBF Decision Checklist: Go/No-Go

### GREEN LIGHTS (Take RBF)

- [ ] Revenue >$15K/month
- [ ] Revenue growing 10%+ MoM consistently
- [ ] Gross margin >30%
- [ ] Clear ROI on capital deployment (2.5x+)
- [ ] Business model proven and validated
- [ ] Unit economics understood and profitable
- [ ] Can articulate monthly RBF payment in cash flow
- [ ] Comfortable with monthly obligation psychology
- [ ] Not in survival mode (have 6+ month runway)
- [ ] Can negotiate seasonal adjustments (if seasonal)
- [ ] Personal credit score >700 (if personal guarantee required)
- [ ] Trust the process (not desperate for capital)
- [ ] Have plan B if revenue dips 25%
- [ ] Founder is actively involved (not passive)
- [ ] Existing debt <15% of revenue

**Threshold**: 12+ green lights = RBF is reasonable

### RED FLAGS (Don't Take RBF)

- [ ] Revenue declining or flat
- [ ] Revenue <$10K/month
- [ ] Can't explain ROI of capital
- [ ] Business model in flux (testing)
- [ ] Gross margin <20%
- [ ] Profit margin <10%
- [ ] Taking RBF for survival (not growth)
- [ ] Seasonal revenue >30% variation
- [ ] Uncomfortable with fixed monthly obligation
- [ ] Not actively managing business
- [ ] Existing debt >20% of revenue
- [ ] Personal credit score <650
- [ ] No plan for revenue decline
- [ ] Desperate for capital (bad time to negotiate)
- [ ] Haven't validated product-market fit

**Threshold**: 3+ red flags = RBF is RISKY (reconsider)

## Alternative Funding Sources (When RBF is Wrong)

### If You're Too Early-Stage

**Better Options**:
- Friends & family ($10K-$100K)
- Bootstrapping (slow but safe)
- Revenue-only scaling (no capital)
- Grants (for specific industries/situations)
- Pre-sales/customer deposits (selling in advance)

---

### If Revenue is Declining

**Better Options**:
- Restructuring business (cut costs)
- Fixing product (improve retention)
- Repositioning (new market)
- Pivot (different revenue model)
- Wind-down (if business is failing)

---

### If Unit Economics Unproven

**Better Options**:
- Validate with bootstrap capital
- Run experiments (small, cheap tests)
- Talk to customers (understand LTV)
- A/B test pricing
- Prove cohort economics before scaling

---

### If Survival Mode

**Better Options**:
- Emergency capital from personal resources
- Fire sale (sell off assets)
- Debt restructuring (talk to lenders)
- Strategic partnership (revenue share)
- Wind-down professionally (don't default)

---

### If Need Large Amount ($2M+) or Scale Fast

**Better Options**:
- Equity funding (VC, angels)
- Venture debt (traditional term loan)
- Combination (RBF + equity hybrid)
- Strategic investor (with industry expertise)

## Post-RBF Regret Recovery

### You Took RBF and Now Regret It

**If Within 30 Days**

1. Contact provider immediately
2. Explain: "Changed mind, don't want to proceed"
3. Ask: "Can we cancel before funding?"
4. Most providers will accommodate (no penalties if not yet funded)
5. Do NOT use the capital (makes it harder to unwind)

**If Already Funded**

1. Can you refinance with better terms?
   - Talk to other providers (Lighter, Rapid)
   - Can you pay early without penalty?
   - Can you trade down to smaller RBF?

2. Can you improve business to support payments?
   - Cut costs to improve margin
   - Focus on retention (improve unit economics)
   - Launch new revenue stream
   - Accelerate growth

3. Can you raise equity to pay off RBF?
   - Yes: Equity round specifically to pay off RBF early
   - RBF becomes bridge financing tool (not growth capital)
   - Dilutes equity but removes cash flow pressure

4. Accept and adapt
   - If can't refinance: Accept the obligation
   - Rebudget around the $X/month payment
   - Focus on business fundamentals
   - Pay on time (avoiding default worse than RBF itself)

## Final Honest Assessment

### The Core Truth About RBF

RBF is neither "good" nor "bad." It's a **tool that works for specific situations**.

**RBF is Great If:**
- Your business is proven and growing
- You have >$20K/month stable revenue
- You understand capital ROI
- You don't mind fixed monthly obligation
- You can support 1.3x-1.5x repayment multiple
- You want to preserve equity and control

**RBF is Terrible If:**
- You haven't validated business model
- You're struggling with current revenue
- You can't trace capital to revenue
- You need flexibility (capital will constrain it)
- You're in survival mode
- You're uncomfortable with risk

### The Real Cost of RBF

It's not the repayment multiple (1.35x vs 1.40x).

It's:
- **Psychological cost**: 18-24 months of monthly obligation
- **Cash flow cost**: Tighter margins month-to-month
- **Flexibility cost**: Can't pivot easily or raise equity
- **Opportunity cost**: Conservative capital deployment (less experimentation)
- **Personal cost**: Personal guarantee ties your personal net worth to business

These are real and worth factoring in.

### When You Should Say "No Thanks"

The best time to turn down RBF is:
1. Before you apply (save time)
2. When you get the offer (preserve relationship)
3. Never while using the capital (too late)

If you're unsure, **the answer is probably no**. Only take RBF when you're 80%+ confident it's right.

## Recommended Pre-RBF Actions

### 1. Talk to Founders Who Have Done RBF

- Find 3-5 founders who raised RBF
- Ask: "Would you do it again?"
- Ask: "What surprised you?"
- Ask: "What would you do differently?"
- Listen for regrets/doubts

**Where to Find Them**:
- Provider case studies
- ProductHunt (search "RBF" in launches)
- Indie Hackers community
- Y Combinator alumni
- Your investor network

### 2. Create Detailed Cash Flow Model

```
Months 1-24 Cash Flow Forecast

Assumptions:
- Revenue growth rate: [X]% MoM
- Operating cost increase: [Y]% MoM
- Tax rate: [Z]%
- RBF payment: [8]% of revenue

For each month calculate:
- Revenue projection
- RBF payment due
- Operating costs
- Taxes owed
- Net cash flow
- Ending cash balance

FAIL CASE: Model with 0% growth (flat revenue)
- Can you still service RBF?
- What's your cash balance month 12?
- Can you survive?

If fail case shows negative cash, don't take RBF
```

### 3. Interview 2-3 RBF Providers

- Ask about alternative structures
- Ask about seasonal clauses
- Ask about early payoff terms
- Ask about flexibility if revenue declines
- Negotiate BEFORE accepting offer

### 4. Talk to a Business Lawyer

- Review RBF agreement ($500-1,000 well-spent)
- Understand personal guarantee implications
- Understand default triggers
- Ask about negotiable terms

### 5. Run the "Regret Test"

Imagine 12 months from now:
- Revenue grew slower than expected
- RBF payment is becoming burden
- You wish you hadn't taken it

**Ask yourself**: "How likely is this scenario?"

- 0-10% likelihood → Take RBF
- 10-30% likelihood → Think hard (maybe pass)
- 30%+ likelihood → Don't take RBF
